## Answer A
**Short answer (Spain, state law):** The €3,000 rule is outdated. Since **Ley 18/2022 ("Ley Crea y Crece")**, which amended **Art. 4 of the Ley de Sociedades de Capital (LSC)**, the minimum share capital of a Sociedad Limitada is **€1**. You don't need a workaround or a year as autónomos just to avoid the €3,000. In your situation an SL is usually the better choice from day one. On tax, expect the **15% "new entity" rate** for your first two profitable years, and possibly longer at 15% if you qualify as a certified startup. After that, the rate is somewhere between about 20% and 25%, depending on your turnover.

## 1. The €3,000 myth

**What changed.** Ley 18/2022 (in force since October 2022) cut the SL minimum capital from €3,000 to €1. There are two strings attached while your capital stays below €3,000 (Art. 4 bis LSC; recalled, not verified here, so check the current consolidated text on BOE.es):

- **Mandatory legal reserve.** At least **20% of each year's profit** must go to the legal reserve until reserve plus capital reaches €3,000. Dividends are restricted until then.
- **Liability on liquidation.** If the company is wound up and its assets can't pay creditors, the partners are **jointly liable for the shortfall up to €3,000** (the difference between the capital subscribed and €3,000).

In practice this means capping your personal exposure at €3,000 in a wind-up that goes badly, instead of paying €3,000 in cash today. For most founders that trade is worth making.

**Share capital isn't lost money either.** Even if you put in €3,000, you deposit it before the notary appointment and the bank issues the certificate (Art. 62 LSC). Once the company is registered, the SL can spend that money on servers, salaries or anything else. Share capital is a figure on the balance sheet, not a frozen bank balance. The real cost of a higher capital is having the cash available for a few days, not losing it.

**Practical options:**
- Incorporate with a small capital, for example €2 (€1 each) or a few hundred euros, and accept the reserve and liability rules above.
- Contribute non-monetary assets instead of cash. You could contribute the SaaS code/IP, for instance. There's no bank certificate for those, but the founders are liable for the stated value (Arts. 63 and 73 LSC), so don't overvalue them.
- Incorporate electronically through **CIRCE / PAE** using the model bylaws (*estatutos tipo*). That's usually the cheapest and fastest route, because notary and registry fees are capped. I'm not certain of the current fee caps, so check them with the notary or your PAE.

**Before the notary you'll still need:** your NIE (which you already have as a resident), your co-founder's DNI, a name certificate from the Registro Mercantil Central, the bank certificate (for cash contributions), and a provisional NIF for the company.

## 2. Why the autónomo route probably doesn't fit

- **There are two of you.** Two autónomos can't share one "business" cleanly. You would end up with a sociedad civil or a comunidad de bienes. Since 2016, a sociedad civil carrying on a commercial activity is subject to **Impuesto sobre Sociedades** anyway, but without limited liability. That's the worst of both worlds.
- **Unlimited personal liability.** As an autónomo you answer for business debts with your personal assets (Art. 1911 Código Civil). For a SaaS business carrying contract and data-protection (GDPR) exposure, that's a real risk.
- **You'll likely be in RETA anyway.** Partners who control an SL and work in it must register as self-employed. Under **Art. 305 LGSS**, effective control is presumed at ≥50% of the capital, at ≥33% if you work in the company, or at ≥25% with management duties. As 50/50 working co-founders, you'll almost certainly both be *autónomos societarios*. Choosing the SL doesn't save you the RETA quota, so the RETA quota alone isn't a reason to choose the autónomo route.
- **Progressive IRPF.** Autónomo income is taxed at IRPF progressive rates, with a combined state and Madrid top rate somewhere in the mid-40s%. Profits you keep in an SL are taxed at IS rates instead.
- **Converting later is a hassle.** Moving a running business from autónomo to an SL means transferring contracts, customers and IP, and it can have tax consequences. It's easier to start as an SL.

## 3. Corporate tax (Impuesto sobre Sociedades, Ley 27/2014, "LIS")

| Situation | Rate |

| **General rate** (Art. 29.1 LIS) | 25% |
| **Newly created entity**: first tax period with a positive tax base plus the following one (Art. 29.1 LIS) | **15%** |
| **Certified *empresa emergente*** under the Startups Law (**Ley 28/2022**), with ENISA certification | **15%** for the first profitable period plus the **next three** (4 years total). Also allows **deferral of tax payments** for the first two profitable years |
| **Microenterprises** (turnover < €1M) after the new-entity period | Reduced rates being phased in by Ley 7/2024. My recollection is that by 2029 it reaches about 17% on the first €50k of base and 20% above that. The intermediate years sit between those and 25%. **Verify the 2026 figures** |

**Worth checking:** whether you qualify for **ENISA *empresa emergente*** certification. A SaaS product is the typical case: an innovative, scalable business model, under 5 years old, not listed. Besides the longer 15% window, certification gives you better tax treatment of employee stock options, which matters if you plan to give equity to early hires.

## 4. US-citizen issues you shouldn't ignore

The US taxes you on worldwide income, so owning a Spanish SL creates US filing obligations:
- **Form 5471.** Holding 10% or more of a foreign corporation typically triggers this filing. Penalties for missing it are steep: $10,000 per form per year, as I recall.
- **CFC / GILTI (now "NCTI").** If US shareholders own more than 50%, the SL is a **controlled foreign corporation (CFC)** and its profits can be taxed to you in the US currently, before any dividend is paid. At 50/50 with a Spanish co-founder, you are **not** over 50%, so you're likely outside CFC status. That changes if you bring in US investors or your share goes up. Keep it in mind when you set the cap table.
- **Social security.** The US–Spain totalization agreement generally means you pay into one system, not both. As a Madrid resident working here, that should normally be Spain (RETA).
- **Beckham Law.** You're probably **not** eligible because you already live in Spain. The regime requires that you haven't been tax-resident in Spain in the prior 5 years and that you apply within 6 months of starting activity or registering. I mention it only so you don't count on it.

A **cross-border accountant** (a Spanish asesor who works with a US CPA, or a firm that does both) is worth paying for from the start.

## 5. Suggested sequence

1. Agree on the cap table and a **pacto de socios** (shareholders' agreement) first. Cover vesting, leaver clauses, IP assignment to the company, and deadlock rules. This matters more than the capital figure.
2. Get the name certificate from the Registro Mercantil Central.
3. Open the bank account and deposit the capital, whatever amount you choose (even €2).
4. Sign before the notary, or incorporate online through CIRCE.
5. Register with the Registro Mercantil, get the final NIF, file the census declaration (Modelo 036), and both register in RETA.
6. Apply to ENISA for *empresa emergente* certification.

## Risks and caveats

- With capital under €3,000 you carry the **20% reserve** restriction and **up to €3,000 personal exposure** in a deficient liquidation.
- Some banks, suppliers and public tenders look at share capital as a sign of solvency. A €2 SL may face more questions, which is one reason some founders choose €1,000–€3,000.
- IS rates and the Ley 7/2024 phase-in need to be confirmed for the current tax year. The article numbers above are from memory and should be checked against the consolidated texts on BOE.es.

## 1. Share capital: what the law says now

**The minimum is €1.** Ley 18/2022 changed Art. 4 of the *Ley de Sociedades de Capital* (LSC, Real Decreto Legislativo 1/2010). It dropped the €3,000 floor to €1 (recalled, not verified; check the consolidated text of Art. 4 LSC on boe.es). Much of the guidance online was written before this change. Capital below €3,000 comes with two conditions:

- **Legal reserve.** Each year, at least 20% of profit must go to the legal reserve until the reserve plus the capital reaches €3,000.
- **Liquidation liability.** If the company is wound up and can't pay its debts, the partners are jointly liable for the gap between €3,000 and the capital they subscribed. In practice that means at most about €3,000 split between the two of you.

The old *sociedad de formación sucesiva* regime, which was the earlier low-capital option, was abolished at the same time.

**You may not need a bank certificate.** For an SL, the founders can declare in the deed that they are jointly liable for the capital contributions actually being made. This replaces the bank deposit certificate (Art. 62 LSC, as amended in 2013; recalled, not verified). With €1–€100 of capital, many founders simply do this.

**The deposit isn't locked away.** Capital you contribute belongs to the company and can pay for operating costs, including servers, from the first day. It's working capital, not a guarantee held in escrow.

**What I'd suggest:** put in a modest capital amount, from a few hundred euros up to whatever you can spare, and fund the rest through partner loans (*préstamo de socio*) as you need it. Partner loans are much easier to repay to yourselves later than capital.

## 2. Why "autónomo first" is probably the wrong trade-off here

| Factor | Autónomo (each of you) | SL |
|---|---|---|
| Liability | Unlimited, personal | Limited to the company, with the caveat above |
| Two founders, one product | Awkward. You'd need a *sociedad civil* or informal split, and ownership of the IP and equity is unclear | Clear cap table and clear IP ownership |
| Tax on profit | Progressive IRPF, with the top combined rate in Madrid roughly in the mid-40s % | 15% in the first profitable years, then reduced or 25% (see below) |
| Fundraising / ESOP | Not possible | Standard |
| Social security | RETA, with the reduced *tarifa plana* for the first 12 months | Working partner-directors with effective control usually still register in RETA as *autónomos societarios*. So the SL doesn't remove this cost |

Moving from autónomo to SL later means transferring the business and the IP. That's more paperwork and tax exposure than just starting with the SL. For a two-founder SaaS company, I'd usually form the SL now.

## 3. Corporate tax (Impuesto sobre Sociedades, Ley 27/2014, "LIS")

These are recalled, not verified. Rates changed recently, so confirm them for the tax year that applies to you.

- **General rate:** 25% (Art. 29 LIS).
- **Newly created companies:** 15% for the first tax period with a positive tax base and the period after it. This doesn't apply if the business was previously run by a related person or if the company belongs to a group.
- **Certified start-up (*empresa emergente*, Ley 28/2022):** if ENISA certifies you as innovative, which a SaaS company can often qualify for, the 15% rate extends to **four** profitable periods. The certification also brings other benefits, such as better tax treatment of employee stock options and an option to defer tax payments. It's worth applying.
- **Small-company reductions** (Ley 7/2024, phased in from 2025):
  - Companies with turnover under €1M get graduated rates. As I recall, these fall toward **17% on the first €50,000 of profit and 20% on the rest** by the end of the phase-in.
  - Companies classed as *reducida dimensión* (turnover under €10M) see their rate fall year by year toward 20%.

  I'm not confident of the exact rate for each year, so check them for 2026 and 2027.

**Realistically,** you'd pay 15% for the first couple of profitable years (or four, with ENISA), then roughly 20–23% if you stay small.

## 4. Two things to check that are specific to you

1. **Your residence permit.** Check that it allows you to work, in particular as a self-employed person or as a director. A *residencia no lucrativa* (non-lucrative residence) permit **does not** allow work. You can own shares on that permit, but being an active, paid director would need a change of permit, for example to *cuenta propia* (self-employment) or the digital nomad or entrepreneur route under Ley 14/2013. This could be your biggest risk.
2. **US tax, since the US taxes its citizens wherever they live.**
   - If you own 10% or more of the SL, you'll probably have to file **Form 5471** with the IRS.
   - With a 50/50 split, US shareholders don't own *more than* 50%, so the SL generally wouldn't count as a controlled foreign corporation (CFC) for GILTI purposes. Any shareholding above 50% would change that.
   - The SL isn't a "per se" corporation for US purposes, so a check-the-box election is available. That can be good or bad depending on your situation.

   Coordinate with a US-Spain cross-border tax advisor before you sign the deed.

## 5. Practical steps

1. Get a free name certificate (*certificación negativa de denominación*) from the Registro Mercantil Central.
2. Each of you needs a NIE and a digital certificate, which you likely already have.
3. Incorporate online through CIRCE/PAE using standard bylaws. With low capital this has reduced notary and registry fees, typically a few hundred euros in total including incidentals (recalled; confirm).
4. Get a provisional NIF, register the company's tax census entries (Modelo 036), register the working directors in RETA, and register the beneficial owner (*titular real*).
5. Before you start building the product in earnest, sign a founders' agreement (*pacto de socios*) covering vesting and IP assignment.

**Risks to keep in mind:** the liability of up to €3,000 on liquidation if capital is under €3,000; losing the 15% new-company rate if the business counts as a continuation of an existing activity; your permit's work authorization; and US reporting penalties if Form 5471 or FBAR filings are missed. A Spanish *abogado* or *gestor* and a US-Spain tax advisor should confirm all of this before you incorporate.
