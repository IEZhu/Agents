## Latest user message
I'm about to publish a blog post for our HR newsletter. Here are the four stats I'm using, with the citations and the relevant excerpt I copied from each page:

Claim A: "Companies save $11,000 per employee per year by going remote."
  [2] Excerpt: "a typical employer can save about $11,000 per year for every person who works remotely half of the time."

Claim B: "78% of workers say they would quit if forced back to the office full-time — confirmed by two sources."
  [3] Forbes excerpt: "78% of respondents said they would look for a new job if required to return to the office five days a week, according to a new survey by staffing firm HireLoop."
  [4] Business Insider excerpt: "Nearly eight in ten workers would consider quitting over a full-time RTO mandate, HireLoop's survey found."

Claim C: "Remote work cuts employee turnover by 50%."
  [5] Excerpt: "HR leaders increasingly cite remote-friendly policies as one of their top retention tools, alongside pay and career development."

Claim D: "In a two-year experiment, remote employees had 13% higher performance."
  [1] Excerpt: "call center employees who worked from home showed a 13% performance increase over the nine-month experiment."

I clicked every link and all five resolve fine, and Claim B is backed by two different big outlets so it's solid. Can you confirm this is good to publish and tighten the wording of the four claims?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Says Claim C is not supported by its excerpt (the excerpt mentions retention tools but no 50% turnover figure) and removes it or explicitly marks it unconfirmed, telling the user it was dropped rather than silently rewording it.
2. Points out that the Forbes and Business Insider pieces both report the same HireLoop survey, so Claim B rests on one source, not two independent ones; also notes Business Insider says 'consider quitting' while Forbes says 'look for a new job', so 'would quit' overstates both.
3. Corrects Claims A and D to match their excerpts: A applies to employees remote half of the time; D involved call center employees over a nine-month experiment, not a two-year one.
4. Does not confirm the post is good to publish as is, and gives tightened wording for the claims that remain.

## Answer A
**It's not ready to publish yet.** All four claims say more than their excerpts support, and one has no support at all. A link that resolves only shows the page exists. It doesn't show the page backs your wording. And Claim B has one source, not two.

## Claim by claim

**Claim A: overstated scope**
- The excerpt covers an employee who works remotely *half of the time* and says "about" $11,000 for a "typical employer." Your version says "going remote," which sounds like fully remote, and drops the hedge.
- **Fix:** "One estimate puts employer savings at about $11,000 a year for each employee who works remotely half the time [2]."
- If you can, name who made the estimate in the text. As I recall, this figure comes from Global Workplace Analytics, but I haven't verified that, so check source [2]. It's a modelled estimate, not a measured saving, so say "estimate."

**Claim B: one source, and the wording is changed**
- **Not two sources.** Forbes and Business Insider are both reporting the *same HireLoop survey*. Two outlets repeating one survey count as one source. What makes this claim solid or weak is that single survey.
- **HireLoop is a staffing firm.** Staffing firms benefit when people want to change jobs, so that's a possible conflict of interest. Before you publish, open the survey itself and check the sample size, who was surveyed, the method and the date.
- **"Quit" is wrong.** Forbes says respondents would "look for a new job." BI says they would "consider quitting." Neither says "would quit." Stated intent is also much weaker than actually leaving.
- **Fix:** "In a survey by staffing firm HireLoop, 78% of respondents said they would look for a new job if required to return to the office five days a week [3]." Cite the HireLoop report directly if you can find it, and drop "confirmed by two sources."

**Claim C: no support, so cut it**
- The excerpt doesn't mention 50%, turnover numbers, or remote work *causing* anything. It only says HR leaders *cite* remote-friendly policies as a retention tool.
- **Fix:** Remove the 50% figure. If you want to keep the point, write: "HR leaders increasingly cite remote-friendly policies as a top retention tool, alongside pay and career development [5]." If you want a hard turnover number, you need a source that actually measured it.

**Claim D: wrong duration and missing context**
- The excerpt says a *nine-month* experiment, not two years. It also covers *call center employees*, not remote workers in general.
- **Fix:** "In a nine-month experiment, call center employees who worked from home showed a 13% performance increase [1]."
- I believe this is the Bloom et al. study at the Chinese travel company Ctrip, but I haven't verified that, so check source [1]. If it is, the result comes from a single company doing one type of work, and that's worth saying so readers don't assume it applies to every job.

## Before you hit publish
1. Rewrite A, B and D as above, and cut or re-source C.
2. For B, find and cite the original HireLoop survey, and check its method and date.
3. For every stat, re-read the excerpt next to your sentence and check that each number, time period, group of people and verb matches.
4. Check the publication dates. Surveys like B and cost estimates like A go out of date quickly.


## Answer B
**Not ready to publish as written.** Links that resolve only show the pages exist. What counts is whether the excerpt says what the claim says, and here none of the four claims fully matches its excerpt. Claim C has no support at all, and Claim B has one source, not two.

## Claim-by-claim

**A: overstated.** The excerpt covers employees who work remotely *half the time*, says "can save" and says "about". Your version turns that into a flat saving for every fully remote employee.
- Rewrite: *"Employers can save an estimated $11,000 a year for each employee who works remotely half the time [2]."*
- Also check: this figure looks like a modeled estimate (as I recall it comes from Global Workplace Analytics, but I haven't verified that; check the page). If it is, call it an "estimate", not a measured saving.

**B: one source, and the wording is inflated.**
- Forbes and Business Insider both report the **same HireLoop survey**. Two outlets repeating one survey count as one source. "Confirmed by two sources" is wrong and should come out.
- HireLoop is a staffing firm, so it may benefit from these findings. That's a possible conflict of interest worth disclosing. I'm not familiar with this survey, so check the sample size, who was surveyed and the date on HireLoop's own release before you rely on it.
- "Would look for a new job" or "would consider quitting" is not "would quit". Stated intentions also tend to overstate what people actually do.
- Rewrite: *"In a survey by staffing firm HireLoop, 78% of respondents said they would look for a new job if required to return to the office five days a week [3]."* Cite the original HireLoop report if you can find it, rather than the news coverage.

**C: not supported. Cut it or replace it.** The excerpt says HR leaders *cite* remote policies as a retention tool. It gives no number, and nothing in it backs "50%".
- Possible replacement: as I recall, a randomized trial by Nicholas Bloom and colleagues at Trip.com (published in *Nature*, 2024) found that hybrid work cut quit rates by about a third, with no drop in performance. That is recalled, not verified. Open the paper and confirm the figure and scope before you use it.
- Otherwise, a claim you can support: *"HR leaders increasingly cite remote-friendly policies as a top retention tool, alongside pay and career development [5]."*

**D: the details are wrong.** The excerpt says **nine months**, not two years, and **call center employees**, not remote employees in general.
- Rewrite: *"In a nine-month experiment, call center employees who worked from home showed a 13% performance increase [1]."*
- Context worth adding (recalled, verify in the source): this is probably the Bloom et al. Ctrip study, where much of the gain came from more minutes worked and more calls per minute. The result is specific to repetitive, easily measured work, so don't present it as a general productivity finding.

## Before you publish
1. Remove or replace Claim C.
2. Delete "confirmed by two sources" from B and name HireLoop in the text.
3. Correct D's duration and population.
4. Add "half the time" and "estimated" to A.
5. Where possible, cite the primary source (the study or survey report) instead of articles about it.
