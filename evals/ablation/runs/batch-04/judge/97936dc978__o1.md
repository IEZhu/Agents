## Answer A
> **Disclaimer:** I am an AI assistant, not a lawyer admitted in Kazakhstan, the AIFC or Germany. This is general information, not legal advice. Before you act, get advice from counsel who practise before the AIFC Court.

## Bottom line

If the company really is an AIFC private company, your lawyer's advice is probably wrong on both points.

1. **Wrong law.** An AIFC company is governed by AIFC acting law, mainly the AIFC Companies Regulations, which are modelled on English company law. The Kazakh Civil Code and the Law on LLPs, including the right to withdraw and be paid the actual value of your share, apply to a Kazakh ТОО. They do not apply to a company limited by shares incorporated in the AIFC.
2. **Wrong court.** Disputes about the internal affairs of an AIFC participant belong to the **AIFC Court**, or to arbitration if your shareholders' agreement (SHA) says so, for example at the AIFC International Arbitration Centre (IAC). The specialised inter-district economic court (СМЭС) of Astana is the wrong forum.

Your strongest route is probably not an exit at book value. It is an English-style minority protection claim: breach of pre-emption rights and/or **unfair prejudice**, asking the court to set aside the dilution or order the majority holder to buy you out at a fair, *pre-dilution* value.

## Why the lawyer's route doesn't fit

**Step 1: confirm what the company actually is.** Search the AIFC public register. Your lawyer's analysis would fit if:
- the company is really a Kazakh **ТОО**, for example an Astana Hub participant, which founders sometimes describe loosely as "AIFC"; or
- there is a Kazakh ТОО operating company underneath an AIFC holding company.

If it is an ТОО, the Law on LLPs does give a withdrawal right with payment of the share's value. As I recall, payment is due within a year unless the charter says otherwise (recalled, not verified: check the current wording of the Law on LLPs). The СМЭС would then be the right court.

**Even in that case, withdrawing is usually a poor deal for you:**
- The payout is normally calculated from the company's financial statements. The majority holder, who controls those statements, is currently hiding them.
- The value might be measured *after* the dilution, i.e. on 12%, not 30%.
- Withdrawing gives up your standing to challenge the dilution.

**If it is an AIFC private company:**
- It is a company limited by shares. English-style company law generally has **no statutory right for a shareholder to withdraw** and demand repayment.
- Your remedies are claims about how the company was run, plus contract claims under the SHA.
- Under the Constitutional Statute on the AIFC (7 December 2015), the AIFC Court has jurisdiction over disputes between AIFC participants and their participants, and over disputes about operations carried out in the AIFC (as I recall, Article 13; recalled, not verified: confirm the current text, which has been amended).
- AIFC Court judgments are enforced in Kazakhstan in the same way as judgments of Kazakh courts.

## Legal hooks to investigate (AIFC scenario)

1. **Statutory pre-emption on allotment.** As I recall, the AIFC Companies Regulations copy the UK Companies Act 2006 model: new equity securities issued for cash must first be offered to existing shareholders pro rata. A private company's articles can disapply this. Check the articles and the SHA. If pre-emption applied and was ignored:
   - the allotment may be open to challenge;
   - the directors responsible may have to compensate you.
2. **Authority to allot.** Check who approved the issue, and in what capacity (as director or as shareholder). Also check whether the articles or a shareholder resolution actually gave that authority.
3. **Unfair prejudice.** I believe the AIFC Companies Regulations contain an English-style remedy for conduct that unfairly prejudices a member (recalled, not verified: confirm the provision). Your facts fit the classic pattern: a majority holder issues shares to himself without offering them to you, and cuts off information. English case law, which the AIFC Court treats as highly persuasive, often gives a **buyout order at fair value, valued as if the prejudicial act had not happened**. That is the "exit at real value" your lawyer described, but reached through the right mechanism.
4. **SHA contract claims.** Typical clauses to look for:
   - contractual pre-emption or anti-dilution rights;
   - reserved matters needing investor consent, e.g. new share issues;
   - information rights;
   - a board seat;
   - put options;
   - tag-along rights.
   Breaching any of these gives you separate damages and injunction claims.
5. **Information rights.** Shareholders are generally entitled to receive the annual accounts and to inspect certain registers. A refusal strengthens an unfair prejudice case.

## Recommended strategy

1. **Get the SHA and articles now.** Their governing-law and dispute-resolution clauses decide where you litigate. If the SHA sends disputes to IAC or LCIA arbitration, SHA claims go there, while the company-law claims may still go to the AIFC Court. Map this carefully.
2. **Pull the public record.** From the AIFC Registrar, obtain:
   - the current shareholder register;
   - filings relating to the new allotment: date, price, number of shares;
   - the current directors.
3. **Don't acquiesce.** Send a formal written letter to the company and the majority holder that:
   - objects to the issue;
   - reserves all rights;
   - demands the accounts, board and shareholder minutes, and the allotment documents;
   - demands that you be offered your pro-rata share.
   Delay, or conduct that looks like acceptance, can weaken both equitable remedies and interim relief.
4. **Consider urgent interim relief.** Examples are an injunction against further issues, transfers of assets or IP, or dilution. This matters most if cash or IP could be moved into another entity. The AIFC Court can grant interim remedies.
5. **Instruct the right counsel.** You need lawyers experienced in English-law shareholder disputes who can appear before the AIFC Court, not only Kazakh civil-law litigators. A German adviser should check the tax effects of any buyout or settlement.
6. **Use the claim as leverage.** Most cases like this settle with a negotiated buyout, or with the dilutive issue being reversed or topped up. A credible, well-documented claim in the correct forum is what makes that happen.

## 1. Why the "withdraw under the Civil Code" advice likely doesn't fit

**Two separate legal systems apply inside Kazakhstan:**

| | Main Kazakh jurisdiction | AIFC |
|---|---|---|
| Legal tradition | Civil law | Common law, based on English law |
| Company law | Civil Code of RK + Law on LLPs (ТОО) | AIFC Constitutional Statute + AIFC Companies Regulations and Rules |
| Courts | State courts, including the specialized inter-district economic courts | AIFC Court (and the AIFC International Arbitration Centre) |

- The right to **leave an LLP and be paid the actual value of your share within a year** comes from the Kazakh LLP regime. It is a feature of a **ТОО**. As far as I know, the AIFC Companies Regulations have no equivalent automatic exit right for a shareholder in an AIFC private company. They follow the English model: a shareholder generally cannot force the company to buy them out unless the articles or the SHA give that right, or a court orders it. (This is my recollection. Confirm it against the current Regulations.)
- The AIFC Constitutional Statute (Constitutional Law of RK No. 438-V of 7 December 2015) makes AIFC acts the applicable law for relations among AIFC participants and for their internal affairs. It also gives the **AIFC Court** jurisdiction over disputes between AIFC participants, disputes about operations carried out in the AIFC and governed by AIFC law, and disputes the parties refer to it by agreement. I am citing the general effect from memory, not the exact article text. Check Article 13 of the Constitutional Statute and the AIFC Court Regulations.
- Even if the withdrawal route were available, it would work against you. A buyout valued *after* the dilution could be priced at about 12%, not 30%. You would be giving up your best argument, that the share issue is invalid or unfairly prejudicial, in exchange for a discounted exit.

**One fact could change all of this.** Some startups are ordinary Kazakh **ТОО**s that hold AIFC or Astana Hub *participant status*, or they are described loosely as being "in the AIFC." If your company is actually a ТОО, your lawyer's framework becomes much more relevant. So the first step is to confirm which one you have (see below).

## 2. What is likely wrong with the share issue

Under an English-style AIFC regime, several questions come up. Treat each one as something to check, not as a confirmed rule:

1. **Pre-emption rights.** English-model company law usually requires new shares to be offered to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings first, unless this is validly disapplied. Your SHA very likely contains its own pre-emption and anti-dilution clause as well. An issue made only to the majority holder without offering you shares is a textbook breach.
2. **Thresholds.** Disapplying pre-emption, or changing the articles, often needs a **special resolution**. Under the English model that means 75%. **Your co-founder holds 70%.** If the AIFC Regulations or your articles use a 75% threshold for any step he needed, he could not pass it alone. This is potentially decisive, so get the exact threshold from the AIFC Companies Regulations and your articles.
3. **Reserved matters / veto.** VC-style SHAs almost always list share issues as a reserved matter that needs investor consent. A 30% investor normally negotiates such a veto.
4. **Directors' duties.** If he approved the issue as a director, issuing shares mainly to entrench his control or dilute a minority is a classic breach of the duty to act for proper purposes and in the company's interests.
5. **Information rights.** Refusing to share financials may breach the SHA's information covenants and your statutory rights to inspect records and receive accounts.

**Remedies usually available under this model.** Treat each as an item to verify in the AIFC Regulations:
- An **unfair prejudice petition**, modelled on s. 994 of the UK Companies Act 2006. The court has wide discretion. It can set aside the allotment, or order the majority to **buy your shares at a fair value, often calculated as at a date before the prejudicial conduct and without a minority discount**. That is the buyout you actually want, priced at 30%.
- A claim to have the allotment declared invalid or set aside, and the register rectified.
- Contractual claims under the SHA (breach of pre-emption, veto, information rights), including damages or specific performance.
- **Interim injunctions** to stop further allotments, transfers, or asset stripping while the case runs.
- As a last resort, winding up on "just and equitable" grounds.

## 3. Where the dispute would be heard

In order of likelihood:

1. **Whatever the SHA's dispute-resolution clause says.** This matters most, and you haven't checked it yet. Possible outcomes:
   - AIFC Court: the natural forum for an AIFC company.
   - AIFC International Arbitration Centre (IAC), or another seat such as LCIA or SIAC. The award would then be enforced through the New York Convention, which Kazakhstan has joined.
   - Foreign courts (for example, English courts) under a foreign governing-law clause. This is possible but less likely for AIFC-internal corporate matters.
2. **With no clause, or for claims about the company's internal affairs:** the **AIFC Court**, not the state economic court. Its judgments are enforceable in Kazakhstan in the same way as judgments of Kazakh courts. That is my recollection of the Constitutional Statute; check it.

One caution: claims about a company's constitution and statutory remedies such as unfair prejudice may belong to the AIFC Court even if the SHA sends contract claims to arbitration. Look at the SHA clause and the corporate remedies together.

## 4. Practical strategy

**This week:**
1. **Get the SHA and the articles of association.** Read the governing law, dispute resolution, pre-emption and anti-dilution, reserved matters, information rights, put or exit options, and any deadlock or buyout clauses.
2. **Search the AIFC public register** (AIFC Registrar of Companies) for the company. Confirm that it is an AIFC private company, not a ТОО. Pull its filings: the statement or return of allotment, the current register of shareholders, and the director list.
3. **Preserve evidence**: the resolution approving the issue, any notices you received or didn't receive, emails showing he stopped sharing financials, and the investment documents for your $400k.

**Next 2–4 weeks:**
4. **Hire counsel with rights of audience before the AIFC Court, or with AIFC-law experience.** Many firms in Astana and Almaty have English-qualified lawyers who practise there. Your current lawyer's answer suggests they may not work in the AIFC regime.
5. **Send a formal letter before action.** Reserve all rights, object to the allotment as invalid and prejudicial, demand the financial information under the SHA and the Regulations, and demand that no further allotments or transfers be made.
6. **Consider urgent interim relief** if there is a risk of further dilution, a sale of the business, or IP or cash being moved out.

**Then pick a route:**
- **If you want to stay in:** set aside the allotment, restore 30%, and enforce your information and veto rights.
- **If you want to exit:** use an unfair prejudice claim or the SHA's put option to push for a buyout at fair value on a pre-dilution basis. This also gives you leverage for a negotiated settlement, which is how many of these disputes end.

Don't take any step that could be read as accepting the new share structure, such as voting on the issued shares or signing updated registers, until you have advice.
