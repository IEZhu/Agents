## 1. Why the "withdraw under the Civil Code" advice likely doesn't fit

**Two separate legal systems apply inside Kazakhstan:**

| | Main Kazakh jurisdiction | AIFC |
|---|---|---|
| Legal tradition | Civil law | Common law, based on English law |
| Company law | Civil Code of RK + Law on LLPs (ТОО) | AIFC Constitutional Statute + AIFC Companies Regulations and Rules |
| Courts | State courts, including the specialized inter-district economic courts | AIFC Court (and the AIFC International Arbitration Centre) |

- The right to **leave an LLP and be paid the actual value of your share within a year** comes from the Kazakh LLP regime. It is a feature of a **ТОО**. As far as I know, the AIFC Companies Regulations have no equivalent automatic exit right for a shareholder in an AIFC private company. They follow the English model: a shareholder generally cannot force the company to buy them out unless the articles or the SHA give that right, or a court orders it. (This is my recollection. Confirm it against the current Regulations.)
- The AIFC Constitutional Statute (Constitutional Law of RK No. 438-V of 7 December 2015) makes AIFC acts the applicable law for relations among AIFC participants and for their internal affairs. It also gives the **AIFC Court** jurisdiction over disputes between AIFC participants, disputes about operations carried out in the AIFC and governed by AIFC law, and disputes the parties refer to it by agreement. I am citing the general effect from memory, not the exact article text. Check Article 13 of the Constitutional Statute and the AIFC Court Regulations.
- Even if the withdrawal route were available, it would work against you. A buyout valued *after* the dilution could be priced at about 12%, not 30%. You would be giving up your best argument, that the share issue is invalid or unfairly prejudicial, in exchange for a discounted exit.

**One fact could change all of this.** Some startups are ordinary Kazakh **ТОО**s that hold AIFC or Astana Hub *participant status*, or they are described loosely as being "in the AIFC." If your company is actually a ТОО, your lawyer's framework becomes much more relevant. So the first step is to confirm which one you have (see below).

## 2. What is likely wrong with the share issue

Under an English-style AIFC regime, several questions come up. Treat each one as something to check, not as a confirmed rule:

1. **Pre-emption rights.** English-model company law usually requires new shares to be offered to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings first, unless this is validly disapplied. Your SHA very likely contains its own pre-emption and anti-dilution clause as well. An issue made only to the majority holder without offering you shares is a textbook breach.
2. **Thresholds.** Disapplying pre-emption, or changing the articles, often needs a **special resolution**. Under the English model that means 75%. **Your co-founder holds 70%.** If the AIFC Regulations or your articles use a 75% threshold for any step he needed, he could not pass it alone. This is potentially decisive, so get the exact threshold from the AIFC Companies Regulations and your articles.
3. **Reserved matters / veto.** VC-style SHAs almost always list share issues as a reserved matter that needs investor consent. A 30% investor normally negotiates such a veto.
4. **Directors' duties.** If he approved the issue as a director, issuing shares mainly to entrench his control or dilute a minority is a classic breach of the duty to act for proper purposes and in the company's interests.
5. **Information rights.** Refusing to share financials may breach the SHA's information covenants and your statutory rights to inspect records and receive accounts.

**Remedies usually available under this model.** Treat each as an item to verify in the AIFC Regulations:
- An **unfair prejudice petition**, modelled on s. 994 of the UK Companies Act 2006. The court has wide discretion. It can set aside the allotment, or order the majority to **buy your shares at a fair value, often calculated as at a date before the prejudicial conduct and without a minority discount**. That is the buyout you actually want, priced at 30%.
- A claim to have the allotment declared invalid or set aside, and the register rectified.
- Contractual claims under the SHA (breach of pre-emption, veto, information rights), including damages or specific performance.
- **Interim injunctions** to stop further allotments, transfers, or asset stripping while the case runs.
- As a last resort, winding up on "just and equitable" grounds.

## 3. Where the dispute would be heard

In order of likelihood:

1. **Whatever the SHA's dispute-resolution clause says.** This matters most, and you haven't checked it yet. Possible outcomes:
   - AIFC Court: the natural forum for an AIFC company.
   - AIFC International Arbitration Centre (IAC), or another seat such as LCIA or SIAC. The award would then be enforced through the New York Convention, which Kazakhstan has joined.
   - Foreign courts (for example, English courts) under a foreign governing-law clause. This is possible but less likely for AIFC-internal corporate matters.
2. **With no clause, or for claims about the company's internal affairs:** the **AIFC Court**, not the state economic court. Its judgments are enforceable in Kazakhstan in the same way as judgments of Kazakh courts. That is my recollection of the Constitutional Statute; check it.

One caution: claims about a company's constitution and statutory remedies such as unfair prejudice may belong to the AIFC Court even if the SHA sends contract claims to arbitration. Look at the SHA clause and the corporate remedies together.

**Then pick a route:**

Don't take any step that could be read as accepting the new share structure, such as voting on the issued shares or signing updated registers, until you have advice.
