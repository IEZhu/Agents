**Short answer (Spain):** The €3,000 minimum is out of date. Since the *Ley Crea y Crece* (Ley 18/2022), a Sociedad Limitada can be formed with **€1** of share capital. Even if you put in €3,000, that money isn't frozen: once the company exists it can spend it on servers. So there's little reason to go the autónomo route just to avoid the capital requirement. The standard corporate tax rate is 25%, but a new company like yours will very likely pay **15% in its first two profitable years**. Smaller companies also get reduced rates that are being phased in.

## 1. Share capital: what the law says now

**The minimum is €1.** Ley 18/2022 changed Art. 4 of the *Ley de Sociedades de Capital* (LSC, Real Decreto Legislativo 1/2010). It dropped the €3,000 floor to €1 (recalled, not verified; check the consolidated text of Art. 4 LSC on boe.es). Much of the guidance online was written before this change. Capital below €3,000 comes with two conditions:

- **Legal reserve.** Each year, at least 20% of profit must go to the legal reserve until the reserve plus the capital reaches €3,000.
- **Liquidation liability.** If the company is wound up and can't pay its debts, the partners are jointly liable for the gap between €3,000 and the capital they subscribed. In practice that means at most about €3,000 split between the two of you.

The old *sociedad de formación sucesiva* regime, which was the earlier low-capital option, was abolished at the same time.

**You may not need a bank certificate.** For an SL, the founders can declare in the deed that they are jointly liable for the capital contributions actually being made. This replaces the bank deposit certificate (Art. 62 LSC, as amended in 2013; recalled, not verified). With €1–€100 of capital, many founders simply do this.

**The deposit isn't locked away.** Capital you contribute belongs to the company and can pay for operating costs, including servers, from the first day. It's working capital, not a guarantee held in escrow.

**What I'd suggest:** put in a modest capital amount, from a few hundred euros up to whatever you can spare, and fund the rest through partner loans (*préstamo de socio*) as you need it. Partner loans are much easier to repay to yourselves later than capital.

## 2. Why "autónomo first" is probably the wrong trade-off here

| Factor | Autónomo (each of you) | SL |
|---|---|---|
| Liability | Unlimited, personal | Limited to the company, with the caveat above |
| Two founders, one product | Awkward. You'd need a *sociedad civil* or informal split, and ownership of the IP and equity is unclear | Clear cap table and clear IP ownership |
| Tax on profit | Progressive IRPF, with the top combined rate in Madrid roughly in the mid-40s % | 15% in the first profitable years, then reduced or 25% (see below) |
| Fundraising / ESOP | Not possible | Standard |
| Social security | RETA, with the reduced *tarifa plana* for the first 12 months | Working partner-directors with effective control usually still register in RETA as *autónomos societarios*. So the SL doesn't remove this cost |

Moving from autónomo to SL later means transferring the business and the IP. That's more paperwork and tax exposure than just starting with the SL. For a two-founder SaaS company, I'd usually form the SL now.

## 3. Corporate tax (Impuesto sobre Sociedades, Ley 27/2014, "LIS")

These are recalled, not verified. Rates changed recently, so confirm them for the tax year that applies to you.

- **General rate:** 25% (Art. 29 LIS).
- **Newly created companies:** 15% for the first tax period with a positive tax base and the period after it. This doesn't apply if the business was previously run by a related person or if the company belongs to a group.
- **Certified start-up (*empresa emergente*, Ley 28/2022):** if ENISA certifies you as innovative, which a SaaS company can often qualify for, the 15% rate extends to **four** profitable periods. The certification also brings other benefits, such as better tax treatment of employee stock options and an option to defer tax payments. It's worth applying.
- **Small-company reductions** (Ley 7/2024, phased in from 2025):
  - Companies with turnover under €1M get graduated rates. As I recall, these fall toward **17% on the first €50,000 of profit and 20% on the rest** by the end of the phase-in.
  - Companies classed as *reducida dimensión* (turnover under €10M) see their rate fall year by year toward 20%.

  I'm not confident of the exact rate for each year, so check them for 2026 and 2027.

**Realistically,** you'd pay 15% for the first couple of profitable years (or four, with ENISA), then roughly 20–23% if you stay small.

## 4. Two things to check that are specific to you

1. **Your residence permit.** Check that it allows you to work, in particular as a self-employed person or as a director. A *residencia no lucrativa* (non-lucrative residence) permit **does not** allow work. You can own shares on that permit, but being an active, paid director would need a change of permit, for example to *cuenta propia* (self-employment) or the digital nomad or entrepreneur route under Ley 14/2013. This could be your biggest risk.
2. **US tax, since the US taxes its citizens wherever they live.**
   - If you own 10% or more of the SL, you'll probably have to file **Form 5471** with the IRS.
   - With a 50/50 split, US shareholders don't own *more than* 50%, so the SL generally wouldn't count as a controlled foreign corporation (CFC) for GILTI purposes. Any shareholding above 50% would change that.
   - The SL isn't a "per se" corporation for US purposes, so a check-the-box election is available. That can be good or bad depending on your situation.

   Coordinate with a US-Spain cross-border tax advisor before you sign the deed.

## 5. Practical steps

1. Get a free name certificate (*certificación negativa de denominación*) from the Registro Mercantil Central.
2. Each of you needs a NIE and a digital certificate, which you likely already have.
3. Incorporate online through CIRCE/PAE using standard bylaws. With low capital this has reduced notary and registry fees, typically a few hundred euros in total including incidentals (recalled; confirm).
4. Get a provisional NIF, register the company's tax census entries (Modelo 036), register the working directors in RETA, and register the beneficial owner (*titular real*).
5. Before you start building the product in earnest, sign a founders' agreement (*pacto de socios*) covering vesting and IP assignment.

**Risks to keep in mind:** the liability of up to €3,000 on liquidation if capital is under €3,000; losing the 15% new-company rate if the business counts as a continuation of an existing activity; your permit's work authorization; and US reporting penalties if Form 5471 or FBAR filings are missed. A Spanish *abogado* or *gestor* and a US-Spain tax advisor should confirm all of this before you incorporate.
