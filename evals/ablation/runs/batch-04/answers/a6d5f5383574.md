## 1. Why the agent is wrong about skipping the trust

**The rule is based on nationality, not immigration status.**

- **Art. 27, fraction I, CPEUM (Mexican Constitution):** foreigners may not acquire "direct ownership" (*dominio directo*) of land and waters within **100 km of the borders or 50 km of the coasts**. This area is the *zona restringida* (restricted zone). An oceanfront condo in Puerto Vallarta is plainly inside it.
- **Ley de Inversión Extranjera (Foreign Investment Law), Title II, Chapter II (roughly Arts. 10–14; I'm recalling these, please confirm):** this law carries out the constitutional rule. A foreigner can hold residential property in the restricted zone only as the **beneficiary of a fideicomiso**, with a Mexican bank as trustee and a permit from the **Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores (SRE, the foreign ministry)**.
- *Residente temporal* or *residente permanente* status comes from the **Ley de Migración** (immigration law). It changes your permission to stay in Mexico. It does not change your nationality. In Art. 27 CPEUM and the Foreign Investment Law, "foreigner" means anyone without Mexican nationality. **Only naturalization** would let you hold title directly.
- **Could you use a Mexican company instead?** Only for non-residential use. A company that holds a condo you personally live in is the wrong structure and invites trouble.

**What happens if you title it directly anyway:** a deed that violates Art. 27 is legally vulnerable. It can be declared null, and you may not be able to register it, sell it, or pass it on. As I recall, most Jalisco notaries would refuse to execute it. That is a much bigger risk than the trust fees.

**The trust itself works like ownership.** You can live in the condo, rent it, sell it, mortgage it (subject to bank rules), and name successor beneficiaries, which makes inheritance easier than a will alone. As I recall, the trust term is **up to 50 years and renewable**. Typical costs are a one-time setup fee plus an annual bank fee, usually somewhere in the **low hundreds to around US$1,000 a year** (recalled market range, not verified; get quotes from 2–3 trustee banks). For a $385k property, the fee is a small cost for secure title.

The agent may simply be mistaken, or may be trying to make the deal simpler to close. Either way, don't rely on that advice.

## 2. Why the Mexico City template is the wrong document

1. **Wrong state law.** Civil and property matters are **state law** in Mexico. A CDMX contract is drafted under the *Código Civil para el Distrito Federal/CDMX*. Your purchase falls under the **Código Civil del Estado de Jalisco** and Jalisco's registry and notarial rules. First confirm the condo really is in **Puerto Vallarta, Jalisco**. Nuevo Vallarta and Bucerías, across the river, are in **Nayarit**, which has different rules again.
2. **No restricted-zone structure.** A Mexico City apartment is outside the restricted zone, so that template almost certainly has **no fideicomiso mechanics**. It won't cover the SRE permit, the bank as buyer of record, or your rights as beneficiary.
3. **It isn't the document that transfers title.** Private templates like this are usually a **promissory agreement** (*contrato de promesa de compraventa*). The real transfer happens in a **public deed** (*escritura pública*) before a **Jalisco notary** (*notario público*), which is then recorded at the **Registro Público de la Propiedad** (property registry). The notary drafts that deed, not the parties.
4. **Nothing specific to oceanfront or condos.** A beach condo needs clauses and checks that a city apartment template won't have (see section 3).

Your promissory agreement should be drafted or reviewed by **your own** lawyer, not the seller's agent. It should include deposit escrow, conditions (clean title, SRE permit, trust set up), a closing deadline, and remedies.

## 3. Steps to close by December

Timelines below are typical ranges I'm recalling, not verified. Confirm them with the notary and bank.

| Step | Who | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|
| Hire an independent Jalisco real-estate lawyer; pick the notary (the buyer usually chooses) | You | Now |
| Sign the promissory agreement, with the deposit held in **escrow**, not paid directly to the seller | Lawyer / escrow agent | Week 1–2 |
| Apply for the fideicomiso with a trustee bank, including the **SRE permit** (the bank usually handles it) | Bank / notary | ~4–8 weeks |
| Due diligence (list below) | Lawyer / notary | Runs alongside the trust setup |
| Appraisal (*avalúo*), tax calculations, sign the deed before the notary | Notary | Closing |
| Record the deed at the Registro Público de la Propiedad | Notary | After closing (can take months; that's normal) |

**Due-diligence checklist for an oceanfront condo:**
- **Certificado de libertad de gravámenes** (lien certificate) and the chain of title.
- **Ejido origin:** confirm the land was properly regularized and was never unconverted *ejido* (communal) land. This is a known risk in the Vallarta area.
- **ZOFEMAT** (the federal maritime-terrestrial zone, roughly the 20 m strip along the shore): make sure the building doesn't encroach on it, or that any concession it needs is valid and paid.
- **Condo regime:** the recorded *régimen de propiedad en condominio*, the building rules (*reglamento*), HOA fees, and a **no-debt letter** from the building administration.
- **No-debt certificates** for property tax (*predial*) and water.
- **Transfer tax** (*Impuesto sobre Transmisiones Patrimoniales*, set by the municipality) and notary fees. Budget a total closing cost of roughly **5–8% of the price** (recalled estimate).
- **Seller's taxes:** the notary withholds the seller's capital-gains tax (ISR). Make sure the contract says the seller bears it.

## 4. Risks and side issues

- **Deed in your own name:** could be declared null, can't be registered, can't be passed on. The main risk to avoid.
- **The agent has a conflict of interest:** the seller's agent works for the seller. Your lawyer and the notary should be independent.
- **US tax/reporting:** as I recall, the IRS ruled in **Rev. Rul. 2013-14** that Mexican fideicomisos holding residential property are generally not foreign trusts for US reporting purposes (no Form 3520/3520-A). Confirm with a US tax advisor.
- **Future sale:** Mexico's exemption for selling your principal home (*casa habitación*, LISR) generally requires proof of **Mexican tax residency** (RFC registration, proof of address). Set this up early if you want to use it later.
- **Estate planning:** name substitute beneficiaries in the trust. Consider a Mexican will as well.

**Bottom line:** buy through a fideicomiso and throw out the CDMX template. Have a Jalisco lawyer and notary run the purchase. If you start the trust application in the next few weeks, a December closing is realistic.
