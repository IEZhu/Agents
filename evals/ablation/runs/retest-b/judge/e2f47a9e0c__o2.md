## Latest user message
I'm modeling a wall-mounted bracket in Blender to hold a 1.2 kg router, going to print it on a Prusa MK4 with a 0.4 mm nozzle. Current design: the arms are 0.8 mm thick shells, I embossed our house number on the face with 0.3 mm wide strokes, and there's a 14 mm unsupported gap between two tabs where the screw plate sits. Before I export the STL, anything that'll bite me? And what slicer settings should I use?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Flags the 0.3 mm embossed strokes as too thin for a 0.4 mm nozzle and suggests widening them (to at least about the nozzle width, ideally more).
2. Flags both the 0.8 mm arm shells as too thin for a load-bearing part (recommends thickening well above 1 mm) and the 14 mm unsupported bridge as a sag risk with a fix (bridge tuning, reorienting, adding a support or chamfer).
3. Gives settings appropriate for a functional part, e.g. PETG (or ABS/ASA) with about 3-4 perimeters and roughly 30-50% infill, rather than decorative-print defaults.
4. Addresses print orientation relative to the load, e.g. orienting the bracket so the arms are not stressed across layer lines, since FDM parts are weakest between layers.

## Answer A
Yes. Two of your three features will cause problems, and the third needs checking. In order of severity:

## 1. 0.8 mm arm shells: this is the one that can drop your router

- **0.8 mm is below the FDM minimum.** It is exactly two 0.4 mm perimeters with nothing between them. That's fine for a cosmetic shell, but not for a cantilever arm carrying 1.2 kg.
- **The arms are the most stressed part of the bracket.** The router's weight tries to bend each arm where it meets the wall plate. That spot sees the highest stress, and it's also where layer adhesion is weakest if the layers run the wrong way (see orientation below).
- **Creep matters more than the first-day strength.** A router is a constant load and it runs warm. PLA slowly sags under a constant load, and warmth makes it worse. A bracket that holds on day one can droop or crack after a few months.

**Fix:**
- Make the arms **solid, or at least 3–4 mm thick**. For this part size, extra plastic costs almost nothing.
- Add a **fillet of 3 mm or more** (or a triangular gusset) where each arm meets the back plate. Sharp inside corners are where cracks start.
- As a rough starting point for a 1.2 kg load (a judgment call, not a calculation), aim for arms about 4 mm thick and 10–15 mm tall in the direction of the load. Arm height adds far more stiffness than arm width.

## 2. 0.3 mm house-number strokes: they'll disappear or turn to blobs

- A 0.4 mm nozzle lays down a line about 0.45 mm wide. A 0.3 mm stroke is narrower than one line of plastic.
- The slicer will do one of two things. It may drop those strokes entirely, which leaves gaps in the digits. Or it may stretch them to its minimum line width, which gives you uneven, blobby numbers.
- Always check the embossed text in the slicer preview. This is a common surprise.

**Fix:**
- Make strokes **at least 0.8 mm wide**, which is two lines and looks clean. Treat 0.5 mm as the absolute floor.
- Make the raised height at least 0.6 mm (three layers at 0.2 mm) so the numbers stand out.
- Alternative: **engrave** the numbers 0.6–1.0 mm deep instead of raising them. Engraved text survives thin strokes better, and if the face prints against the bed it needs no supports.
- Either way, make sure the text is **joined into the body with a boolean union and the result applied**. If the text just overlaps or sits on the surface, you get internal faces and non-manifold edges. Slicers often "repair" that in odd ways.

## 3. 14 mm bridge under the screw plate: printable, but check what it's doing

- Bridging 14 mm is usually fine on an MK4 in PLA or PETG with the fan on. It's under the ~10 mm point where bridges typically start needing tuning, and still within what a tuned printer handles well.
- Expect the first bridge layer to sag a little, probably 0.2–0.5 mm. The underside will be rough.
- The problem is if **the screw heads bear on that bridged area**, or if that surface has to sit flat against the wall. Then the sag becomes your mounting surface: the bracket rocks, or the screws pull through the weakest layers.

**Fix, in order of preference:**
1. **Re-orient the part** so the screw plate prints flat on the bed. Then there's no bridge at all.
2. If you can't, add a **45° chamfer or wedge** under the gap so it supports itself.
3. Leave it as a bridge only if that face isn't load-bearing or visible.

For the screw holes:
- Make them about 0.3–0.4 mm oversize so the screws pass through freely.
- Counterbore them or use washers so the clamping force spreads out.
- Keep at least 3 mm of material around each hole.

## 4. Print orientation (this decides the arm strength)

- **Lay the bracket on its side**, so the arm profile (the L or J shape) is visible from above. Each layer then contains the full arm and the arm-to-plate corner, and bending loads run along the printed lines instead of peeling layers apart.
- **Avoid printing it standing upright** with the back plate on the bed. The arm roots then become layer boundaries, and that's where FDM parts snap.
- Printing on its side may conflict with the best orientation for the embossed numbers. If it does, choose strength. Engraved numbers on a side face still read fine.

## 5. Before you export the STL

- **Apply all transforms:** `bpy.ops.object.transform_apply(location=True, rotation=True, scale=True)`. An unapplied scale is the usual reason a part imports at 1/1000 or 1000× size.
- **Apply all modifiers**, especially any Boolean used for the numbers.
- **Run the 3D-Print Toolbox checks** for non-manifold edges, wall thickness (set the threshold to 1.2 mm; it should flag your current 0.8 mm arms) and overhangs. In Blender 4.2 and later I believe the toolbox is an Extension rather than a bundled add-on (recalled, not verified; check Edit → Preferences → Get Extensions).
- **Recalculate normals to point outward**: in Edit Mode, Mesh → Normals → Recalculate Outside.
- **Check the dimensions in PrusaSlicer** after import against your design sizes.

## Slicer settings (PrusaSlicer, MK4, 0.4 mm nozzle)

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Material | **PETG** (or ASA) over PLA | Much less creep under constant load, better heat tolerance near a warm router |
| Layer height | 0.2 mm | Good balance of strength and speed; use 0.15 only if the numbers matter visually |
| Perimeters | **4–5** | Perimeters carry bending loads much better than infill |
| Top / bottom layers | 5 / 5 | Solid skins on the arms |
| Infill | 40% gyroid or cubic | Stiff in every direction without being wasteful |
| Supports | None if oriented and chamfered as above | Otherwise "support on build plate only" |
| Bridging | Default MK4 profile, fan at 100% for bridges | 14 mm is within its comfort zone |
| Perimeter generator | Arachne (default, I believe) | Handles thin text strokes better than Classic, but it can't save 0.3 mm strokes |
| Brim | Optional, 3–5 mm | Only if a tall, thin part lifts at the corners in PETG |

With PETG, use a thin layer of glue stick or the textured sheet on the MK4 smooth PEI sheet. PETG can bond too strongly to smooth PEI and pull chunks out of it.

## Summary

- **Must fix:** make the arms 3–4 mm or solid and fillet their roots.
- **Must fix:** widen the number strokes to at least 0.5 mm, ideally 0.8 mm, or engrave them instead.
- **Should fix:** make sure the screw plate isn't sitting on a sagging bridge.
- **Then:** print on its side in PETG with 4–5 perimeters. With those changes, 1.2 kg is a comfortable load for this bracket.


## Answer B
Yes. Two of these will cause problems, and one is probably fine.

## 1. The 0.8 mm arm shells are the real problem

- **Printability:** with a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.8 mm is exactly two perimeters, and it's below the 1.2 mm FDM minimum. It will print, but there's no infill between the perimeters, so each arm is just two strands of plastic.
- **Strength:** 1.2 kg is only about 12 N, which sounds small. But the load sits on a cantilever: the arm root takes a bending moment, and it takes it all the time. Bent thin shells tend to buckle or crack at the root, especially if a cable gets tugged or someone bumps the router. On top of that, a router runs warm, and PLA under a constant load creeps (sags slowly) as it gets warmer. My guess is that 0.8 mm arms would hold at first and then droop or crack within weeks to months. I haven't calculated that; it's based on experience.
- **Fix:**
  - Make the arms solid or thick-walled, at least 3–4 mm and ideally 5–6 mm.
  - Add a fillet at the arm-to-plate root. 3–5 mm radius is a good start, and bigger is better.
  - A triangular gusset under each arm, if you have room, adds more stiffness than any slicer setting can.

## 2. The 0.3 mm house number strokes will disappear

A 0.4 mm nozzle lays down lines about 0.4–0.45 mm wide. The slicer will drop features narrower than one extrusion, or turn them into blobs. PrusaSlicer's Arachne wall generator has a minimum feature size setting, but 0.3 mm is below what it will reliably keep.

- Make the strokes **at least 0.8–1.0 mm wide** (two extrusion widths) so they come out clean.
- Emboss height of **0.6 mm or more** (3 layers at 0.2 mm) makes the number readable.
- If the number sits on a face that ends up vertical on the bed, 1 mm strokes are a safer choice.
- Debossing (cutting the number into the face) usually prints cleaner than embossing on a vertical face.

## 3. The 14 mm bridge is probably fine

A 14 mm bridge is well within what an MK4 handles in PLA or PETG with default bridging settings, so it doesn't need supports. Two things to check:

- The bridge must actually be horizontal in the orientation you print in. If it ends up as an overhang under a sloped face instead, that's a different problem.
- If the screw plate's clamp force goes through that bridged layer, give it 1–2 mm of solid material above the bridge. The first bridge layer is always a bit saggy and weak.

## 4. Print orientation matters more than any setting

FDM parts are much weaker between layers than along them. If you print the bracket with the wall plate flat on the bed and the arms pointing up, the bending load at the arm root pulls the layers apart. That's the weakest possible setup.

**Print the bracket lying on its side**, with the L-profile (plate plus arm) flat on the bed. The layer lines then run continuously through the corner and along the arm. The trade-off is that the house number will be on a vertical face, which is one more reason for the thicker strokes above.

## 5. Other things to check before exporting

- **Material:** use **PETG** rather than PLA. It resists creep better and softens at a higher temperature, which matters next to a warm router. ASA works too if you have an enclosure.
- **Screw holes:** leave clearance for the screws, about 0.2–0.3 mm over the screw diameter. Add a countersink or use a washer so the head doesn't split the layers. Put at least 3–4 mm of material around each hole, and use proper anchors if you're mounting into drywall.
- **Mesh:** run the 3D Print Toolbox checks (Solid / Intersections / Thickness) in Blender. Embossed text is the usual source of non-manifold edges and overlapping faces, so apply a Union boolean to the text rather than leaving it as a separate overlapping object.
- **Units:** confirm your scene scale so the STL comes out in mm (scene unit scale 0.001 with lengths in mm, or scale 1.0 treating 1 unit as 1 mm). A bracket that imports 1000× too small or too large is a classic Blender mistake.
- **STL export:** Blender 4.2+ uses the new built-in STL exporter (`bpy.ops.wm.stl_export`). The old `bpy.ops.export_mesh.stl` add-on operator was removed around that version. I recalled this rather than checking it, so confirm against your Blender version if you script the export.

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|

| Layer height | 0.20 mm | Good balance of strength and time |
| Perimeters | 5–6 | Most of a part's strength comes from its walls |
| Top/bottom solid layers | 5 / 5 | |
