## Latest user message
We forecast weekly demand for ~4,000 SKUs at a grocery chain. Two analysts built competing models and I have to choose one for next quarter:

- Model A (seasonal naive + promo uplift multipliers): overall WAPE 21%. On long-running staple SKUs it's 14%, but on SKUs launched in the last 6 months it's 48%.
- Model B (LightGBM global model with price, promo, weather, holiday features): overall WAPE 19%. On staples it's 17%, on new SKUs 29%. During the two Christmas weeks it was 40% vs A's 26%.

Staples are ~70% of volume, new SKUs ~10%, the rest is in between. Leadership wants "one model". Which one do we ship?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Does not just pick the model with the lower overall WAPE; proposes combining them (e.g. routing by SKU segment and/or period, or a weighted ensemble) and explains which model covers which segment using the numbers given
2. Handles the 'one model' constraint, e.g. by framing the combination as one forecasting system/pipeline or explaining the trade-off to leadership
3. Flags a risk of the merge, such as overfitting segment rules to one backtest, SKUs moving between segments over time, or needing to validate the combined approach on a holdout period
4. Numerical reasoning is consistent with the provided figures (does not misstate which model wins on which segment)

## Answer A
**Short answer:** Ship Model B as the base engine, but don't ship it alone. Use A's forecast for staples and for the Christmas weeks. That is one forecasting system with one output, which should meet "one model" in the sense that matters to leadership. If a single pure model is truly required, the answer depends on whether next quarter includes Christmas. Given today's date, it probably does (Q4). That makes B's 40% Christmas WAPE the deciding risk, not the 2-point overall gap.

## 1. What the numbers show when split by segment

WAPE breaks down as a volume-weighted average of segment WAPEs. That holds if your 70/10/20 shares are shares of actual volume in the evaluation window. Under that assumption, I can back out the missing "in-between" segment:

| Segment (volume share) | Model A | Model B | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staples (70%) | 14% | 17% | A |
| Mid-tier (20%), implied | **~32%** | **~21%** | B |
| New SKUs (10%) | 48% | 29% | B |
| Overall | 21% | 19% | B |
| Christmas weeks (2) | 26% | 40% | A |

How the implied mid-tier row is derived: A gives 21 − 0.7×14 − 0.1×48 = 6.4 points, and 6.4 / 0.2 ≈ 32%. B gives 19 − 11.9 − 2.9 = 4.2 points, and 4.2 / 0.2 ≈ 21%. Please check this against the real mid-tier figures. If they differ a lot, the segments weren't scored on the same basis.

What this means:
- **B's overall win comes from the mid-tier and new SKUs.** On staples, which are 70% of volume and probably most of the revenue at stake, A is better by 3 points.
- **A's 48% on new SKUs is structural, not bad tuning.** A seasonal naive model needs a year of history, and these SKUs have less than 6 months. A can't fix this. B can, by pooling information across SKUs.
- **B's Christmas failure is probably fixable.** A global model that misses Christmas usually lacks strong year-over-year signal: no lag-52 / same-week-last-year feature, holiday flags too coarse, or only one or two Christmases in the training data. A gets Christmas "for free" because it copies last year.

## 2. The hybrid probably beats both

If you route by segment (A for staples, B for everything else), the same arithmetic gives:

0.7×14 + 0.2×21 + 0.1×29 ≈ **16.9% WAPE**, compared with 19% for B and 21% for A.

Add a rule for the two Christmas weeks (use A, or a blend weighted toward A) and you also remove the worst-week risk. Caveats:
- This is an in-sample estimate from the same backtest you used to choose the routing. Expect some shrinkage out of sample.
- Segment boundaries need a clear rule, for example "at least 52 weeks of history and stable velocity counts as a staple". SKUs will move between segments over time.

You can present this to leadership as one pipeline with segment-aware logic. That is standard practice in retail forecasting, not two competing models.

## 3. If you must pick one pure model

- **Next quarter includes Christmas (likely, if it's Q4):** I would lean toward **A**, or at least not ship B unfixed. The two Christmas weeks are probably your highest-volume and highest-cost weeks. A 40% error there turns into empty shelves or perishable write-offs exactly when it hurts most. To settle it, recompute each model's WAPE for the whole quarter over the same 13-week window from last year, with Christmas volume weighted as it really is. That number decides the question, not the annual 19 vs 21.
- **Next quarter has no major holiday:** **B**. It wins overall and on 30% of volume, and it is the only one of the two that will keep up as the assortment changes.

## 4. Checks before you commit

1. **Same evaluation setup?** Both models need the same SKUs, weeks and forecast horizon, and a rolling-origin backtest, not a single split. A 2-point gap on one split may be noise. Compare weekly WAPE in pairs (for example with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on weekly differences) across folds.
2. **How many Christmases are in the test set?** If only one, "40% vs 26%" is n = 1 and fragile in both directions.
3. **Bias, not just WAPE.** WAPE is symmetric, but your costs aren't: over-forecasting perishables means waste, under-forecasting staples means lost sales and unhappy customers. Report mean signed error per segment. A model with slightly higher WAPE and less bias can be the better business choice.
4. **Revenue- or margin-weighted error.** Volume weighting may undervalue high-margin categories.
5. **Can B be fixed in the time you have?** Add same-week-last-year and more detailed holiday features, then backtest Christmas again. If B's Christmas WAPE drops near 26%, B alone becomes the clear pick.

**Recommendation, in priority order:**
1. Run the Q4-window backtest from section 3.
2. Ship B with A overrides for staples and Christmas as the Q4 system.
3. In parallel, try B with year-over-year features, to retire A next year if it closes the gap.


## Answer B
**Ship Model B, with one condition: if next quarter includes Christmas, B cannot run those weeks as it stands.** Outside the holidays, B wins where most of the error comes from. Its Christmas failure is serious enough that you need a holiday override or a fix before Q4. That override can stay inside "one model" as far as leadership is concerned.

### Where the error actually comes from

WAPE is volume-weighted, so overall WAPE is the volume-share-weighted sum of segment WAPEs. That lets us work out the middle segment you didn't report (assuming your 70/10/20 shares hold in the test period):

| Segment (volume share) | A WAPE | B WAPE | A error contribution | B error contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staples (70%) | 14% | 17% | 9.8 pts | 11.9 pts |
| New SKUs (10%) | 48% | 29% | 4.8 pts | 2.9 pts |
| Middle (20%) | **32%** (implied) | **21%** (implied) | 6.4 pts | 4.2 pts |
| **Total** | 21% | 19% | 21.0 | 19.0 |

- A's staples advantage is real but modest: 2.1 pts of total error.
- B gains 1.9 pts on new SKUs and, by implication, 2.2 pts on the middle tier. You didn't mention the middle-tier gap, and it may be the strongest argument for B. Confirm the 32% vs 21% figures directly, because they only hold if the shares are exact.
- B's structure also fits where the assortment is going. A seasonal naive model has no history to draw on for new launches, and every new SKU starts out that way. A global model shares information across SKUs. A's 48% on new SKUs will persist as long as you keep launching products.

### The Christmas problem

40% vs 26% over two weeks is not noise. The likely cause (my inference, not verified) is that a global model has seen only 2–3 Christmases in training. It can't learn the holiday spike from features that barely vary. Seasonal naive essentially copies last year's Christmas, which is exactly the right prior for that week.

Whether this outweighs B's advantage depends on the quarter. Here is an illustrative calculation with assumed numbers: 11 normal weeks plus 2 Christmas weeks at 1.5x normal volume, and B ahead by about 2.3 pts in normal weeks. B's normal-week gain is about 25 volume-weighted points. Its Christmas loss is about 14 × 3 = 42. **In a Q4 quarter, unmodified B would probably be worse than A overall.** Christmas weeks are also when forecast errors cost the most in stockouts and waste.

### What to ship

1. **Next quarter is not Q4:** ship B.
2. **Next quarter is Q4:** ship B with a rule-based holiday override. For Christmas weeks, and ideally other major holidays after checking them, use A's forecast or a blend weighted toward A. Better still, add A's seasonal-naive forecast to B as a feature. That usually fixes this kind of problem, and it keeps a single model artifact. Test it on the Christmas weeks before relying on it.
3. **Present it to leadership as one forecasting system with a documented holiday rule.** Most production retail forecasting works like this. Two competing models would be a real problem, but a model with a calendar override is not.

### Check before you commit

- **Is 19% vs 21% stable?** A 2-point gap from a single holdout split may not hold up. Run a rolling-origin backtest across several cutoffs, and compare paired per-week errors with a bootstrap over weeks or a Diebold-Mariano test. If the gap changes sign between cutoffs, the decision is closer than it looks.
- **Bias, not just WAPE.** WAPE treats over- and under-forecasting equally, and grocery costs don't. Over-forecasting perishables means waste, and under-forecasting staples means empty shelves. Check each model's signed bias by segment. If B systematically under-forecasts staples, its 3-point loss there costs more than the WAPE suggests.
- **Error weighted by cost.** If staples carry the traffic-driving, out-of-stock-sensitive items, weight the comparison by margin or stockout cost as well as volume. That's the scenario where A's 14% could win back the decision.
- **Other holidays.** Check Easter, Thanksgiving and local holidays for the same pattern. If B fails on all of them, the override needs to cover every major holiday, not just Christmas.
