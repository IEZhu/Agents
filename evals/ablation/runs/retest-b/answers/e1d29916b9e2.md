## Why onboarding, and why it's not a slam dunk

**For onboarding:**
- It's the largest stated churn reason (35% say "too hard to set up"). It also lines up with a behavioural signal: 40% of churned accounts never connected a bank. When survey data and product data agree, that's worth more than either one alone.
- An invoicing tool with no bank connected probably never reaches its core value, which is getting paid and reconciling. That makes bank connection a plausible activation event.

**The two holes in that case:**
1. **The 40% has no base rate.** If 35–40% of *retained* accounts also never connected a bank, the stat tells you nothing. If only 10% of retained accounts didn't connect, it's a strong signal. You need that comparison.
2. **Churn *rose* over two quarters, so something changed.** Onboarding friction probably existed before the rise. The three outages happened *this quarter*. So it's quite possible that reliability explains more of the *increase* than onboarding does, even if onboarding explains more of the baseline. Also, onboarding fixes only help new cohorts, and the payoff shows up months later. Reliability fixes protect all ~1,200 accounts right away.

That's why I wouldn't frame this as onboarding vs. reliability. Invoicing is mission-critical for your customers' cash flow. Three outages in a quarter are a retention risk you can't choose to skip.

## Why not QuickBooks two-way sync (this quarter)

- **36 of 54 eng-weeks is two-thirds of capacity on one project**, and two-way accounting sync is the kind of work that usually overruns: conflict resolution, edge cases, API limits. If it slips, the quarter slips with it.
- **"Top request in sales calls" is an acquisition signal, not a retention one.** Your problem is churn. And the 25% who said "missing integrations" didn't necessarily mean QuickBooks. You don't know that split yet.
- Cheaper ways to test demand: a one-way export, or a connector through an integration platform. Also check whether QuickBooks users churn more than non-users.

## What to check in week 1 (before committing)

1. **Churn by tenure cohort**, before vs. after the rise. If the increase is concentrated in accounts under 90 days old, go with onboarding. If it's in tenured accounts, reliability or competitive pressure is the bigger lever, and you should rebalance.
2. **Bank-connection rate: retained vs. churned accounts.** This supplies the missing base rate.
3. **Churn among accounts hit by the outages or heavy PDF users** vs. everyone else.
4. **Breakdown of "missing integrations"** by the integration named.
5. **The 20% who cited price.** Did you change pricing, or did a competitor launch? None of the three proposals addresses this, so flag it to the CEO separately.

## Metrics to commit to

- **Onboarding:** % of new accounts that connect a bank within 7 days (leading indicator), and 90-day churn of Q4 signup cohorts (lagging).
- **Reliability:** zero Sev-1 outages in Q4, and a PDF p95 target the Core squad agrees is achievable.
- **Overall:** a monthly churn target back toward ~2%. Be honest that most of the onboarding impact will show up in Q1, not by the end of Q4.

## One thing that costs zero engineering

Onboarding fixes won't reach existing accounts that never connected a bank. Have CS or success run a white-glove bank-connection push for those accounts now. That's the fastest retention win available, and it also tests the onboarding hypothesis: if connected accounts stop churning, you've validated the flagship before it ships.

**How to pitch it to the CEO:** "Our flagship is fixing activation, because it's the biggest churn driver we can see. We're funding reliability as a floor, not a bet, because outages put the whole base at risk. QuickBooks goes to discovery until we know it moves retention and not just sales demos."
