## Latest user message
My writing group says this opening paragraph of my short story 'feels flat' but nobody could tell me why. Can you rewrite it so it's stronger? Keep it about the same length (under 180 words) and keep all the facts, including Dave being annoyed with her. Then tell me in 2-3 sentences what you changed.

"Maya walked into her grandmother's kitchen for the first time since the funeral. She felt very sad and a little guilty, because she hadn't visited much in the last two years since she moved to Chicago for her job at the consulting firm, which had been really demanding. Her grandmother had raised her after her parents divorced when she was seven, so they had been very close. Her uncle Dave, standing by the sink, thought Maya looked tired and was annoyed that she had come so late to help sort things out. Maya looked around the kitchen and felt overwhelmed by memories. Everything reminded her of her grandmother. She realized she would never cook with her again, and she started to cry."

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. The rewrite stays in one viewpoint (Maya's): Uncle Dave's thoughts are not narrated from inside, and his annoyance is kept but shown only through what Maya can observe (his tone, gesture, a remark)
2. Maya's grief and guilt are conveyed through concrete detail (objects, sensory details, actions) rather than stated with labels like 'sad', 'guilty' or 'overwhelmed'
3. The backstory (Chicago job, being raised by her grandmother after the divorce) is compressed or folded into the scene rather than delivered as a block of explanation, while the facts are not contradicted
4. The rewrite stays under about 180 words and is followed by a short 2-3 sentence explanation that correctly names what was changed

## Answer A
The kitchen still smelled of cumin and dish soap, as if nobody had told it. Maya stopped in the doorway, her first time back since the funeral.

"You look tired," said her uncle Dave from the sink. He didn't turn around. "Would've been nice to have you here Tuesday. Or last week."

Every answer she had started with Chicago. Two years at the consulting firm, eighty-hour weeks, visits shrinking to phone calls, then to texts on Sundays. And before Chicago, this room, and the woman who had taken in a seven-year-old after the divorce and raised her here without once making it sound like a favor.

The flour tin with the dented lid. The wooden spoon, scorched black at the tip. The step stool Maya used to stand on to stir the rice, still tucked beside the stove.

She picked up the spoon. Nobody would hand it to her again and say, *Slower, let it catch.*

Dave turned the tap on hard. Under the noise, she let herself cry.

---

**What I changed:** Your original tells us Maya's feelings ("very sad," "a little guilty," "overwhelmed by memories"), so I cut those labels and let the guilt, closeness, and grief come through things she sees, touches, and can't say. I also fixed a point-of-view slip: the paragraph jumped into Dave's head, so now his tiredness comment and his annoyance come out in what he says and does, and we stay with Maya. Two small details are mine, not yours (the "eighty-hour weeks" and the cooking objects), so swap in the real ones if they don't fit your story.


## Answer B
The kitchen still smelled of cardamom and dish soap, as if her grandmother had only stepped out to the garden. Maya stopped in the doorway. It was her first time back since the funeral.

Uncle Dave stood at the sink, sleeves pushed up, newspaper-wrapped plates stacked beside him. He looked her over. "You look tired." Then, drying his hands harder than he needed to: "We started Tuesday."

Two years in Chicago, at a consulting firm that had eaten her weekends, then her holidays, then the phone calls she kept meaning to return. She had told herself there would be time.

She had grown up in this kitchen, from the autumn her parents divorced, when she was seven. Her grandmother had tied an apron twice around her waist and handed her a wooden spoon. The spoon was still in the crock by the stove, its edge worn flat.

Maya picked it up. She would never stand at this stove beside her again.

She was crying before she could put it down.

---

**What I changed:** The original names every feeling ("very sad," "a little guilty," "overwhelmed by memories"), so the reader is told what to feel instead of feeling it; I swapped those labels for concrete things (the smell, the worn spoon, the excuse she made to herself) and let one object stand in for "everything reminded her." I also moved Dave's annoyance and his view that she looks tired out of his head and into what he says and does, which keeps the paragraph in Maya's point of view and adds tension. The backstory is now broken into short pieces between the present-moment details, so the scene keeps moving and the paragraph builds to her tears.
