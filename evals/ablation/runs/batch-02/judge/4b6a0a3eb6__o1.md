## Answer A
No, don't double it yet. The dose probably isn't the problem. How and when you take the iron is. Your current routine is close to a worst case for absorption:

- **Calcium tablet at the same time.** Calcium inhibits both heme and non-heme iron absorption, and you are taking a full supplement dose alongside the iron.
- **Latte.** Coffee polyphenols bind iron, and the milk adds more calcium.
- **Greek yogurt.** More calcium and dairy protein.
- **Granola.** If it's whole-grain or oat-based, it contains phytates, which also block absorption.

Only a small fraction of oral iron gets absorbed even in ideal conditions. Take away most of that fraction and a ferritin gain of 3 ng/mL over 5 months is roughly what you'd expect. Doubling to 130 mg under the same conditions mostly doubles the GI side effects.

### Why "morning and night" is also the wrong direction
Iron absorption is regulated by **hepcidin**, a hormone that blocks iron uptake in the gut:

1. **An iron dose raises hepcidin for about 24 hours.** So a second dose the same day, or even the next morning, is absorbed less well. Studies in iron-depleted young women (Stoffel, Zimmermann et al., Lancet Haematology 2017 and 2020; recalled, not verified here, so confirm the details) found that **alternate-day dosing** gave higher fractional absorption than daily or split dosing. Total iron absorbed over the period was similar or better, with fewer side effects.
2. **Hepcidin rises over the course of the day**, so evening doses are absorbed worse than morning ones.
3. **Exercise also raises hepcidin**, peaking roughly 3–6 hours after a hard or long session. Most of this research comes from Peeling's group in endurance athletes (recalled, not verified). The usual practical advice is to take iron in the morning, before training or within about 30–60 minutes after it, and not in the afternoon following a morning long run.

### A better protocol to bring to your doctor
- **Dose:** 60–65 mg elemental iron, **every other day**. Keep the same pill and take it less often.
- **Timing:** morning, on an empty stomach (or with a small non-dairy snack if you get nausea). Ideally before your run or right after it.
- **Take with:** vitamin C, such as a glass of orange juice or about 100–200 mg ascorbic acid. The benefit is modest but real.
- **Separate by at least 2 hours from:** the calcium+D tablet (move it to dinner or bedtime), coffee and tea, and dairy.
- **Food with it:** a meal containing heme iron (meat, fish) helps. Dairy and whole grains do the opposite.
- **Retest:** ferritin, hemoglobin, transferrin saturation and CRP after 8–12 weeks. Adding CRP matters because inflammation, including from a recent hard race block, can push ferritin up and hide true iron status.

### What else to raise with your doctor
If ferritin still barely moves once absorption is fixed, don't keep raising the oral dose. Ask about:
- **Iron losses:** heavy menstrual bleeding is the most common cause in your group. GI losses and foot-strike hemolysis add some at 60 km/week, but usually not enough to explain it on their own.
- **Absorption problems:** celiac disease screening (tTG-IgA) is a standard step when oral iron doesn't work. *H. pylori* and PPI use are other causes.
- **IV iron:** a reasonable option when oral iron fails or you need a faster fix. It's a medical decision, so don't self-prescribe it.

### About feeling flat on long runs
A ferritin of 17 in a female endurance athlete is low. Many sports-medicine sources treat roughly 30–35 ng/mL as the minimum for athletes (thresholds vary, so confirm with your doctor), so iron is a plausible cause. It isn't the only one, though. At 60 km/week, also check whether you're eating enough overall. Low energy availability (RED-S) causes the same flat feeling, lowers iron status, and is common in female marathoners. Signs include irregular or missing periods, weight loss, and frequent injuries.

**Stop the iron and contact your doctor** if you get black stools with abdominal pain, or GI symptoms bad enough that you can't stay consistent.

*Not medical advice. Check any change in dose or schedule with your doctor, especially since they prescribed the current plan.*


## Answer B
No, I wouldn't double it. Your dose is probably fine. The problem is almost certainly how much of it you're absorbing, and splitting it into morning and night doses would likely make that worse.

## Why ferritin barely moved

You're taking the iron with almost everything that blocks it:

| What you take it with | Effect on non-heme iron absorption |
|---|---|
| **Calcium + D tablet** | Calcium inhibits iron absorption directly. It's one of the strongest dietary blockers. |
| **Latte (coffee)** | Coffee polyphenols can cut absorption substantially. Studies have reported drops of around 40-60% from a cup of coffee with a meal (recalled, not verified). |
| **Greek yogurt + milk** | More calcium, plus casein. |
| **Granola (whole grains)** | Phytates bind iron. |

So you've probably been absorbing a small fraction of the 65 mg. Doubling a dose that mostly isn't getting in doesn't fix that.

## Why morning + night is the wrong move

The mechanism is **hepcidin**, the liver hormone that shuts down iron uptake in the gut. An oral iron dose raises hepcidin for roughly 24 hours, so a second dose the same day gets absorbed less well. Work from Stoffel and colleagues (Lancet Haematology 2017 and 2020, recalled, so check the details) found that in iron-deficient young women, a single dose on **alternate days** gave higher fractional absorption, and similar or better total absorption, than daily or split dosing, with fewer GI side effects.

Exercise also raises hepcidin, peaking about 3-6 hours after a hard or long session. At 60 km/week that's relevant for you.

## What I'd suggest (take this to your doctor)

1. **Keep 65 mg elemental iron, but take it every other day** instead of daily. If your doctor wants a higher single dose, it goes in **one** morning dose, never split.
2. **Take it on an empty stomach** with water and a vitamin C source, like a glass of orange juice or about 100-200 mg vitamin C (recalled range). Vitamin C helps keep iron in the absorbable ferrous form.
3. **Timing around training**: take it in the morning, either before your run or within about 30-60 minutes afterward, before the exercise-driven hepcidin rise kicks in. Avoid taking it in the afternoon after a hard session.
4. **Separate the blockers**: no coffee, dairy or calcium for about 1-2 hours before and after. Move the calcium + D tablet to lunch or dinner.
5. **Retest in 8-12 weeks**, and ask for more than ferritin: CBC with hemoglobin, **transferrin saturation**, and **CRP**. Ferritin rises with inflammation, and a hard training block can push it up too, so a low ferritin with normal CRP is more trustworthy.

## Worth raising with your doctor

- **Why you're losing iron in the first place.** Common causes for a female marathoner are heavy menstrual bleeding, foot-strike hemolysis, sweat and GI losses, and low dietary intake. Heavy periods are the most common big drain and are worth addressing directly.
- **Celiac screening (tTG-IgA).** Poor response to oral iron is a classic prompt to check for it.
- **Energy availability.** Low energy availability (RED-S) can also cause flat long runs and can blunt iron status.
- **IV iron** if oral iron still fails after you've fixed the absorption issues. It's a legitimate option for athletes who don't respond to oral iron, and it's a decision for your doctor.

## About feeling flat

A ferritin of 17 is low for an endurance athlete. Sports medicine sources often aim for at least 30-50 ng/mL (recalled; exact cutoffs vary between guidelines). Your flat long runs plausibly fit iron deficiency even if your hemoglobin is normal. Until your levels come up, I'd keep long runs mostly Zone 2 and hold off on adding volume.

**See your doctor promptly if** you notice shortness of breath at easy effort, a racing heart at rest, dizziness, or black or tarry stools *together with* abdominal pain. Iron by itself darkens stool and that's normal, but pain alongside it isn't.

*Not medical advice. Consult a doctor before changing your dose or schedule.*
