No, don't double it yet. The dose probably isn't the problem. How and when you take the iron is. Your current routine is close to a worst case for absorption:

- **Calcium tablet at the same time.** Calcium inhibits both heme and non-heme iron absorption, and you are taking a full supplement dose alongside the iron.
- **Latte.** Coffee polyphenols bind iron, and the milk adds more calcium.
- **Greek yogurt.** More calcium and dairy protein.
- **Granola.** If it's whole-grain or oat-based, it contains phytates, which also block absorption.

Only a small fraction of oral iron gets absorbed even in ideal conditions. Take away most of that fraction and a ferritin gain of 3 ng/mL over 5 months is roughly what you'd expect. Doubling to 130 mg under the same conditions mostly doubles the GI side effects.

### Why "morning and night" is also the wrong direction
Iron absorption is regulated by **hepcidin**, a hormone that blocks iron uptake in the gut:

1. **An iron dose raises hepcidin for about 24 hours.** So a second dose the same day, or even the next morning, is absorbed less well. Studies in iron-depleted young women (Stoffel, Zimmermann et al., Lancet Haematology 2017 and 2020; recalled, not verified here, so confirm the details) found that **alternate-day dosing** gave higher fractional absorption than daily or split dosing. Total iron absorbed over the period was similar or better, with fewer side effects.
2. **Hepcidin rises over the course of the day**, so evening doses are absorbed worse than morning ones.
3. **Exercise also raises hepcidin**, peaking roughly 3–6 hours after a hard or long session. Most of this research comes from Peeling's group in endurance athletes (recalled, not verified). The usual practical advice is to take iron in the morning, before training or within about 30–60 minutes after it, and not in the afternoon following a morning long run.

### A better protocol to bring to your doctor
- **Dose:** 60–65 mg elemental iron, **every other day**. Keep the same pill and take it less often.
- **Timing:** morning, on an empty stomach (or with a small non-dairy snack if you get nausea). Ideally before your run or right after it.
- **Take with:** vitamin C, such as a glass of orange juice or about 100–200 mg ascorbic acid. The benefit is modest but real.
- **Separate by at least 2 hours from:** the calcium+D tablet (move it to dinner or bedtime), coffee and tea, and dairy.
- **Food with it:** a meal containing heme iron (meat, fish) helps. Dairy and whole grains do the opposite.
- **Retest:** ferritin, hemoglobin, transferrin saturation and CRP after 8–12 weeks. Adding CRP matters because inflammation, including from a recent hard race block, can push ferritin up and hide true iron status.

### What else to raise with your doctor
If ferritin still barely moves once absorption is fixed, don't keep raising the oral dose. Ask about:
- **Iron losses:** heavy menstrual bleeding is the most common cause in your group. GI losses and foot-strike hemolysis add some at 60 km/week, but usually not enough to explain it on their own.
- **Absorption problems:** celiac disease screening (tTG-IgA) is a standard step when oral iron doesn't work. *H. pylori* and PPI use are other causes.
- **IV iron:** a reasonable option when oral iron fails or you need a faster fix. It's a medical decision, so don't self-prescribe it.

### About feeling flat on long runs
A ferritin of 17 in a female endurance athlete is low. Many sports-medicine sources treat roughly 30–35 ng/mL as the minimum for athletes (thresholds vary, so confirm with your doctor), so iron is a plausible cause. It isn't the only one, though. At 60 km/week, also check whether you're eating enough overall. Low energy availability (RED-S) causes the same flat feeling, lowers iron status, and is common in female marathoners. Signs include irregular or missing periods, weight loss, and frequent injuries.

**Stop the iron and contact your doctor** if you get black stools with abdominal pain, or GI symptoms bad enough that you can't stay consistent.
