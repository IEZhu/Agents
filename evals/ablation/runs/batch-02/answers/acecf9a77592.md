No, I wouldn't double it. Your dose is probably fine. The problem is almost certainly how much of it you're absorbing, and splitting it into morning and night doses would likely make that worse.

## Why ferritin barely moved

You're taking the iron with almost everything that blocks it:

| What you take it with | Effect on non-heme iron absorption |
|---|---|
| **Calcium + D tablet** | Calcium inhibits iron absorption directly. It's one of the strongest dietary blockers. |
| **Latte (coffee)** | Coffee polyphenols can cut absorption substantially. Studies have reported drops of around 40-60% from a cup of coffee with a meal (recalled, not verified). |
| **Greek yogurt + milk** | More calcium, plus casein. |
| **Granola (whole grains)** | Phytates bind iron. |

So you've probably been absorbing a small fraction of the 65 mg. Doubling a dose that mostly isn't getting in doesn't fix that.

## Why morning + night is the wrong move

The mechanism is **hepcidin**, the liver hormone that shuts down iron uptake in the gut. An oral iron dose raises hepcidin for roughly 24 hours, so a second dose the same day gets absorbed less well. Work from Stoffel and colleagues (Lancet Haematology 2017 and 2020, recalled, so check the details) found that in iron-deficient young women, a single dose on **alternate days** gave higher fractional absorption, and similar or better total absorption, than daily or split dosing, with fewer GI side effects.

Exercise also raises hepcidin, peaking about 3-6 hours after a hard or long session. At 60 km/week that's relevant for you.

## What I'd suggest (take this to your doctor)

1. **Keep 65 mg elemental iron, but take it every other day** instead of daily. If your doctor wants a higher single dose, it goes in **one** morning dose, never split.
2. **Take it on an empty stomach** with water and a vitamin C source, like a glass of orange juice or about 100-200 mg vitamin C (recalled range). Vitamin C helps keep iron in the absorbable ferrous form.
3. **Timing around training**: take it in the morning, either before your run or within about 30-60 minutes afterward, before the exercise-driven hepcidin rise kicks in. Avoid taking it in the afternoon after a hard session.
4. **Separate the blockers**: no coffee, dairy or calcium for about 1-2 hours before and after. Move the calcium + D tablet to lunch or dinner.
5. **Retest in 8-12 weeks**, and ask for more than ferritin: CBC with hemoglobin, **transferrin saturation**, and **CRP**. Ferritin rises with inflammation, and a hard training block can push it up too, so a low ferritin with normal CRP is more trustworthy.

## Worth raising with your doctor

- **Why you're losing iron in the first place.** Common causes for a female marathoner are heavy menstrual bleeding, foot-strike hemolysis, sweat and GI losses, and low dietary intake. Heavy periods are the most common big drain and are worth addressing directly.
- **Celiac screening (tTG-IgA).** Poor response to oral iron is a classic prompt to check for it.
- **Energy availability.** Low energy availability (RED-S) can also cause flat long runs and can blunt iron status.
- **IV iron** if oral iron still fails after you've fixed the absorption issues. It's a legitimate option for athletes who don't respond to oral iron, and it's a decision for your doctor.

## About feeling flat

A ferritin of 17 is low for an endurance athlete. Sports medicine sources often aim for at least 30-50 ng/mL (recalled; exact cutoffs vary between guidelines). Your flat long runs plausibly fit iron deficiency even if your hemoglobin is normal. Until your levels come up, I'd keep long runs mostly Zone 2 and hold off on adding volume.

**See your doctor promptly if** you notice shortness of breath at easy effort, a racing heart at rest, dizziness, or black or tarry stools *together with* abdominal pain. Iron by itself darkens stool and that's normal, but pain alongside it isn't.

*Not medical advice. Consult a doctor before changing your dose or schedule.*
