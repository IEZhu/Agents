## Answer A
**Short answer (Spain, Andalucía):** You don't have to find a way around the €3,000 minimum, because for an SL it no longer exists. Since **Ley 18/2022 ("Ley Crea y Crece")** the minimum share capital of a Sociedad Limitada is **€1**. The guides you're reading are out of date. You can form an SL with a very small capital, and the trade-offs are manageable. Whether an SL or autónomo status suits you better is a separate question, and at a small scale autónomo often wins anyway (see below).

## 1. The €1 SL: what the law says

Ley 18/2022 amended the **Ley de Sociedades de Capital (LSC)**. As I recall the rules (not checked here, so confirm against the consolidated LSC on boe.es):

- **Art. 4.1 LSC**: SL minimum capital lowered from €3,000 to **€1**.
- **Art. 4.2 LSC (safeguards while capital is below €3,000)**:
  - **Legal reserve**: at least **20% of annual profit** must go to the legal reserve until the reserve plus capital reach €3,000.
  - **Liquidation shortfall**: if the company is wound up (voluntarily or forcibly) and its assets can't pay its debts, the shareholders are **jointly liable for the gap between the capital subscribed and €3,000**. In practice your personal exposure is capped at roughly €3,000 minus what you put in. It is not unlimited liability.
- The old *sociedad en régimen de formación sucesiva* was repealed by the same law. The €1 route replaces it.

**The practical point:** a low-capital SL still gives you limited liability. You just carry a contingent exposure of up to about €3,000 if it fails with unpaid debts, and part of your early profits is locked in the reserve.

**Other options that don't involve cash:**
- **Non-cash contributions (aportaciones no dinerarias, Arts. 63–64 LSC)**: you can pay in capital with assets such as your computer, software licences or equipment. There's no bank certificate for these. The deed describes the assets and values them, and you as founder are liable for that valuation (Art. 73 LSC, recalled).
- **Bank deposit certificate**: for cash contributions the notary normally needs a bank certificate of deposit (Art. 62 LSC). With €1–€500 of capital this is trivial, although some banks charge for opening the account. Shop around.
- **Online formation via CIRCE/PAE** with standard-form articles (*estatutos tipo*) is the cheapest and fastest route. Setup is typically a few hundred euros in notary and registry fees (estimate, not verified).

## 2. Check this first: your immigration status

As a US citizen, **an NIE is only an identification number. It does not authorise you to work in Spain.** Before you trade in Spain, either as an autónomo or as the working director of your own SL, your residence permit must cover **self-employment (trabajo por cuenta propia)**:

- A **residencia no lucrativa** does **not** allow work. Starting a business on it could put renewal at risk.
- The **digital nomad visa** (Ley 14/2013 as amended by Ley 28/2022) is aimed at work for foreign clients. As I understand it, it allows only a limited share of work for Spanish clients. Check your specific conditions.
- A permit for **cuenta propia**, or the **entrepreneur residence route** (Ley 14/2013), covers running your own business.

If you're not sure which permit you have, check this before choosing between SL and autónomo. It is the bigger risk here.

## 3. SL or autónomo at your size

| | Autónomo | SL (sole shareholder and director) |
|---|---|---|
| Setup cost | Close to zero. Register with the AEAT (Modelo 036/037) and with RETA | Notary, Registro Mercantil, bank; a few hundred € |
| Social security | RETA quota based on your actual income (system since 2023). New starters get the *tarifa plana* of about €80/month for 12 months (recalled; verify current amount and conditions) | You will usually still pay RETA as an **autónomo societario** if you control and work for the company, generally with a higher minimum contribution base and, as I recall, no *tarifa plana* in many cases |
| Tax | IRPF, progressive (state plus Andalucía scale) | IS at 25%, or **15%** for the first profitable years of a new company (Art. 29.1 LIS, recalled). Salary and dividends are then taxed again in IRPF |
| Liability | Unlimited, personal | Limited, apart from the ~€3,000 shortfall rule above |
| Admin | Quarterly VAT/IRPF filings, simple bookkeeping | Company accounts, annual deposit of accounts, corporate tax return; in practice you'll need a *gestor* (~€100–200+/month, estimate) |

**Rule of thumb, which is a judgment call and not a legal rule:** for a one-person web design business with modest profits, being an autónomo is usually cheaper and simpler. An SL starts to pay off once profits are high enough that the IS rate beats your marginal IRPF rate, or when liability protection matters, for example with larger client contracts. Ask a *gestor* in Málaga to run your actual numbers.

## 4. The US side

As a US citizen you stay taxable in the US on worldwide income, and the structure changes what you file there. From memory, to be confirmed with a US/Spain cross-border tax advisor:
- **SL**: by default a single-owner SL is usually treated as a **foreign corporation**. That brings **Form 5471** and possible GILTI/Subpart F issues. You can elect disregarded-entity treatment on **Form 8832**, which changes the picture.
- **Autónomo**: reported on Schedule C. The **US–Spain totalization agreement** should stop you paying social security in both countries (you'd get a certificate of coverage for Spain). Foreign tax credits and the FEIE interact with Spanish IRPF.
- **FBAR/FATCA** reporting applies to Spanish accounts in either case.

## Answer B
**Short answer:** The €3,000 minimum no longer applies. Since the *Ley Crea y Crece* (Ley 18/2022) came into force in late 2022, you can form a Spanish SL with **as little as €1** in share capital. There are strings attached, covered below. The guides you've been reading are out of date.

Before choosing between an SL and autónomo, though, check one thing. **A NIE is only a tax ID number. It does not give you the right to work in Spain.** More on that at the end, because it matters more than the capital question.

## 1. The €1 SL: what the law says now

Ley 18/2022 amended **Art. 4 of the Ley de Sociedades de Capital (LSC, RDLeg. 1/2010)**. The SL minimum capital dropped from €3,000 to €1. In exchange, companies with capital **below €3,000** get two protective rules. This is from memory, not checked against the current consolidated text on BOE.es:

- **Mandatory reserve:** at least **20% of annual profit** goes to the legal reserve until the reserve plus capital reaches €3,000. You can't pay yourself dividends from that portion.
- **Liquidation liability:** if the company is wound up and its assets can't cover its debts, the shareholders are **jointly liable for the gap between €3,000 and the capital actually subscribed**. With €1 of capital, your personal exposure is capped at about €2,999, not unlimited.

The same law **abolished the old "sociedad de formación sucesiva"** regime, which was the earlier workaround for the minimum.

**In practice:** many founders choose a symbolic amount such as €1, €100 or €500. You still need a bank certificate for a cash contribution (see below), but the amount is trivial.

## 2. Other options that avoid a cash deposit

Even with capital of €3,000 or more, a bank deposit is not strictly required:

- **Non-cash contributions (*aportación no dineraria*)**. You can contribute existing business assets such as your computer, software licences or domain names. The deed has to describe and value them. For an SL, **no independent expert valuation is required**, but the founders are **jointly liable for the accuracy of that valuation** (Arts. 63 and 73 LSC, recalled, verify). Don't inflate the value.
- **Founders' declaration instead of a bank certificate**. **Art. 62.2 LSC** lets SL founders skip proving cash contributions at the notary. To do this, they declare in the deed that they are **jointly liable** to the company and its creditors for the contributions being real. The money still has to exist. This only removes the proof step, and notaries and banks vary in how willing they are to use it. Recalled, not verified: confirm with your notary.

## 3. Capital is not the real cost difference

Rough comparison for a one-person web design business. All figures are recalled, not verified for 2026, so confirm with a *gestor*.

| | Autónomo | SL (you as sole director and shareholder) |
|---|---|---|
| Setup cost | ~€0 (Modelo 036/037 plus RETA registration) | Notary + Registro Mercantil + gestor, typically a few hundred €. Online formation via CIRCE/PAE can be cheaper |
| Social Security | RETA. *Tarifa plana* of ~€80/month for the first 12 months for new autónomos | **You still pay RETA** as an *autónomo societario*, because a controlling director must register. The SL does not remove this cost |
| Income tax | IRPF, progressive, reaching the high 40s % at the top depending on the Comunidad Autónoma | Impuesto sobre Sociedades. 25% general rate, 15% for the first two profitable years of a new company, and reduced micro-company rates being phased in under Ley 7/2024. Extracting money as salary or dividends is taxed again in IRPF |
| Accounting | Simplified books, quarterly 130/303 filings | Full double-entry accounts, annual accounts deposited at the Registro, Modelo 200, more gestor fees (often €100–250+/month) |
| Liability | **Unlimited** personal liability | Limited (subject to the €3,000 rule above) |

**Rule of thumb (a judgment call, not a law):** an SL usually starts to pay off once net profit reaches roughly €40–60k a year, or when you need limited liability because of a large client or contractual risk. Below that, autónomo is cheaper and simpler. Web design is generally low liability, and professional indemnity insurance covers most of it.

## 4. Two US-specific points guides for Spaniards leave out

- **US tax on a Spanish SL.** As a US citizen you are taxed on worldwide income. A Spanish SL you control is a **controlled foreign corporation**. It likely means filing **Form 5471** and dealing with the anti-deferral regimes (Subpart F, and what used to be called GILTI, renamed and modified by the 2025 tax law). An SL is not on the "per se corporation" list, so you can elect via **Form 8832 (check-the-box)** to treat it as a disregarded entity for US purposes. That often simplifies things a lot. All of this is recalled, not verified: get a US expat CPA before forming the company. Compliance costs here can outweigh any Spanish advantage.
- **Autónomo is simpler on the US side.** Your income goes on **Schedule C**, and Spanish IRPF offsets US tax through the foreign tax credit (Form 1116). The **US–Spain Totalization Agreement** means that paying Spanish RETA should exempt you from US self-employment tax. You'd need a certificate of coverage from the Seguridad Social (recalled, verify).

## 5. The threshold issue: are you allowed to work?

Your NIE on its own doesn't let you register as autónomo or actively run your own SL. You need a status that allows self-employment:

- **Non-lucrative visa (*residencia no lucrativa*):** **no work is allowed**, either as an employee or self-employed. Setting up either structure would breach your permit conditions.
- **Digital nomad / international teleworker visa (Ley 14/2013, as amended by Ley 28/2022):** this covers remote work for **foreign** clients. Income from **Spanish clients is capped at around 20%** of your work (recalled, verify).
- **Cuenta propia (self-employment) residence and work authorisation,** or long-term residence: needed to freely run a Spain-facing business.

If you're currently on a non-lucrative visa, sort this out before anything else. It is a bigger legal risk than any capital question.

## Suggested path

1. **Confirm your immigration status allows self-employment.** If it doesn't, talk to an immigration lawyer first.
2. If it does, **register as autónomo now** and use the *tarifa plana* for the first year. Cash is tight and profits are probably modest, so this is likely the better choice anyway, not just a stopgap.
3. **Reconsider an SL** once profits approach €40–60k or liability becomes a real concern. When you do, you won't need €3,000: a €1–€500 SL is legal.
4. **Speak to a US expat CPA before forming the SL,** not after.
