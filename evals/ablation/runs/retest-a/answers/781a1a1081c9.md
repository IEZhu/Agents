**Short answer (Spain, Andalucía):** You don't have to find a way around the €3,000 minimum, because for an SL it no longer exists. Since **Ley 18/2022 ("Ley Crea y Crece")** the minimum share capital of a Sociedad Limitada is **€1**. The guides you're reading are out of date. You can form an SL with a very small capital, and the trade-offs are manageable. Whether an SL or autónomo status suits you better is a separate question, and at a small scale autónomo often wins anyway (see below).

## 1. The €1 SL: what the law says

Ley 18/2022 amended the **Ley de Sociedades de Capital (LSC)**. As I recall the rules (not checked here, so confirm against the consolidated LSC on boe.es):

- **Art. 4.1 LSC**: SL minimum capital lowered from €3,000 to **€1**.
- **Art. 4.2 LSC (safeguards while capital is below €3,000)**:
  - **Legal reserve**: at least **20% of annual profit** must go to the legal reserve until the reserve plus capital reach €3,000.
  - **Liquidation shortfall**: if the company is wound up (voluntarily or forcibly) and its assets can't pay its debts, the shareholders are **jointly liable for the gap between the capital subscribed and €3,000**. In practice your personal exposure is capped at roughly €3,000 minus what you put in. It is not unlimited liability.
- The old *sociedad en régimen de formación sucesiva* was repealed by the same law. The €1 route replaces it.

**The practical point:** a low-capital SL still gives you limited liability. You just carry a contingent exposure of up to about €3,000 if it fails with unpaid debts, and part of your early profits is locked in the reserve.

**Other options that don't involve cash:**
- **Non-cash contributions (aportaciones no dinerarias, Arts. 63–64 LSC)**: you can pay in capital with assets such as your computer, software licences or equipment. There's no bank certificate for these. The deed describes the assets and values them, and you as founder are liable for that valuation (Art. 73 LSC, recalled).
- **Bank deposit certificate**: for cash contributions the notary normally needs a bank certificate of deposit (Art. 62 LSC). With €1–€500 of capital this is trivial, although some banks charge for opening the account. Shop around.
- **Online formation via CIRCE/PAE** with standard-form articles (*estatutos tipo*) is the cheapest and fastest route. Setup is typically a few hundred euros in notary and registry fees (estimate, not verified).

## 2. Check this first: your immigration status

As a US citizen, **an NIE is only an identification number. It does not authorise you to work in Spain.** Before you trade in Spain, either as an autónomo or as the working director of your own SL, your residence permit must cover **self-employment (trabajo por cuenta propia)**:

- A **residencia no lucrativa** does **not** allow work. Starting a business on it could put renewal at risk.
- The **digital nomad visa** (Ley 14/2013 as amended by Ley 28/2022) is aimed at work for foreign clients. As I understand it, it allows only a limited share of work for Spanish clients. Check your specific conditions.
- A permit for **cuenta propia**, or the **entrepreneur residence route** (Ley 14/2013), covers running your own business.

If you're not sure which permit you have, check this before choosing between SL and autónomo. It is the bigger risk here.

## 3. SL or autónomo at your size

| | Autónomo | SL (sole shareholder and director) |
|---|---|---|
| Setup cost | Close to zero. Register with the AEAT (Modelo 036/037) and with RETA | Notary, Registro Mercantil, bank; a few hundred € |
| Social security | RETA quota based on your actual income (system since 2023). New starters get the *tarifa plana* of about €80/month for 12 months (recalled; verify current amount and conditions) | You will usually still pay RETA as an **autónomo societario** if you control and work for the company, generally with a higher minimum contribution base and, as I recall, no *tarifa plana* in many cases |
| Tax | IRPF, progressive (state plus Andalucía scale) | IS at 25%, or **15%** for the first profitable years of a new company (Art. 29.1 LIS, recalled). Salary and dividends are then taxed again in IRPF |
| Liability | Unlimited, personal | Limited, apart from the ~€3,000 shortfall rule above |
| Admin | Quarterly VAT/IRPF filings, simple bookkeeping | Company accounts, annual deposit of accounts, corporate tax return; in practice you'll need a *gestor* (~€100–200+/month, estimate) |

**Rule of thumb, which is a judgment call and not a legal rule:** for a one-person web design business with modest profits, being an autónomo is usually cheaper and simpler. An SL starts to pay off once profits are high enough that the IS rate beats your marginal IRPF rate, or when liability protection matters, for example with larger client contracts. Ask a *gestor* in Málaga to run your actual numbers.

## 4. The US side

As a US citizen you stay taxable in the US on worldwide income, and the structure changes what you file there. From memory, to be confirmed with a US/Spain cross-border tax advisor:
- **SL**: by default a single-owner SL is usually treated as a **foreign corporation**. That brings **Form 5471** and possible GILTI/Subpart F issues. You can elect disregarded-entity treatment on **Form 8832**, which changes the picture.
- **Autónomo**: reported on Schedule C. The **US–Spain totalization agreement** should stop you paying social security in both countries (you'd get a certificate of coverage for Spain). Foreign tax credits and the FEIE interact with Spanish IRPF.
- **FBAR/FATCA** reporting applies to Spanish accounts in either case.

## Next steps
1. Confirm your residence permit allows self-employment.
2. If it does and your profits are modest, register as an autónomo now: AEAT Modelo 036, then RETA via Import@ss, and apply for the tarifa plana.
3. When the numbers justify it, convert to an SL. You can form it with €1 or more, or with non-cash contributions, via CIRCE/PAE.
4. Coordinate with a US-aware tax advisor before forming the SL.

**Risks:** the capital-shortfall liability and the profit reserve if you use a low-capital SL; working on the wrong permit; the US corporate reporting burden if you set up an SL without planning; and rates, quotas and thresholds that change every year.
