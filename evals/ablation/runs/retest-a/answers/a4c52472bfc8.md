## Bottom line

Push back on this plan. Adding Cypress specs will slow CI and make it more flaky, and it probably won't raise your coverage number much. Don't just say no on Thursday, though. Offer a plan that still makes real progress on coverage by Q1: unit and request specs written against a risk map, a gate on changed code, stabilizing the Cypress suite you already have, and a revised overall target that you can defend with numbers.

---

## Why the Cypress plan fails (with numbers)

**1. It may not move the metric at all.** Your coverage number almost certainly comes from SimpleCov, which measures the Ruby process running the tests. Cypress drives a browser against a separately booted Rails server. That server's lines only count if you start SimpleCov inside it and merge its results with the RSpec/Minitest results. It can be done, but it isn't the default, and it's easy to get wrong. Before Thursday, check how your coverage is actually collected. If E2E runs aren't instrumented, the contractors' work counts for nothing.

**2. Flakiness grows with suite size.** If 1 in 8 runs fails with 120 specs, the per-spec flake rate is about 0.11%, since (1 − p)^120 ≈ 0.875 gives p ≈ 0.0011. At the same rate:

| Total Cypress specs | P(a run is green) | Serial runtime (at ~23 s/spec) |
|---|---|---|
| 120 (today) | ~88% | 47 min |
| 400 | ~64% | ~2.6 h |
| 620 | ~50% | ~4 h |

With "every screen" covered, about half of all CI runs go red for reasons unrelated to the change being tested. At that point the team stops trusting red builds, and the whole suite stops protecting you.

**3. The gap is too big for this approach.** 90% of 180k LOC means about 106k more covered lines (from ~56k to ~162k). E2E tests mostly run the happy path of controllers and views. They rarely reach error branches, edge cases in service objects, background jobs, mailers or rake tasks, and those are where the uncovered lines usually are. Most E2E suites plateau well below 90% however many screens they visit (that's a judgment from experience, not a measured figure).

**4. Coverage from E2E is executed, not asserted.** A screen test that loads a page runs hundreds of lines and checks two of them. You would hit the number without getting the thing the VP presumably wants, which is confidence that changes don't break production.

**5. Contractors without domain knowledge write tests that check very little.** This problem exists at every layer of the test pyramid, but at the E2E layer it's also the slowest and most expensive kind.

## What to propose instead

### Track 1: Put the coverage work at the right layer (the contractors go here)
- **Request specs** (or integration tests) for controllers. One test covers routing, controller, model and serialization, and it runs in milliseconds, not ~23 seconds. This gets you the most covered lines per hour of effort.
- **Model and service specs** for domain logic, prioritized by a risk map:
  - churn (git log frequency) × complexity × incident history
  - billing, auth, permissions and data-mutation paths first
- **Characterization tests** for legacy code nobody understands. They pin down current behavior before anyone refactors it.
- Turn on **branch coverage** in SimpleCov (`enable_coverage :branch`, recalled, not verified; confirm for your SimpleCov version). Line coverage alone overstates how well you're tested.

### Track 2: Stop the bleeding with a gate on changed code
- Require about 90% coverage on changed and new lines in each PR. Codecov/Coveralls patch coverage or the `undercover` gem can enforce this (tool names recalled; pick whichever fits your CI).
- This is the cheapest way to raise overall coverage over time, and it fits the VP's intent. Every PR is covered from now on.
- Add a **ratchet**: CI fails if overall coverage drops below the last recorded value.

### Track 3: Shrink the denominator honestly
- In a 180k-LOC monolith, a good share of code is probably dead. Production coverage tooling such as **Coverband** (recalled; verify fit) shows which code never runs in production.
- Delete dead code. Don't exclude it in the SimpleCov config. Deleting code is legitimate; adding exclusions for live code is gaming the metric, and someone will eventually notice.

### Track 4: Fix Cypress before growing it
- **Quarantine** the flaky specs, and track and fix them: usually fixed sleeps, shared test data, no network stubbing, or animation timing.
- **Reduce** to about 20–40 critical user journeys (signup, checkout, core workflow). Push the rest down to request or system specs.
- **Parallelize** the suite across CI nodes. Target under 15 minutes wall-clock and under 1% flake rate per run.
- New Cypress specs only for journeys that no lower layer can test.

## Target to put on the table

Here's a rough estimate, with generous rounding. Assume a request or model spec adds ~30–100 covered lines, so ~106k lines takes about 1,500–3,000 meaningful tests. Two contractors at about 10 good tests per day each, over roughly 60 working days, produce about 1,200. **So 90% overall by end of Q1 is probably not reachable honestly**, even with the better approach. If your Q1 is a fiscal quarter that ends sooner, it's even less likely.

A proposal you can defend:

| Metric | By end of Q1 | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Changed-code coverage | ≥ 90%, enforced in CI from week 1 | Fits the mandate's intent, starting now |
| Critical domains (billing, auth, etc.) | ≥ 85–90% line + branch | Coverage where incidents cost the most |
| Overall line coverage | ~55–65%, ratcheted | Realistic, keeps rising after Q1 |
| Cypress | ≤ 15 min, < 1% flaky runs | Restores trust in CI |
| Dead code removed | measured in LOC | Honestly lowers the denominator |

Your team's velocity and your codebase could shift these numbers either way. Present them as an estimate to revisit after 3–4 weeks of real throughput data.

## How to run the meeting

1. **Open by agreeing with the goal:** "We need much more test coverage, and I support the mandate's intent."
2. **Show the flake table.** It's the strongest argument you have, because it's your own data taken to its conclusion.
3. **Ask the VP one question:** "What outcome is 90% standing in for? Fewer incidents, faster releases, safer refactoring?" If the answer is outcomes, your plan serves them better. If the literal number really matters (an audit or a customer contract), you still get there faster through Tracks 1–3 than through Cypress.
4. **Give the QA lead a real role.** They own the Cypress stabilization and the critical-journey list. That's a better use of their expertise, and it turns a disagreement into a joint plan.
5. **Commit to a checkpoint:** report coverage, test throughput and CI health at week 4, then re-forecast.

One risk to mention before anyone else does: if leadership only rewards the number, people will write tests that assert nothing. Spot-check contractor PRs, or run a mutation-testing tool like `mutant` (recalled) on one critical module, to show the new tests actually catch bugs.
