## Answer A
**Short version:** Those papers answer a different question from your dad's. They tested whether vitamin D helps people who *aren't* short of it. Your dad is. At 14 ng/mL he's clearly deficient, and correcting a deficiency is a standard reason to take vitamin D whatever those trials found. So the real question isn't "should he take it?" but "what dose has his doctor chosen, and is he actually taking it?"

## What the papers say, in plain terms

| Study | Who was in it | Result | Why it doesn't really apply to your dad |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VITAL** (2019) | ~25,900 generally healthy adults 50+ | 2000 IU/day didn't lower cancer or heart attacks/strokes overall. A secondary analysis hinted at fewer cancer deaths | Most participants had normal vitamin D levels to begin with. Adding more to a normal level isn't the same as fixing a low one |
| **D2d** (2019) | ~2,400 adults with prediabetes | Slightly fewer people progressed to diabetes (HR 0.88), but the difference wasn't statistically significant | This group is closest to your dad. Most participants still weren't deficient at the start |

**The takeaway to share with him:** vitamin D is not a general "prevent cancer, heart disease and diabetes" pill for people who already have enough. That doesn't touch his situation, because his level is low.

One more point, which I recall but haven't checked here: I believe a follow-up analysis of D2d found a possible benefit in the small subgroup with very low starting levels (roughly under 12 ng/mL). That was a post-hoc finding, so it's only a hint. It is still the subgroup closest to your dad, so it's worth asking his doctor about.

## Metformin

- **Vitamin D and metformin:** I'm not aware of any meaningful interaction between them.
- **Vitamin B12:** this is the one worth raising. Long-term metformin is well known to lower B12. If his doctor hasn't checked B12 recently, ask at the 3-month recheck. Low B12 can cause tingling or numbness in the feet, tiredness and memory problems, which at 68 are easy to put down to "just age."

## Answer B
**Short version:** these papers answer a different question from your dad's. They tested whether giving vitamin D to people who mostly weren't deficient prevents cancer, heart disease, diabetes or fractures. The answer was "not much." ## What each paper actually says

**1. VITAL (2019): cancer and heart disease**
- About 25,900 healthy adults aged 50 and over took 2000 IU/day or placebo for roughly 5 years.
- There was no overall drop in cancer or major heart events.
- A possible reduction in cancer deaths showed up only after leaving out the first 1–2 years. That's a secondary analysis, so treat it as something worth studying further, not as proof.
- **Takeaway:** if you're not deficient, taking vitamin D "just in case" doesn't clearly prevent cancer or heart disease.

**2. D2d (2019): prediabetes to diabetes**
- This is the one most relevant to your dad, because the participants had prediabetes like him.
- 4000 IU/day gave about 12% fewer people progressing to diabetes (HR 0.88), but the result wasn't statistically significant. So it could be a small real effect or it could be chance.
- **Takeaway:** vitamin D probably isn't a meaningful way to prevent diabetes. His metformin, weight, diet and exercise matter far more.
- Worth knowing: as I recall, a follow-up analysis of D2d suggested people who started with *very low* vitamin D levels might have benefited more. That was a subgroup finding and I haven't verified it here, so ask his doctor rather than relying on it.

**3. VITAL fracture study (2022)**
- Vitamin D didn't reduce fractures in generally healthy people. Your notes include the key caveat: these people were **not selected for deficiency or osteoporosis**.
- **Takeaway:** this says nothing against treating someone who *is* deficient.

**4. His blood test: 25(OH)D = 14 ng/mL**
- **LOW.** Most guidelines treat below 20 ng/mL as deficient or inadequate, and some consider around 30 ng/mL the target. These cutoffs are recalled and vary somewhat by guideline.
- At this level, correcting it is standard care, mainly for bone and muscle health and fall risk at his age. The "recheck in 3 months" note suggests the doctor plans to treat it or retest after treatment.
