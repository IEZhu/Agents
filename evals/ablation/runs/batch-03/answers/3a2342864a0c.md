**Short version:** these papers answer a different question from your dad's. They tested whether giving vitamin D to people who mostly weren't deficient prevents cancer, heart disease, diabetes or fractures. The answer was "not much." Your dad's level is 14 ng/mL, which his doctor has called deficient. For him the question is whether to correct a deficiency, and that is what the doctor is already managing. The trials don't argue against that.

## What each paper actually says

**1. VITAL (2019): cancer and heart disease**
- About 25,900 healthy adults aged 50 and over took 2000 IU/day or placebo for roughly 5 years.
- There was no overall drop in cancer or major heart events.
- A possible reduction in cancer deaths showed up only after leaving out the first 1–2 years. That's a secondary analysis, so treat it as something worth studying further, not as proof.
- **Takeaway:** if you're not deficient, taking vitamin D "just in case" doesn't clearly prevent cancer or heart disease.

**2. D2d (2019): prediabetes to diabetes**
- This is the one most relevant to your dad, because the participants had prediabetes like him.
- 4000 IU/day gave about 12% fewer people progressing to diabetes (HR 0.88), but the result wasn't statistically significant. So it could be a small real effect or it could be chance.
- **Takeaway:** vitamin D probably isn't a meaningful way to prevent diabetes. His metformin, weight, diet and exercise matter far more.
- Worth knowing: as I recall, a follow-up analysis of D2d suggested people who started with *very low* vitamin D levels might have benefited more. That was a subgroup finding and I haven't verified it here, so ask his doctor rather than relying on it.

**3. VITAL fracture study (2022)**
- Vitamin D didn't reduce fractures in generally healthy people. Your notes include the key caveat: these people were **not selected for deficiency or osteoporosis**.
- **Takeaway:** this says nothing against treating someone who *is* deficient.

**4. His blood test: 25(OH)D = 14 ng/mL**
- **LOW.** Most guidelines treat below 20 ng/mL as deficient or inadequate, and some consider around 30 ng/mL the target. These cutoffs are recalled and vary somewhat by guideline.
- At this level, correcting it is standard care, mainly for bone and muscle health and fall risk at his age. The "recheck in 3 months" note suggests the doctor plans to treat it or retest after treatment.

## How to frame it for him tonight

> "Those big studies found vitamin D doesn't do much for people who already have enough. But your level is low, so this isn't about prevention. It's about fixing a shortage, and that's what your doctor is checking."

## Things to ask his doctor
1. **Did you intend for me to start vitamin D now, and at what dose?** I'd let the doctor set the dose rather than guess one. Deficiency is sometimes treated with higher doses than the trials used, and the dose depends on his kidney function and other medications.
2. **Should I take calcium too,** or is my diet enough?
3. **Since I'm on metformin, should my B12 be checked?** This is a separate issue from vitamin D. Long-term metformin is well known to lower B12, and low B12 can cause tiredness, numbness or tingling. It's often overlooked.

## Safety notes
- Don't let him self-start megadoses. Very high long-term intake can raise calcium levels, and signs include nausea, constipation, confusion, excessive thirst and kidney stones. Tell him to call his doctor if he notices any of these, especially if he has a history of kidney stones, kidney disease or high calcium.
- Standard vitamin D supplements have no important interaction with metformin that I know of.
- One limit on all this: I don't know the rest of his health picture, such as his kidney function, other medications, bone density or falls history. Those details affect the plan, so the doctor's judgment should win.
