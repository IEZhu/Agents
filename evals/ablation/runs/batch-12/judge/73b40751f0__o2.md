## Bottom line

This flow has three likely conversion killers. In rough order of impact:

1. **The full cost, delivery fee included, first appears at the payment step.** For furniture, where delivery fees are large, this is probably your biggest leak. It's bigger than forced sign-up.
2. **Forced account creation** with strict password rules, on mobile.
3. **Delivery date is hidden until late.** For furniture, "when does it arrive" is part of deciding to buy, not a detail to sort out afterwards.

Everything else adds friction on top of those three.

## Step-by-step review

### 1. Cart → "Checkout"
- **No cost or delivery preview.** The cart should show an estimated delivery fee and the earliest delivery date once the user enters a ZIP code (one field). Right now the cart hides what the customer most needs to know. *(Nielsen #1, visibility of system status; #2, match with the real world)*
- **Missing payment shortcuts.** Put Apple Pay / Google Pay buttons in the cart. On mobile they skip steps 2, 3 and 5 almost completely.
- **Mobile:** keep the Checkout button sticky at the bottom, at least 44px tall, with the total next to it.

### 2. Mandatory account creation (severity: critical)
- **Forced sign-up before purchase** is one of the most commonly cited reasons for abandoning checkout. In Baymard Institute's surveys it comes up for roughly a quarter of people who abandon (recalled, not verified; check Baymard's current cart-abandonment report before quoting it).
- **Password rules make it worse.** "10+ characters including a symbol", typed on a phone keyboard, then typed again. Current NIST guidance (SP 800-63B) advises against composition rules like "must include a symbol" (recalled; confirm in the source).
- **"Confirm password" is unnecessary.** A show/hide toggle does the same job with half the typing.
- **Fix:** make guest checkout the default. Ask for email only (you need it for the receipt anyway). After the order, offer "Save your details: add a password to create an account." That post-purchase conversion is the standard pattern, and it keeps most of the account sign-ups you'd otherwise lose.

### 3. Shipping form: 14 fields, no autocomplete, ZIP checked only on submit (severity: high)
- **Too many fields.** Most checkouts manage with roughly 7–8 in total (Baymard benchmark, recalled). Look for the usual culprits: separate first and last name, "Address line 2" shown by default, separate phone and email confirmation, company name. Merge fields, cut them, or hide them behind "+ Add apartment, suite, etc."
- **No address autocomplete.** On mobile this matters a lot. Add an address lookup service, and in any case set the correct HTML `autocomplete` attributes (`street-address`, `postal-code`, `address-level2`, `tel`, `email`) so the browser can autofill. The attributes are nearly free to add.
- **ZIP validated only on submit.** Validate inline on blur, fill in city and state from the ZIP, and use `inputmode="numeric"` so a number pad appears. Validating only on submit means users fix errors after the fact on a long form, which is the most frustrating way to fail. *(Nielsen #5, error prevention; #9, error recovery)*
- **Furniture-specific fields that matter:** floor or elevator, access notes, and whether they want the room-of-choice or white-glove service. These fields do affect the delivery, so keep them but make them optional and grouped.

### 4. Delivery date picker appears only after choosing a shipping method (severity: high)
- **Hidden dependency.** Users don't know a date choice is coming, so they can't compare methods on the thing they care about. Someone choosing between "Standard $79" and "Premium $149" wants to know "arrives in 3 weeks" vs "arrives Tuesday".
- **Fix:** show each shipping option with its fee *and* its earliest date together, e.g. "Standard, $79, from Oct 14". Then show the date picker right under the selected option on the same screen. *(Nielsen #6, recognition over recall)*
- **Accessibility:** custom date pickers are a common WCAG failure. They need full keyboard support, properly labelled days, and a text-entry fallback.

### 5. Payment: card only, total shown for the first time (severity: critical)
- **Late cost reveal.** Unexpected extra costs are the #1 reason for abandonment in Baymard's surveys, cited by close to half of people who abandon (recalled, not verified). For a $1,500 sofa with a $150 delivery fee, finding out at the last step feels like a bait-and-switch.
- **Fix:** keep a running order summary (items, delivery, tax estimate, total) visible from the cart onward. On mobile, use a collapsible "Order total: $1,629 ▾" bar at the top of each step.
- **Card only is a big gap for furniture.** Add wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal). Also consider buy-now-pay-later or financing: for high-ticket furniture it's common and expected, though whether it pays off for you depends on your price mix. Test it rather than assume.
- **Card form basics:** `inputmode="numeric"`, the right `autocomplete="cc-*"` attributes, automatic card-type detection, and a "billing same as shipping" option that is checked by default.

### 6. "Place order" → spinner, no email for up to 10 minutes (severity: high, and also an operations problem)
- **Ambiguous state.** A bare spinner followed by 10 minutes of silence leads to "did it go through?" People refresh or tap again, which risks double orders, and support calls go up. *(Nielsen #1)*
- **Fix:**
  - Disable the button once tapped and show "Placing your order…". Make sure the backend can't create the same order twice from repeated taps.
  - Take people straight to a confirmation page with the order number, items, total, delivery date and "confirmation sent to jane@…".
  - Send the transactional email within about a minute. If the 10-minute delay comes from batch processing, send an immediate "Order received" email and follow up with the detailed one later.
  - Announce the result to screen readers with an `aria-live` region, not only a visual spinner.

## The conversion number for your board deck

Here's what I can honestly give you, and why it's more useful than a gut number.

**Why no one can give you one exact figure:** the lift depends on your current checkout completion rate, how many of your customers already have accounts, what share are returning buyers, and whether you ship guest checkout alone or together with the cost-transparency fix. Published cases vary a lot. The best-known one is Jared Spool's "$300 million button" story, where removing forced registration reportedly increased purchases by about 45% (recalled; verify before citing). That was one retailer with one baseline, not a rule.

**A number you can defend.** Here's a transparent back-of-envelope model:
- Suppose roughly 70% of people who start checkout abandon (close to industry averages, recalled).
- Suppose about 25% of those cite forced account creation.
- Assume only about 40% of that group would actually finish if the barrier were gone.

That's roughly 7 extra completions per 100 people who start checkout. On a ~30% baseline completion rate, that works out to a relative lift in the ~20% area. Every input is an assumption, so I'd **put 10% on the slide as a conservative planning target**, with the wording below:

> "Guest checkout: planning assumption **+10% relative checkout conversion** (conservative, based on published abandonment research). To be validated by A/B test, with results by [date]."

That's a single number, it's honest, and if the test comes in higher you look good instead of exposed. Plug in your real checkout-start and completion rates from analytics and I'll rerun the model with your data.

Two things to say to the board:
- **Guest checkout probably isn't the biggest lever.** Showing the delivery cost up front is likely worth as much or more for furniture. Shipping both together will give a larger combined effect, but you won't be able to credit each change separately unless you test them separately.
- **This is a low-risk, reversible change.** Ship it behind a flag and measure. There's no reason to argue about forecasts when you can have real data in a few weeks.

## Testing plan

- **A/B test:** at 40k sessions/month, with mobile checkout starts probably in the low thousands, you'll likely need **2–4 weeks per test** to detect a lift of around 10% (an estimate; run a sample-size calculator with your actual baseline). Test the highest-impact changes first: (1) early cost display plus guest checkout, then (2) wallets and financing.
- **Qualitative:** five moderated mobile sessions with real furniture shoppers, each buying a sofa with delivery on their own phone. Watch where they hesitate over cost and dates. This typically takes a week and will find most of the remaining problems.
- **Instrumentation:** track drop-off at each step, which fields produce errors (especially ZIP and password), and payment-method mix. Without this you can't tell which fix is working.

## Accessibility checklist for this flow

- Error messages appear inline, tied to their fields (`aria-describedby`), and state what to do. Color is not the only signal.
- The date picker works fully by keyboard and screen reader, with a text-entry fallback.
- Touch targets are at least 44px, including radio buttons for shipping options.
- Contrast is at least 4.5:1 for prices and fees. Don't use light gray for fee text.
- Order status is announced through `aria-live`.
- Password fields allow paste and password managers. WCAG 2.2 "Accessible Authentication" (3.3.8) is relevant here (recalled; confirm the criterion number).

## Step-by-step review

### 1. Cart → "Checkout" button
Hard to judge without seeing the page. Things to check:
- **Is the full cost visible here?** Show estimated delivery fee (ZIP-based), taxes and total on the cart page. Furniture delivery fees are large, so hiding them is the worst thing in this flow (see step 5).
- **Is the delivery window visible?** For furniture, "when does it arrive" often decides the purchase. "Arrives in 3–5 weeks" belongs on the cart page, not buried behind a shipping-method choice.
- **Express wallets above the fold**: Apple Pay / Google Pay buttons next to "Checkout" let mobile users skip steps 2, 3 and 5.
- Sticky "Checkout" button on mobile, at least 44px tall, full width.

### 2. Mandatory account creation (critical)
- **Forcing account creation is one of the most documented causes of checkout abandonment.** Baymard Institute's abandonment surveys have repeatedly put "the site wanted me to create an account" among the top reasons, at roughly a quarter of abandoners (recalled, not verified; check Baymard's current figure before citing it).
- Furniture makes this worse: people buy rarely, so few customers want an ongoing relationship at the moment they pay.
- **Password rules make it worse still.** 10+ characters plus a required symbol, typed twice on a phone keyboard, is a big hit to effort and error rate. Current NIST guidance (SP 800-63B) advises against composition rules like "must include a symbol" and prefers length plus breached-password checks (recalled; confirm the current revision).
- The confirm-password field doubles the typing and catches almost nothing. Use one field with a show/hide toggle instead.

**Fix:** Guest checkout as the default. Put email first (you need it for the receipt anyway). Offer "Save your details for next time? Just add a password" **on the confirmation page**, once the purchase is done. This is the standard pattern and it usually recovers a good share of the account sign-ups you'd otherwise lose.

### 3. Shipping address, 14 fields, no autocomplete, ZIP validated on submit
- **14 fields is too many for mobile.** Most checkouts can get the address down to about 6–8 fields: name, address line 1, optional line 2 (collapsed), ZIP, city/state (auto-filled from ZIP), and phone (needed for furniture delivery).
- **No autocomplete** means you're ignoring two free wins:
  - **Browser autofill**: add correct `autocomplete` attributes (`shipping given-name`, `shipping address-line1`, `shipping postal-code`, `tel`, `email`). This is nearly free and helps a lot on mobile.
  - **Address lookup** (Google Places, Loqate, Smarty and similar) also reduces bad addresses. For furniture, a failed delivery costs a truck trip.
- **Validating ZIP only on submit** breaks Nielsen #5 (error prevention) and #9 (error recovery). The user fills in 14 fields, taps submit, and then has to hunt for the error. Validate inline on blur, use `inputmode="numeric"` for ZIP, and fill in city/state from it.
- **Furniture-specific fields** (floor, elevator, stairs, access notes, white-glove) are legitimate, but they shouldn't make the core form longer. Put them in an optional "Delivery details" section, or collect them after the order.

### 4. Delivery date picker only appears after choosing shipping method
- This is hidden dependency. Users can't compare options by the thing they care about (the date), so they have to guess, reveal, back out, and try again. That breaks Nielsen #6 (recognition over recall).
- **Fix:** Show each shipping method with its delivery window or next available date on the option itself:
  - `( ) Standard — $49 — arrives Oct 14–18`
  - `( ) White-glove — $149 — pick a date from Oct 9`

  Then show the date picker straight away for methods that need a specific date.
- **Accessibility:** custom date pickers often fail keyboard and screen-reader use. Make sure dates can be reached with arrow keys, have proper labels ("Thursday, October 9, available"), and don't mark unavailable dates by color alone.

### 5. Payment page, card only, total shown for the first time (critical)
- **A surprise total at the last step is the worst problem in this flow.** "Extra costs too high" (shipping, fees, taxes) has been the top abandonment reason in Baymard's surveys for years, at roughly half of abandoners (recalled, not verified). With a delivery fee that may be $50–$200, users who reach this step after all that effort feel ambushed, and many leave.
- **Fix:** Show the running total, including the delivery estimate, from the cart onward. Update it live when the shipping method changes. On mobile, a collapsible order summary at the top ("Total: $1,289 ▾") works well.
- **Card only** is a real gap for mobile:
  - **Apple Pay / Google Pay**: large mobile conversion gains, because no card typing is needed.
  - **PayPal**: many shoppers use it as their trust fallback with a store they don't know.
  - **Buy now, pay later / financing** (Affirm, Klarna, etc.): fairly standard in furniture given typical order values. Test it; don't assume.
- Card form basics: `autocomplete="cc-number"` etc., numeric keypad, card-type detection, inline Luhn check, and no separate "card type" dropdown.

### 6. "Place order" → spinner, confirmation email up to 10 minutes later
- **A spinner with no status** breaks Nielsen #1. On a flaky mobile connection users will tap again (double orders) or leave and wonder whether they were charged. That leads to support calls and chargebacks.
- **Fix:**
  - Disable the button once tapped and change its label to "Placing your order…". Announce it with `aria-live="polite"`.
  - Make the request idempotent on the server, so a double tap can't create two orders.
  - Go to a **confirmation page** with the order number, items, total, delivery window, and "we've emailed you at x@y.com".
- **A 10-minute email delay** is almost always a backend issue: batch jobs, queues, or sending synchronously through a slow provider. Transactional email should arrive within seconds. On a $1,000+ purchase, ten minutes of silence produces "did my order go through?" tickets and duplicate orders.

## Cross-cutting issues

- **Progress indicator**: show "Shipping → Delivery → Payment" so users know how much is left (Nielsen #1).
- **Labels, not placeholders**: placeholder-only fields disappear as the user types, which hurts recall and accessibility.
- **Error messages**: say what's wrong and how to fix it ("ZIP must be 5 digits"), next to the field, linked with `aria-describedby`, and move focus to the first error.
- **Touch targets** at least 44×44px, and **contrast** at least 4.5:1 for text, especially grey helper text and disabled states.
- **Trust signals** near the payment step: return policy, delivery guarantee, and secure-payment note. These matter for high-value purchases from a store people may not know.
- **Performance**: each step should load fast on 4G. A slow third-party script on the payment page costs conversions just as surely as a bad form.

## Getting a defensible number for Monday

Rather than an invented figure, use one you can defend when a board member asks where it came from.

1. **Pull your funnel from analytics:** of users who reach step 2 (account creation), what percentage continue to step 3? Compare that with the drop-off on your other steps.
2. **Estimate the recoverable share.** Say 40% of people leave at step 2, while a light step in your funnel loses about 10%. The excess (~30 points) is roughly the most guest checkout can recover at that step. Assume you recover part of it, not all of it.
3. **Turn that into orders.** Excess drop-off recovered × users reaching step 2 per month × downstream completion rate = extra orders per month. That gives you one number, built from your own data.
4. **Frame it for the board** as a target to be tested, not a prediction: *"We project +X% checkout conversion from guest checkout, based on our step-2 drop-off; we'll confirm with an A/B test."* A board will accept a stated assumption with a validation plan much more readily than a borrowed number that falls apart under one question.

**On testing, be realistic about your traffic.** At 40k sessions per month and typical furniture conversion rates, an A/B test measured on sessions could take a couple of months to detect a modest lift. Measure against checkout starts rather than all sessions, and expect about 4–8 weeks (a rough estimate that depends on your baseline and on how small a lift you need to detect). Also consider fixing the hidden total at the same time as guest checkout. I suspect that change is worth as much or more, and it's a low-risk change you probably don't need to test before shipping.

**Qualitative check this week:** watch 5 people on their phones try to buy one item. Session recordings (Hotjar, Clarity) of step-2 and step-5 exits will show you the problems above faster than any benchmark.
