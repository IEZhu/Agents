## Latest user message
Doing a Secret Santa for 6 people: Anna, Ben, Carla, Dev, Eli, Fran. Rules: nobody draws themselves, and the two couples (Anna & Ben, Carla & Dev) must not draw their own partner. Eli and Fran aren't a couple, so they can draw each other. How many valid assignments are there in total? I'm writing a little randomizer that just shuffles until it hits a valid one and I want to know what fraction of all 720 shuffles are valid so I can estimate retries. I got two different numbers doing it by hand so I don't trust myself.

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. States that there are 116 valid assignments.
2. Gives the fraction as 116/720 (about 16.1%, i.e. roughly 6.2 shuffles expected per valid result), consistent with its count.
3. Reaches the count by at least two independent approaches (e.g. inclusion-exclusion over forbidden positions and casework/conditioning on who Anna draws, or a rook polynomial) and shows that they agree; merely citing that there are 265 derangements of 6 does not count as a second approach.
4. Clearly lays out the method so the user can see which step their hand counts may have gone wrong at.

## Answer A
**There are 116 valid assignments, so 116/720 = 29/180 ≈ 16.1% of shuffles are valid.** On average your randomizer needs 180/29 ≈ **6.2 shuffles**.

I counted it two different ways and got 116 both times.

## Method 1: Inclusion–exclusion with a forbidden board

Write the forbidden (giver → receiver) pairs as cells in a 6×6 grid:

- Anna and Ben can't draw anyone in {Anna, Ben}. That's a full 2×2 block.
- Carla and Dev can't draw anyone in {Carla, Dev}. That's another full 2×2 block.
- Eli can't draw Eli, and Fran can't draw Fran. Those are two single cells.

These pieces share no rows or columns, so the board's rook polynomial is the product of the pieces' polynomials. $r_k$ is the number of ways to place $k$ forbidden pairs that don't clash:

- 2×2 block: $1 + 4x + 2x^2$
- Single cell: $1 + x$

$$(1+4x+2x^2)^2(1+x)^2 = 1 + 10x + 37x^2 + 64x^3 + 56x^4 + 24x^5 + 4x^6$$

Valid count $= \sum_k (-1)^k r_k (6-k)!$:

| k | $r_k$ | $(6-k)!$ | term |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 720 | +720 |
| 1 | 10 | 120 | −1200 |
| 2 | 37 | 24 | +888 |
| 3 | 64 | 6 | −384 |
| 4 | 56 | 2 | +112 |
| 5 | 24 | 1 | −24 |
| 6 | 4 | 1 | +4 |

Total: **116**

## Method 2 (independent check): start from derangements

Six people can be assigned so that nobody draws themselves in $D_6 = 265$ ways. Next, remove the ones where someone draws their partner. There are four such events: A→B, B→A, C→D, D→C.

- **Single events:** Of the derangements, 53 have A→B, and the same goes for each of the other three. Sum: 4 × 53 = 212
- **Pairs of events:**
  - A→B and B→A: the other 4 people form a derangement, so 9. C→D with D→C also gives 9.
  - One arc from each couple, e.g. A→B and C→D: Ben, Dev, Eli and Fran must go to Anna, Carla, Eli and Fran, with Eli≠Eli and Fran≠Fran. That's 24 − 6 − 6 + 2 = 14 ways, and there are 4 such pairs, so 56.
  - Sum: 9 + 9 + 56 = 74
- **Triples of events,** e.g. both Anna/Ben arcs plus C→D: Dev, Eli and Fran go to Carla, Eli and Fran with no one drawing themselves. That's 6 − 2 − 2 + 1 = 3 ways, and there are 4 such triples, so 12.
- **All four events:** Eli and Fran must swap, so 1.

Bad derangements = 212 − 74 + 12 − 1 = 149, so valid = 265 − 149 = **116** ✓

## For your retry estimate

- P(one shuffle is valid) = $p = 29/180 \approx 0.161$
- The number of shuffles is geometric, with mean $1/p \approx 6.2$
- P(still no valid shuffle after $n$ tries) $= (151/180)^n$:
  - after 10 tries: about 17%
  - after 20 tries: about 3%
  - after 27 tries: under 1%
  - after 50 tries: about 0.015%

Rejection sampling works fine at this size. It also gives you a uniformly random valid assignment, which is a good property to keep. If one of your hand counts was **265**, that's the number of derangements, where only "nobody draws themselves" applies and the couple rule is left out.


## Answer B
**There are 116 valid assignments, so 116/720 = 29/180 ≈ 16.1% of shuffles are valid.** On average your randomizer needs about **6.2 shuffles** to hit one.

I counted it two independent ways and both give 116.

### Method 1: count the bad cases and subtract (inclusion–exclusion)

Start from the shuffles where nobody draws themselves. For 6 people there are D(6) = **265** of these. Then remove the ones where someone draws their partner. There are four "partner" events: A→B, B→A, C→D, D→C.

- **One event fixed, e.g. A→B:** the other 5 people fill the remaining 5 slots, and C, D, E, F can't draw themselves. B can't draw B anyway, because B's slot is already taken. That gives 5! − 4·4! + 6·3! − 4·2! + 1! = 120 − 96 + 36 − 8 + 1 = **53**. With 4 such events: 4 × 53 = 212.
- **Two events fixed:**
  - Both halves of one couple (A→B and B→A): the remaining 4 people must be deranged, D(4) = 9. Two couples give 2 × 9 = 18.
  - One event from each couple (e.g. A→B and C→D): the remaining 4 people are B, D, E, F, drawing A, C, E, F. Only E and F are restricted, so 4! − 2·3! + 2! = 14. There are 4 such pairs: 56.
  - Total for pairs: 74.
- **Three events fixed (e.g. A→B, B→A, C→D):** the remaining people D, E, F draw C, E, F with E and F not drawing themselves. That gives 3! − 2·2! + 1 = 3. There are 4 such triples: 12.
- **All four events fixed:** E and F must swap, which is 1 way.

Bad cases = 212 − 74 + 12 − 1 = **149**, so valid = 265 − 149 = **116**.

### Method 2: rook polynomial (a separate check)

Draw a 6×6 grid of giver × receiver and mark the forbidden cells:
- a full 2×2 block for {Anna, Ben} (AA, AB, BA, BB)
- a full 2×2 block for {Carla, Dev}
- the single cells EE and FF

The rook polynomial of the forbidden cells is:

```
(1 + 4x + 2x²)² · (1 + x)² = 1 + 10x + 37x² + 64x³ + 56x⁴ + 24x⁵ + 4x⁶
```

The number of valid assignments is Σ (−1)ᵏ rₖ (6−k)!:

```
720 − 10·120 + 37·24 − 64·6 + 56·2 − 24·1 + 4·1
= 720 − 1200 + 888 − 384 + 112 − 24 + 4
= 116  ✓
```

As a sanity check, the same method with only the 6 self-cells, (1+x)⁶, gives 265, which is the known derangement count. It also makes sense that the answer is well below 265: the couple rule removes a lot of cases, because it blocks 4 of the 30 non-self edges.

### What this means for your retry loop

Each shuffle is an independent trial that succeeds with probability p = 29/180 ≈ 0.161. The number of shuffles you need follows a geometric distribution.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Expected shuffles | 1/p = 180/29 ≈ **6.2** |
| P(still not done after k shuffles) | (151/180)ᵏ ≈ 0.839ᵏ |
| Done within 18 shuffles | ≥ 95% |
| Done within 27 shuffles | ≥ 99% |
| Done within 60 shuffles | ≈ 99.997% |

Rejection sampling is fine here. It's cheap, and every valid assignment comes up with equal probability (1/116 each), so you don't need anything smarter. If your two hand counts came out as 265 and something else, the 265 was probably plain derangements without the couple rule applied.
