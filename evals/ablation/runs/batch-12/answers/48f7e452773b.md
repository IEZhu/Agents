## Bottom line

This flow has at least three problems that are probably costing you real revenue: forced account creation, a total that stays hidden until the last step, and card as the only payment method. It has several more that add friction, mostly on mobile. The fix is well understood: guest-first, costs shown up front, fewer fields, wallets, and instant confirmation. Guest checkout is one piece of that. On its own it is probably not the biggest one.

## Step-by-step review

### 1. Cart → "Checkout" button
Hard to judge without seeing the page. Things to check:
- **Is the full cost visible here?** Show estimated delivery fee (ZIP-based), taxes and total on the cart page. Furniture delivery fees are large, so hiding them is the worst thing in this flow (see step 5).
- **Is the delivery window visible?** For furniture, "when does it arrive" often decides the purchase. "Arrives in 3–5 weeks" belongs on the cart page, not buried behind a shipping-method choice.
- **Express wallets above the fold**: Apple Pay / Google Pay buttons next to "Checkout" let mobile users skip steps 2, 3 and 5.
- Sticky "Checkout" button on mobile, at least 44px tall, full width.

### 2. Mandatory account creation (critical)
- **Forcing account creation is one of the most documented causes of checkout abandonment.** Baymard Institute's abandonment surveys have repeatedly put "the site wanted me to create an account" among the top reasons, at roughly a quarter of abandoners (recalled, not verified; check Baymard's current figure before citing it).
- Furniture makes this worse: people buy rarely, so few customers want an ongoing relationship at the moment they pay.
- **Password rules make it worse still.** 10+ characters plus a required symbol, typed twice on a phone keyboard, is a big hit to effort and error rate. Current NIST guidance (SP 800-63B) advises against composition rules like "must include a symbol" and prefers length plus breached-password checks (recalled; confirm the current revision).
- The confirm-password field doubles the typing and catches almost nothing. Use one field with a show/hide toggle instead.

**Fix:** Guest checkout as the default. Put email first (you need it for the receipt anyway). Offer "Save your details for next time? Just add a password" **on the confirmation page**, once the purchase is done. This is the standard pattern and it usually recovers a good share of the account sign-ups you'd otherwise lose.

### 3. Shipping address, 14 fields, no autocomplete, ZIP validated on submit
- **14 fields is too many for mobile.** Most checkouts can get the address down to about 6–8 fields: name, address line 1, optional line 2 (collapsed), ZIP, city/state (auto-filled from ZIP), and phone (needed for furniture delivery).
- **No autocomplete** means you're ignoring two free wins:
  - **Browser autofill**: add correct `autocomplete` attributes (`shipping given-name`, `shipping address-line1`, `shipping postal-code`, `tel`, `email`). This is nearly free and helps a lot on mobile.
  - **Address lookup** (Google Places, Loqate, Smarty and similar) also reduces bad addresses. For furniture, a failed delivery costs a truck trip.
- **Validating ZIP only on submit** breaks Nielsen #5 (error prevention) and #9 (error recovery). The user fills in 14 fields, taps submit, and then has to hunt for the error. Validate inline on blur, use `inputmode="numeric"` for ZIP, and fill in city/state from it.
- **Furniture-specific fields** (floor, elevator, stairs, access notes, white-glove) are legitimate, but they shouldn't make the core form longer. Put them in an optional "Delivery details" section, or collect them after the order.

### 4. Delivery date picker only appears after choosing shipping method
- This is hidden dependency. Users can't compare options by the thing they care about (the date), so they have to guess, reveal, back out, and try again. That breaks Nielsen #6 (recognition over recall).
- **Fix:** Show each shipping method with its delivery window or next available date on the option itself:
  - `( ) Standard — $49 — arrives Oct 14–18`
  - `( ) White-glove — $149 — pick a date from Oct 9`

  Then show the date picker straight away for methods that need a specific date.
- **Accessibility:** custom date pickers often fail keyboard and screen-reader use. Make sure dates can be reached with arrow keys, have proper labels ("Thursday, October 9, available"), and don't mark unavailable dates by color alone.

### 5. Payment page, card only, total shown for the first time (critical)
- **A surprise total at the last step is the worst problem in this flow.** "Extra costs too high" (shipping, fees, taxes) has been the top abandonment reason in Baymard's surveys for years, at roughly half of abandoners (recalled, not verified). With a delivery fee that may be $50–$200, users who reach this step after all that effort feel ambushed, and many leave.
- **Fix:** Show the running total, including the delivery estimate, from the cart onward. Update it live when the shipping method changes. On mobile, a collapsible order summary at the top ("Total: $1,289 ▾") works well.
- **Card only** is a real gap for mobile:
  - **Apple Pay / Google Pay**: large mobile conversion gains, because no card typing is needed.
  - **PayPal**: many shoppers use it as their trust fallback with a store they don't know.
  - **Buy now, pay later / financing** (Affirm, Klarna, etc.): fairly standard in furniture given typical order values. Test it; don't assume.
- Card form basics: `autocomplete="cc-number"` etc., numeric keypad, card-type detection, inline Luhn check, and no separate "card type" dropdown.

### 6. "Place order" → spinner, confirmation email up to 10 minutes later
- **A spinner with no status** breaks Nielsen #1. On a flaky mobile connection users will tap again (double orders) or leave and wonder whether they were charged. That leads to support calls and chargebacks.
- **Fix:**
  - Disable the button once tapped and change its label to "Placing your order…". Announce it with `aria-live="polite"`.
  - Make the request idempotent on the server, so a double tap can't create two orders.
  - Go to a **confirmation page** with the order number, items, total, delivery window, and "we've emailed you at x@y.com".
- **A 10-minute email delay** is almost always a backend issue: batch jobs, queues, or sending synchronously through a slow provider. Transactional email should arrive within seconds. On a $1,000+ purchase, ten minutes of silence produces "did my order go through?" tickets and duplicate orders.

## Cross-cutting issues

- **Progress indicator**: show "Shipping → Delivery → Payment" so users know how much is left (Nielsen #1).
- **Labels, not placeholders**: placeholder-only fields disappear as the user types, which hurts recall and accessibility.
- **Error messages**: say what's wrong and how to fix it ("ZIP must be 5 digits"), next to the field, linked with `aria-describedby`, and move focus to the first error.
- **Touch targets** at least 44×44px, and **contrast** at least 4.5:1 for text, especially grey helper text and disabled states.
- **Trust signals** near the payment step: return policy, delivery guarantee, and secure-payment note. These matter for high-value purchases from a store people may not know.
- **Performance**: each step should load fast on 4G. A slow third-party script on the payment page costs conversions just as surely as a bad form.

## Getting a defensible number for Monday

Rather than an invented figure, use one you can defend when a board member asks where it came from.

1. **Pull your funnel from analytics:** of users who reach step 2 (account creation), what percentage continue to step 3? Compare that with the drop-off on your other steps.
2. **Estimate the recoverable share.** Say 40% of people leave at step 2, while a light step in your funnel loses about 10%. The excess (~30 points) is roughly the most guest checkout can recover at that step. Assume you recover part of it, not all of it.
3. **Turn that into orders.** Excess drop-off recovered × users reaching step 2 per month × downstream completion rate = extra orders per month. That gives you one number, built from your own data.
4. **Frame it for the board** as a target to be tested, not a prediction: *"We project +X% checkout conversion from guest checkout, based on our step-2 drop-off; we'll confirm with an A/B test."* A board will accept a stated assumption with a validation plan much more readily than a borrowed number that falls apart under one question.

**On testing, be realistic about your traffic.** At 40k sessions per month and typical furniture conversion rates, an A/B test measured on sessions could take a couple of months to detect a modest lift. Measure against checkout starts rather than all sessions, and expect about 4–8 weeks (a rough estimate that depends on your baseline and on how small a lift you need to detect). Also consider fixing the hidden total at the same time as guest checkout. I suspect that change is worth as much or more, and it's a low-risk change you probably don't need to test before shipping.

**Qualitative check this week:** watch 5 people on their phones try to buy one item. Session recordings (Hotjar, Clarity) of step-2 and step-5 exits will show you the problems above faster than any benchmark.
