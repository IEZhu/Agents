I can't honestly write that icon-only tabs are the right call, because the usability evidence points the other way. If a design review doc says otherwise, the claim won't hold up when someone checks it.

**Why labels matter here**

- **Most icons aren't universally understood.** Nielsen Norman Group's research on icon usability (recalled, not verified; confirm in the source) found that only a few icons, such as home, search and print, are recognized reliably without a label. "Plans" vs. "Workouts" and "Progress" vs. "Community" have no standard icons, so users would have to guess or memorize them. That breaks the "recognition over recall" heuristic.
- **Six tabs is already the bigger problem.** As I recall, both Apple's HIG and Material Design recommend 3–5 bottom tabs (confirm in the current guidelines). With six icon-only tabs, users get more choices and less information about each one.
- **Accessibility gets harder.** Screen readers will still work if every tab has an `accessibilityLabel`. But sighted users with cognitive or low-vision needs lose the visible text. The tap targets also look more ambiguous, even when they meet the 44pt minimum.
- **"Premium" apps mostly keep their labels.** Instagram is the best-known exception. It gets away with icon-only tabs because of huge daily use and very standard icons. A fitness app that people open a few times a week doesn't have that advantage.

**Paste-ready rationale (honest version)**

> We explored an icon-only tab bar to achieve a cleaner, more premium look, but decided to keep text labels because research consistently shows that most icons, apart from a few universal ones like Home, are ambiguous without a label, and our Plans/Workouts and Progress/Community tabs have no standard icons users would recognize. To get the cleaner feel, we will instead reduce the bar from six tabs to five by folding Profile into a header avatar (or Community into Home), in line with platform guidance of three to five tabs. We will also refine the visual treatment with a lighter label weight, tighter icon-label spacing and a stronger active state, so the bar feels premium without losing clarity. Labels also keep the bar accessible to sighted users who rely on text, not only screen-reader users. We'll check this with a quick first-click test on 5–8 users, comparing the labeled and unlabeled versions, before we finalize.

**If leadership still wants icon-only**, make it a test rather than a settled decision. Run a first-click test with both versions (5–8 users each, tasks like "find your saved training plan"). Afterward, track tab-level analytics such as mis-taps and time to reach a screen. If icon-only performs as well as the labeled version, you'll have evidence to support it, which is a much stronger position in a design review than an aesthetic preference.
