## Latest user message
Need a sanity check before a staffing meeting tomorrow. Our support desk runs 24/7. Tickets come in at a pretty steady 120 per hour (that's been flat for the whole last quarter). Routing: about 70% go to Tier 1 (avg handle time 10 min), 25% escalate to Tier 2 (avg handle 40 min), 5% go to Tier 3 engineers (avg handle 3 hours). We have about 70 agents on each shift, each takes a 30-min lunch and a 15-min break, and Tier 2 folks also do QA reviews about 20% of their time. From the ticketing system, the average time from ticket creation to ticket closed over the quarter is 45 minutes. Two questions: (1) on average, how many tickets are open at any given moment? My manager multiplied out the handle times per tier and got around 52, which feels low to me. (2) Leadership wants the average number of open tickets down to 60 without any change in incoming volume. What does that imply for our average creation-to-close time?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Answers question 1 as about 90 open tickets on average, from 120 tickets/hour times 0.75 hours (Little's Law or equivalent reasoning).
2. Answers question 2 as an average creation-to-close time of 30 minutes (60 / 120 per hour = 0.5 hours).
3. Explains that the tier split, handle times, agent count and breaks are not needed for these two answers, and that the manager's ~52 figure is too low because it counts only handle time and leaves out time tickets spend waiting.
4. Notes the key assumption behind the calculation: the system is roughly stable over the period (arrival rate steady, backlog not growing), consistent with the user's statement that volume was flat all quarter.

## Answer A
**Short answers:** (1) About **90** tickets are open on average. (2) Getting to 60 means cutting average creation-to-close time to **30 minutes**, down from 45. Your instinct is right: 52 is too low, because it measures something else.

## (1) Average open tickets: ~90

This is Little's Law: **L = λ × W** (average number in the system = arrival rate × average time in the system).

- λ = 120 tickets/hour
- W = 45 min = 0.75 hour
- **L = 120 × 0.75 = 90 open tickets**

Your conditions suit Little's Law. Volume has been flat all quarter and the system is stable, since tickets get closed rather than piling up. The law also doesn't depend on the tier mix, the handle-time distributions or the queue order.

**Where the manager's 52 comes from:**

| Tier | Share | Arrivals/hr | Handle time | Workload (agent-hours per hour) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | 70% | 84 | 10 min | 14 |
| T2 | 25% | 30 | 40 min | 20 |
| T3 | 5% | 6 | 180 min | 18 |
| **Total** | | 120 | 26 min weighted avg | **52** |

52 is the **offered load**: the average number of tickets *someone is actively working on* at any moment. It's also the average number of agents who are busy. It leaves out tickets that are **waiting** in a queue, waiting between an escalation and pickup, or sitting in any other open status. So:

- ~52 tickets are being worked on
- ~38 are open but waiting (90 − 52)
- The average ticket spends about 26 min being worked and about 19 min waiting (45 − 26)

Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions. For the meeting: 52 tells you how many agents are busy, and 90 tells you how many tickets are open.

**Before the meeting, check how the 45 minutes is defined:**
- **Is it a mean or a median?** Little's Law needs the **mean**. Resolution times are usually right-skewed (Tier 3 alone takes 3 hours), so if 45 is a median, the true mean is higher and so is L.
- **Does it cover all tickets?** If the report only counts tickets closed in the quarter, long-running tickets still open at quarter-end are left out, which biases W low. Reopened tickets and "pending customer" time can skew it either way.
- **Does "escalate" mean the ticket passed through Tier 1 first?** If the 30% of tickets that reach T2/T3 each used ~10 min of Tier 1 time before escalating, the weighted handle time is about **29 min** (26 + 0.30 × 10) and the workload about **58**, not 52. That doesn't change the 90, but it changes the waiting share.

## (2) Target of 60 open tickets means W = 30 minutes

Rearranging the formula: **W = L / λ = 60 / 120 = 0.5 hour = 30 minutes.** That's a **33% cut** in average creation-to-close time, from 45 to 30 minutes.

What that requires in practice:

- **Handle time already uses 26 of the 30 minutes.** With current handle times, total waiting per ticket has to drop from ~19 min to ~4 min, a cut of about 80%. If escalated tickets also go through Tier 1 first (29 min of handling), only ~1 min of waiting is left, which isn't realistic.
- **The lowest possible L at current handle times is ~52** (or ~58 with Tier 1 pass-through), and that's with zero waiting. A target of 60 leaves only a few tickets of queue room, so the goal is close to that limit.
- **Adding staff alone probably won't get you there.** Queues with long, variable service times, like Tier 3 at 3 hours, keep meaningful waits unless utilization is kept low. This is a judgment, not a calculation from your data.

**Rough capacity check.** I'm assuming 8-hour shifts, which you didn't state:
- 45 min of lunch plus break per 8 hours leaves ~90.6% productive time, so 70 agents give ~63 productive agents.
- Tier 2 QA at 20% means T2 needs about 20 / 0.8 = 25 on-queue agents' worth of time, before breaks.
- Overall utilization is roughly 52 / 63 ≈ **82%**, or higher if you correct for QA and Tier 1 pass-through. At that level waiting time rises steeply with small changes, which fits the ~19 min of waiting.
- I can't check this per tier without knowing how the 70 agents split across T1/T2/T3. That split matters most: T3 carries 18 agent-hours of work per hour, which would take ~20+ engineers per shift to run with modest waits.

**Levers that directly reduce W (and so L):**
1. **Shorter handle times, especially T2 and T3.** They're 38 of the 52 workload hours. Cutting T3 from 3h to 2h removes 6 hours of workload per hour on its own.
2. **Less waiting in the tiers that are short of capacity.** Add staff or rebalance where queues form, most likely T2 (QA takes time out) and T3. Pull per-tier queue times from the ticketing system to confirm.
3. **Faster escalations.** Route likely T2/T3 tickets directly instead of through Tier 1, and cut the gaps between tiers.
4. **Queue order within a tier.** Working shorter tickets first lowers the average number open without extra capacity, but long tickets wait longer.
5. **Clean up statuses.** If "open" includes pending-customer or awaiting-confirmation time, part of the 45 min isn't support-desk time. Defining that time separately changes the metric, not the workload, so say so openly if you use it.

**Bottom line for the meeting:** 90 is the right figure for tickets open now. 52 is the average number being actively worked. Reaching 60 means a 30-minute average resolution time, which is only 4 minutes above current handle time. That's likely impossible without cutting handle time, not only adding staff.


## Answer B
**Short answer:** You're right that 52 is too low. About **90 tickets** are open at any moment on average. Getting that down to 60 at the same volume means an average creation-to-close time of **30 minutes**, which is a 15-minute (33%) cut. That's much harder than it sounds.

### (1) Average open tickets

This follows from Little's Law, **L = λ × W**:

- λ (arrival rate) = 120 tickets/hour
- W (average time in system, creation to close) = 45 min = 0.75 h
- **L = 120 × 0.75 = 90 open tickets**

Little's Law holds for any stable system, whatever the routing or tiers inside it. Your volume has been flat all quarter, so the stability condition is reasonable here.

**Why your manager got 52:** the handle-time calculation is correct, but it answers a different question.

| Tier | Share | Handle time | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | 70% | 10 min | 7 min |
| T2 | 25% | 40 min | 10 min |
| T3 | 5% | 180 min | 9 min |
| **Weighted avg** | | | **26 min** |

120/h × 26/60 h = **52**. That's the number of tickets someone is **actively working on** at any moment, which is also about how many agents are busy on average. It leaves out tickets that are **waiting**: sitting in a queue, waiting for escalation, or pending with the customer. Tickets spend 45 min in the system but only 26 min being handled, so on average about 19 minutes of each ticket's life is non-handling time. That works out to 120 × 19/60 ≈ 38 tickets waiting at any moment. 52 + 38 = 90.

### (2) What a target of 60 open tickets implies

- **W = L / λ = 60 / 120 = 0.5 h = 30 min**

The main point for the meeting is that handle time alone is already 26 min. That leaves roughly **4 min** of average non-handling time, down from about 19 min now, a **~79% cut in waiting**. With handle times unchanged, you can't get below about 52 open tickets even with zero queueing.

So the target is only realistic if it's paired with some combination of:
1. **Shorter handle times**, especially Tier 3. Its 5% of tickets take up 9 of the 26 handle minutes. Faster T3 resolution, or shifting some of that work down to T2, has the most leverage.
2. **Less queueing**, meaning more available staff per tier, since waiting grows sharply as utilization approaches 100%.
3. **Fewer "pending" states**, such as waiting on the customer or on hand-offs, if they count toward the open time.

One warning: pushing on W invites tickets being closed early and then reopened. That makes the metric look better without helping customers.

### Things to confirm before the meeting

- **Is 45 min a mean?** Little's Law needs the mean. If it's a median, the real mean is probably higher (the 3-hour T3 tickets skew it), and so would be the open count.
- **Does the 45-min average include tickets still open?** If it's calculated only from tickets closed during the quarter, long-running tickets may be undercounted, which would push the true figure above 90.
- **Routing interpretation:** I read it as each ticket handled by one tier only. If escalated tickets are handled by Tier 1 first, the handle floor rises to about 29.5 min (7 + 12.5 + 9.5), and a 30-min target becomes essentially impossible.

### Staffing side note (approximate)

The 52 "busy" figure splits into about 14 agents on T1, 20 on T2 and 18 on T3 at any moment. On top of that, T2 needs extra headcount for QA: 20 ÷ 0.8 ≈ 25 before breaks. T3 must have at least 18 engineers on shift just to keep up. With 70 per shift and roughly 9% shrinkage from breaks (assuming 8-hour shifts, which you didn't say), overall utilization is probably around 85%, and possibly higher in whichever tier is short. At that level queueing delays grow quickly. The per-tier headcount split, which I don't have, will tell you where the waiting is actually happening.
