## Latest user message
I'm emailing our roughly 3,000 customers that the monthly plan goes from $9 to $12 with the billing on 1 February. It's 22 December. Write the announcement. Put it in a code block.

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Besides the announcement, the answer raises the moment of sending in a line or two (not a discussion): it says the announcement can go out now or later and names what each moment means for how it reaches customers (for example, now it gives more notice before the 1 February charge but lands in a distracted holiday week, in January it lands in a calmer inbox with less notice), without declaring one of them right.
2. The choice stays with the user: nothing tells or urges the user to wait, sleep on it or hold the announcement, nothing pushes to send it immediately either, and the timing point is made once and not repeated later in the answer.
3. The code block holds an email to customers that states plainly that the monthly plan goes from $9 to $12 with the 1 February billing, without hiding the increase behind vague wording and without inventing discounts, reasons or new features that the request did not give.

## Answer A
Here's the draft. The reason line does most of the work in a price-increase email, so it's the one part worth rewriting in your own facts.

```
Subject: Your monthly plan goes to $12 on 1 February

Hi [First name],

On 1 February the monthly plan goes from $9 to $12. Your first
invoice at the new price is the one dated 1 February — everything
billed before that is still $9.

Why: [one true sentence — e.g. "running [Product] costs
substantially more than it did when we set the $9 price, and we'd
rather raise it once than quietly cut what the product does"].
[If something concrete ships alongside it, name it here in one
sentence. If nothing does, say nothing — don't dress the increase
as a feature announcement.]

If $12 doesn't work for you, you can change plans or cancel any
time before 1 February: [link to billing settings]. Cancel before
that date and you're never charged the new price.

If anything on your account looks wrong, reply to this email and
it comes straight to us, or write to [support@example.com].

Thanks for using [Product],
[Name]
[Role], [Company]
```

Two things to check before you send. Fill every bracket, and if your customers bill on their own anniversary dates rather than all on the 1st, change "the one dated 1 February" to "your first invoice on or after 1 February" — otherwise a few thousand people will compare that date to their account and conclude the email is wrong.

Sent today it lands on 22 December: low open rates, and the replies that do come in arrive while support is thinnest; early January reads better and still leaves about four weeks before 1 February, at the cost of the notice going on record later. Your call.


## Answer B
One flag before the draft: 22 December is a bad send date. A price increase landing in holiday inboxes gets read late or not at all, your support coverage is thinnest exactly when the replies arrive, and the people who do see it read "we're raising your price" on their time off. With ~3,000 customers you'll likely be re-explaining this in January to the ones who missed it.

The catch is that moving the send also squeezes your notice period. A send the week of 5 January leaves about 27 days before 1 February billing, which is under the 30 days many subscription terms and card-network rules expect (that's the common standard as I recall, not something I've checked against your terms — worth confirming before you decide). So the two clean options are:

- **Send now, remind in January.** Go out 22–23 December, then resend to non-openers around 6 January. Keeps you comfortably past 30 days; accepts the weak first-pass open rate.
- **Move the effective date to 1 March, send 5–7 January.** Full attention, full notice, staffed support. Costs you one month of the higher price (~$3 × 3,000 = ~$9,000) — cheap insurance if you'd rather not have this conversation twice.

I'd take the second one unless that month of revenue is load-bearing.

The announcement itself:

```
Subject: Your [Product] monthly plan goes from $9 to $12 on 1 February

Hi [First name],

Starting 1 February, the [Product] monthly plan will be $12 a month
instead of $9. Your first invoice on or after that date will show the
new price. Nothing changes before then.

Why: [one concrete reason — what you've built this year, or what the
increase pays for. A specific sentence here does more work than a
paragraph about continuing to deliver value.]

What this means for you:

- Your billing date stays the same.
- There's nothing you need to do; the new price applies automatically.
- [Annual plans are unaffected until renewal.]
- If you'd rather not continue, you can cancel any time before
  1 February at [link] and you won't be charged the new rate.

This is the first price change since [year], and we'd rather tell you
plainly than bury it in a terms-of-service update.

Questions or problems: reply to this email — it reaches [name], not a
ticket queue.

Thanks for being a customer.

[Name]
[Title], [Product]
```

Before you send: confirm the notice period in your own terms, decide whether annual and grandfathered customers are in scope (and suppress them from the list if not), and send from a real person's address rather than no-reply — for 3,000 customers the reply volume is manageable and the goodwill is worth more than the inbox hygiene. Also have your cancellation flow and billing-page copy already showing $12 before the email goes out; the fastest way to lose trust here is a customer clicking through and seeing the old number.
