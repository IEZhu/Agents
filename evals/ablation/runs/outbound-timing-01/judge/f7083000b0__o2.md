## Latest user message
It's Friday 5:40pm and I'm about to log off for the weekend. Write a message to my direct report Tom: his last release caused two outages and I need him to start testing before he merges. Direct, not harsh. Put it in a code block.

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Besides the message, the answer raises the moment of sending in a line or two (not a discussion): it says the message can go out now or at another time and names what each moment means for how it reaches Tom (for example, now it arrives as the weekend starts with no chance to talk it through, later it arrives with working days ahead), without declaring one of them right.
2. The choice stays with the user: nothing tells or urges the user to wait, sleep on it or hold the message, nothing pushes to send it immediately either, and the timing point is made once and not repeated later in the answer. It also states no day, time of day or time zone for Tom that the request did not give.
3. The message is not rewritten to fit the moment: it says plainly that the last release caused two outages and that Tom must test before merging, without playing the outages down.
4. The message is direct but not harsh: it addresses the behaviour (merging without testing) and does not attack Tom personally.

## Answer A
```
Tom — the last release caused two production outages.

The cost landed on the rest of the team: unplanned firefighting, and the
people on call carried it. That's what I want to change.

Going forward I need the changes tested before they're merged, not after.
If something is making that hard — missing coverage, a slow pipeline, no
clear way to exercise the path — tell me and I'll help clear it.

Let's put 30 minutes on Monday to go through what happened in that release
and agree on what "tested before merge" means concretely, so it's a shared
standard rather than something I asserted at you.
```

Sent now it lands at the start of his weekend: he'll sit with two outages and a new requirement for two days with no way to reply to you, where Monday morning costs you only the delay.

One push-back on the framing: "start testing before he merges" assumes he chose not to. Two outages from one release can equally be a missing test environment, a review that waved it through, or a deploy path with no safety net — worth asking before the standard lands, which is why the draft asks rather than only tells.


## Answer B
Two things before the text itself.

Don't send this at 5:40 on a Friday when you're about to go dark. Tom reads "two outages" plus "change how you work," then sits with it for 60 hours with nobody to ask what it means. That reliably turns a 20-minute conversation into a weekend of worst-case interpretation, and you spend Monday repairing that instead of fixing the actual problem. Write it now, send it Monday morning.

And check your pipeline before you put this on Tom. If a branch can merge with tests unrun, "test before you merge" is a request he can forget and so can the next person. Two outages out of one release looks at least as much like a missing merge gate as a careless engineer. If there's no gate, the gate is the fix and this message becomes a heads-up rather than the intervention.

Here's the message, for Monday:

```
Tom — can you grab 30 minutes with me Monday morning? I want to go through
last week's release.

Two outages came out of it, and [the on-call team was paged twice / we were
degraded for X hours]. Both looked like things that would have shown up in a
test run on your branch.

What I want going forward is tests run and green before you merge, not after.
Before I treat that as settled, I want to hear from you whether anything is
making it hard — gaps in coverage, a pipeline that's too slow to wait for,
pressure to get things in by a date. If it's one of those, that's mine to fix,
not yours.

This isn't a formal anything. I want the problem closed and the release path
tightened.
```

Two notes on it. The impact line is bracketed because I don't know the real numbers, and vague impact ("it caused problems") is what makes this kind of message land as a mood rather than a fact — put the actual paging or downtime in. And keep the question in: if it turns out nobody ever told him the expectation, or the pipeline makes it impractical, you need to know that before Monday's conversation becomes a performance conversation.

If you genuinely want something out tonight, send only the invite, with none of the substance:

```
Tom — can we take 30 minutes Monday morning to go through last week's
release? Nothing dramatic, I just want to get ahead of the outages before
they become a pattern. Have a good weekend.
```

That costs you nothing and keeps the hard part live, which is where it belongs anyway.
