## Latest user message
Settling a question for a board game I'm designing. A player rolls a fair six-sided die repeatedly and stops as soon as they roll a 6. The turn only counts as a "clean run" if every roll in the turn, including the final 6, was even (so any 1, 3 or 5 along the way spoils it).

I want the average turn length among clean runs only. My co-designer says it's obviously 3: if only 2, 4, 6 are allowed, it's like rolling a 3-sided die until you hit the 6, so the expected length is 3. That seems right to me but the playtest numbers look lower. Is 3 correct? If not, what's the exact value and why is the 3-sided-die argument wrong?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Gives the exact expected clean-run length as 3/2 (1.5), not 3.
2. Explains why the 3-sided-die argument fails: conditioning on no odd rolls is not the same as removing odd faces from the die, because longer turns are much more likely to include an odd roll, so the condition favours short turns.
3. Supports the value with an explicit calculation, e.g. P(clean run of length n) = (1/3)^(n-1) * (1/6), so the length given a clean run is geometric with success probability 2/3 and mean 3/2.
4. Cross-checks the 3/2 result by a second independent route or sanity check (e.g. P(clean run) = 1/4 overall, or the conditional length probabilities 2/3, 2/9, ... summing to 1), and notes that it is consistent with the lower playtest numbers.

## Answer A
No, 3 is wrong. The average length of a clean run is exactly **3/2 = 1.5 rolls**. Your playtest numbers are right to come in lower.

## The exact calculation

Let $N$ be the number of rolls in a turn. A turn is clean with length $n$ exactly when the first $n-1$ rolls are each a 2 or a 4, and roll $n$ is a 6:

$$P(N = n,\ \text{clean}) = \left(\tfrac{2}{6}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} = \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\tfrac{1}{6}$$

**Probability that a turn is clean** (a geometric series with ratio 1/3):

$$P(\text{clean}) = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\tfrac13\right)^{n-1} = \frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\tfrac13} = \frac{1}{4}$$

**Conditional distribution of the length:**

$$P(N=n \mid \text{clean}) = \frac{(1/3)^{n-1}/6}{1/4} = \left(\tfrac13\right)^{n-1}\cdot\tfrac23$$

This is a geometric distribution with success probability $p = 2/3$. Its mean is $1/p$:

$$E[N \mid \text{clean}] = \frac{1}{2/3} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Among clean runs, 2/3 are a single roll (an immediate 6), 2/9 have length 2, 2/27 have length 3, and so on.

## Why the 3-sided-die argument fails

The argument swaps **conditioning** for **a different experiment**. "Roll a die with faces {2, 4, 6} until you get a 6" describes a process where odd numbers never come up. In your game they do come up, and each one throws the turn away. Conditioning on "no odd number appeared" does not remove odd faces from the die. It keeps only the turns that happened to avoid them.

That filter is biased toward short turns. Each extra roll before the 6 is another chance, at 1/2 per roll, for an odd number to spoil the turn. A turn that would have lasted 5 rolls survives the filter much less often than a turn that lasted 1 roll. So the turns that survive are mostly short ones.

Another way to see it: on each roll, the turn is "decided" as soon as you roll any of {1, 3, 5, 6}. That happens with probability 4/6 = 2/3 per roll. The waiting time until then is geometric with mean 3/2. Given that the turn has been decided, the chance that the deciding roll was a 6 is 1/4, and that chance is the same whichever roll it happens on. So whether the turn is clean is **independent** of when it ends. Conditioning on clean therefore leaves the length distribution unchanged: still geometric with mean 3/2.

## Checks

- **Alternative method (direct sum):** $E[N\cdot\mathbf 1_{\text{clean}}] = \tfrac16\sum_{n\ge1} n\left(\tfrac13\right)^{n-1} = \tfrac16\cdot\frac{1}{(1-1/3)^2} = \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac94 = \tfrac38$. Dividing by $P(\text{clean}) = 1/4$ gives $3/2$. ✓
- **Sanity check:** At least 2/3 of clean runs have length 1, so the mean has to be well below 3. ✓
- **Playtest tie-in:** About 1 turn in 4 should be clean, and the average length of *all* turns, clean or not, is 6. If your logs show clean runs averaging about 1.5 rolls, that matches the theory.

If you actually want clean runs to average 3 rolls, you need a different mechanic. For example, re-roll odd results instead of letting them spoil the turn. That makes the game the 3-sided die your co-designer described.


## Answer B
No, 3 is wrong. Among clean runs the average turn length is exactly **3/2 = 1.5 rolls**, and your playtest numbers are pointing the right way.

## The calculation

Let $N$ be the number of rolls in a turn. A clean run of length $n$ means $n-1$ rolls that each came up 2 or 4, followed by a 6:

$$P(N=n,\ \text{clean}) = \left(\tfrac{2}{6}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{1}{6}\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{n-1}$$

**Probability that a turn is clean:**

$$P(\text{clean}) = \sum_{n\ge1} \tfrac{1}{6}\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{n-1} = \frac{1/6}{1-1/3} = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

**Conditional distribution:**

$$P(N=n \mid \text{clean}) = \frac{\tfrac16(\tfrac13)^{n-1}}{\tfrac14} = \tfrac{2}{3}\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{n-1}$$

This is a geometric distribution with success probability $p = 2/3$, so

$$E[N \mid \text{clean}] = \frac{1}{p} = \frac{3}{2}$$

**Direct check:** $\sum_{n} n\cdot\tfrac16(\tfrac13)^{n-1} = \tfrac16\cdot\frac{1}{(1-1/3)^2} = \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac94 = \tfrac38$. Then $\tfrac{3/8}{1/4} = \tfrac32$. ✓

The first few values: $P(N=1\mid\text{clean}) = 2/3$, $P(N=2\mid\text{clean}) = 2/9$, $P(N=3\mid\text{clean}) = 2/27$. Two thirds of all clean runs are a 6 on the very first roll.

## Why the 3-sided-die argument fails

Your co-designer's model throws away the odd rolls and keeps going, as if the die were re-rolled whenever it showed 1, 3 or 5. Your rule does something else: an odd roll doesn't disappear, it **spoils the turn**. Conditioning on "no odd roll happened" is not the same as "odd faces don't exist."

The difference is in how each length gets weighted:

- **3-sided-die model:** each extra non-6 roll costs a factor of $2/3$ (the chance of a 2 or 4 out of {2, 4, 6}). Weights go like $(2/3)^{n-1}$, which gives a mean of 3.
- **Actual rule:** each extra non-6 roll costs a factor of $1/3$ (the chance of a 2 or 4 out of all six faces). Every extra roll is one more chance for an odd number to ruin the turn. Weights go like $(1/3)^{n-1}$, which gives a mean of 1.5.

Long turns are much less likely to survive, so the clean runs that do survive are mostly short. That's selection bias: filtering on "clean" filters out long turns.

## An intuitive way to see 1.5

Look at each roll only for whether it ends the "interesting" part of the turn: a 1, 3, 5 or 6 (4 of the 6 faces). The number of rolls until one of those appears is geometric with $p = 4/6 = 2/3$, so its mean is $3/2$. When that roll comes up, it's equally likely to be any of the four faces, **whatever the roll number is**. The turn is clean exactly when that face is the 6 (probability $1/4$, which matches the result above). The stopping face doesn't depend on when the stop happened, so conditioning on "it was the 6" doesn't change the length distribution. The mean stays $3/2$.

## Summary

- Fraction of turns that are clean: **1/4**
- Average length of a clean run: **3/2 rolls** (not 3)
- Distribution of clean-run length: geometric, $P(n) = \tfrac23(\tfrac13)^{n-1}$

If your playtest averages sit somewhere around 1.5, the game is behaving correctly. With a small number of clean runs you'll see some noise, but the average shouldn't come anywhere near 3.
