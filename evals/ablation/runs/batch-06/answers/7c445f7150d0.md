Sending him to his room last night instead of having it out at midnight was a good call. Neither of you was ready to talk then.

Before the words themselves, one thing to change. If your goal for the opening is to make him feel how selfish he was, you'll probably get the headphones again. At 14, when a kid hears "you are selfish and irresponsible," it lands as an attack on who he is, and he'll defend himself or shut down. What he can actually take in is what his choices cost you. So open with the fear, not the verdict. You'll still get to responsibility, just in a way he can hear.

**Choose the setting.** Talk side by side if you can: in the car, walking the dog, while making food. Teens tend to talk more easily when they aren't being stared at. Keep your opening short. The longer you talk, the sooner he checks out.

### Opening (say roughly this, then stop)

> "I want to talk about Saturday. I'm not going to lecture you. I'm going to tell you what it was like on my end, and then I want to hear what happened on yours.
>
> We agreed on ten. By eleven your phone was off and I'd called six times. By half eleven I was about to start ringing other parents, because I honestly didn't know if you were okay. That's what those hours were for me: scared, not just annoyed.
>
> So what happened?"

**Then stop talking.** Let the silence sit even if it's uncomfortable. This is where he decides whether it's safe to be honest.

### While he answers

- **If he gives a reason** (phone died, lost track of time): don't argue with it yet. Say "Okay. So the phone died. What could you have done once you realised?" That lets him work out the answer himself, like borrowing a friend's phone or asking the friend's parent to text you, instead of you telling him.
- **If he shrugs or says "I dunno":** "I'm not trying to trap you. I actually want to know, because I need to be able to trust the next time you go out."
- **If he gets defensive** ("you're overreacting," "everyone else stays out later"): "Maybe other people's parents are fine with it. I'm not. I don't need you to agree it was a big deal. I need you to get that it was a big deal *to me*, and that we had a deal."
- **If he reaches for the headphones:** "I'm not going to go on for ages. Two more minutes and we're done." Then actually keep to that.

### Closing: agree on a plan together

> "Here's what I need going forward. If you're going to be late, or your phone's dying, I hear from you before the time we agreed, not after. A late text I can deal with. Silence I can't. What would make that easy for you to actually do?"

If you want a consequence, tie it to trust and put a time limit on it, rather than making it feel like revenge. For example: "Next weekend you're home by nine, and if that goes fine we're back to ten." That ties the rule to earning trust back, and at his age that tends to work better than a punishment that just builds resentment.

### What to avoid

- The words "selfish" and "irresponsible." Describe what he did (phone off, 100 minutes late, no contact), not what kind of person he is.
- Opening with "Do you have any idea how...?" He'll hear it as a lecture starting.
- Going back over every detail of the night once he's acknowledged it. Once he's taken it in, stop there. Winning the argument matters less than him coming back to you next time.

If he's honest with you at all, even just "yeah, I turned it off because I didn't want you calling," thank him for telling you the truth before you deal with what he said. That makes it more likely he'll tell you the truth next time.
