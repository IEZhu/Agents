You were trying to lift her up, and that's a caring instinct. It made her more upset because "you're super smart!" argues with how she feels. From her side, it can sound like "your feelings are wrong." It also keeps the conversation on whether she's smart or stupid, and that's the question that is scaring her.

**Why this is happening at 10**

This reaction is very common at her age. Children around 10 are building their sense of being competent: "Am I good at things?" (Erikson calls this stage *industry vs. inferiority*). They also tend to think in all-or-nothing terms, so one bad result can quickly become "I'm bad at everything." For a child who usually gets 85-90%, a 72% can feel like her identity has been shaken, not like one bad day. Refusing homework is most likely self-protection: *if I don't try, I can't fail again.*

**What to say, in this order**

**1. Name the feeling first. Don't fix it yet.**
> "You were really disappointed about that test. When you're used to doing well, a lower grade can feel awful."

Then stop and let her respond. If she cries, stay close and don't hurry her. She needs to feel understood before she can think clearly.

**2. Gently separate the feeling from the conclusion.**
Don't tell her the thought is wrong. Help her notice that it's a thought:
> "It sounds like your brain is telling you 'I'm stupid and I'll never be good at math.' That's what brains say when we feel really bad. It doesn't mean it's true."

You can make it a small detective game: "Let's check the evidence. What were your last few math grades?" Let her say "85, 90..." herself. Kids believe evidence they find themselves more than evidence we hand them.

**3. Move from "smart" to "what happened."**
> "One test tells us something went differently this time. Want to look at it together and figure out what?"

Go through the test with her when she's calmer. Maybe that evening, maybe tomorrow. Look for patterns: a new topic, careless mistakes, running out of time, a bad night's sleep. Now the grade is information she can use, not a judgment about who she is.

**4. Praise effort and strategy, not ability.**
Instead of "you're so smart," try:
> "I noticed you kept working on that problem even when it was hard."
> "You figured out a different way to do it. That's how people get better at math."

Research on "growth mindset" (Carol Dweck's work is the best known) suggests that praising process tends to help kids cope with setbacks better than praising intelligence. The size of the effect is debated in later studies, so treat it as a useful habit, not a magic phrase.

**5. Share one of your own setbacks (briefly).**
> "When I was in school, I once got a bad grade on ___ and felt like giving up. Here's what I did next..."

This shows her that struggling is normal and that people recover from it.

**About the homework tonight**

Don't turn it into a power struggle, and don't let it disappear either. Make the first step very small:
> "You don't have to do all of it right now. Let's just do the first two problems together, and then decide."

Once she gets started, the "there's no point" feeling usually eases. Sit with her at first, then gradually step back.

**When to look more closely**

A strong reaction to one test is normal and usually fades within days. Talk to her teacher or the school counselor if "I'm bad at everything" spreads beyond math, lasts more than a couple of weeks, or comes with changes in sleep, appetite, friendships, or things she usually enjoys. If it keeps happening, a child psychologist can help. The teacher may also know whether the class started a harder unit, which would explain a lot.

**One thing to try today**

Tonight, open with only step 1, "You were really disappointed about that test," and see what she tells you. Leave the reassurance, the problem-solving and the homework until after she feels heard.
