You were scared for almost two hours. Sending him to his room instead of saying something you'd regret was a good call.

Before the script, one honest point. If you open with "selfish and irresponsible," you'll probably get the same headphones response as last time. At 14, a judgment of his character usually sets off defence, not reflection. What tends to get through is your fear, stated plainly, plus a clear expectation. He can argue with "you're selfish." He can't really argue with "I was scared."

## Opening words

Pick a calm, low-pressure moment. Side by side works better than face to face at this age: in the car, on a walk, while cooking. Then say something like:

> "I want to talk about Saturday. I'm not going to lecture you. I actually want to hear what happened. But first I need you to know what it was like on my end.
>
> Ten o'clock came and went. I called you six times and it went straight to voicemail. By half eleven I was about to start ringing other parents because I didn't know if you were okay. I was really scared.
>
> So, what happened?"

Then **stop talking** and let the silence sit, even if it's uncomfortable. Don't fill it.

## What to do with his answer

**If he gives a reason** (phone died, lost track of time, didn't want to be the first to leave):
> "Okay, thanks for telling me. I get that. Leaving before everyone else is awkward. But the deal was 10, and when you went quiet I had no way of knowing you were safe. That's the part that can't happen again."

**If he shrugs or says "I don't know":**
> "Fair enough. Have a think. But I do need us to work out what happens next time, because I'm not going through Saturday night again."

**If he gets defensive** ("You're overreacting," "Everyone else stays out later"):
> "Maybe other parents are fine with it. I'm not arguing about whether 10 was the right time. We can talk about that separately. This is about agreeing to something and then going silent."

## Close with a plan, not a sentence

> "So here's what I need. If you're going to be late, for any reason, you call or text me *before* 10. If your phone's about to die, you borrow a friend's. Deal?"

Then the consequence. State it calmly and tie it to the problem:
> "Because of Saturday, next weekend home time is 9. If that goes fine, we go back to 10."

That's connected to what happened, it's time-limited, and he can earn his way back. For most 14-year-olds that works better than a long or unrelated punishment.

You could also consider (optional, and a good sign of respect at his age):
> "And if you think 10 is too early, bring it to me on a normal day with a good argument and I'll actually listen. Just not by ignoring it."

## What to avoid

- "Selfish," "irresponsible," "you always," "you never." These are labels, not behaviours, and they end the conversation.
- Going back over it again once you've agreed a plan.
- Making it longer than about 10 minutes. Short and clear lands better than long and thorough.

**Today:** Before you talk to him, say the opening lines out loud to yourself once, so you can deliver them calmly rather than as an accusation. How you say them will matter as much as the words.
