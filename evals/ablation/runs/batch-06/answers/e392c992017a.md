You did what almost every loving parent does in that moment, and it makes sense that it backfired. Here is why, and what to say instead.

### Why "You're super smart!" made it worse

It probably backfired for two reasons.

1. **It argued with her feeling.** She said "I'm stupid," and you said "No, you're not." To a hurt 10-year-old that can sound like "your feelings are wrong," so she pushes harder to be understood.
2. **It kept the conversation about fixed traits.** "Smart" and "stupid" are both labels for *what you are*. If she believes she's "smart," a 72% becomes evidence that maybe she isn't. Research on mindset and praise (Carol Dweck's work is the best known) suggests that kids praised for being smart tend to take setbacks harder than kids praised for effort and strategy. The size of that effect is debated, but the direction fits what you saw.

The aim is to move her away from "What does this say about me?" and toward "What happened, and what can I do about it?"

### This is normal for her age, and it's a teachable moment

At 10, kids start comparing themselves to others and tying their sense of worth to competence. Developmental psychology calls this the "industry vs. inferiority" stage. Big, all-or-nothing reactions to a first real setback are common. "I'm bad at *everything*" from *one* test is classic all-or-nothing thinking, not a sign that something is wrong with her.

### What to say, step by step

**1. Name the feeling first, and don't fix it yet.** Wait until she's calm, not in the middle of homework.
> "That test really hurt. You're used to doing well, and this felt like a punch in the stomach."
> "It's really frustrating to work hard and not get the score you expected."

Then stop and let her talk. If she says "I'm stupid" again, don't argue. Say something like:
> "It feels that way right now. That makes sense when you're this upset."

**2. Separate the event from who she is.**
> "One test tells us what you knew on one day about one topic. It doesn't tell us who you are."
> "Your brain isn't done. Math skills grow the same way muscles do, by working on the hard parts."

**3. Play "thought detective" with her.** This suits her age well. Treat the thought as a clue to check, not a fact.
> "Your brain said 'I'm bad at everything.' Let's check that like detectives. What were your last few math grades? What are you good at outside math?"
> "Is 72% 'bad at everything,' or is it 'this topic was harder'?"

Let *her* find the evidence. It works much better when she reaches the conclusion herself than when you hand it to her.

**4. Get curious about the test itself.**
> "Want to look at it together? Let's find out which kind of problems tripped you up."

Often the drop comes from one new concept, careless errors, or running out of time. Each of those has a clear fix. That turns "I'm stupid" into "I need to practice this one thing."

**5. Share a setback of your own.** A short one: something you weren't good at, how it felt, and what you did about it. Kids this age learn a lot from seeing that adults struggle too.

### About the homework refusal

Don't turn it into a power struggle tonight. Keep the expectation but make the first step tiny:
> "We're not going to fix everything tonight. Let's just do the first two problems together, and then you decide if you want to keep going."

Once she's started, she'll usually keep going. When she finishes something, praise **specific effort and strategy**, not ability:
- "You didn't give up on that one, even when it was annoying."
- "You checked your work. That's exactly what catches mistakes."

Avoid "See, you're so smart!" for now.

### Things to avoid for a while
- "It's just one test, it doesn't matter." To her it does matter, so this dismisses her.
- Comparing her to siblings or classmates.
- Any sign of your own disappointment about the grade. She's already harder on herself than you would be.

### When to look closer
If the "I'm bad at everything" talk lasts more than a couple of weeks, spreads beyond math, or comes with changes in sleep, appetite, or friendships, or if she starts avoiding school, talk to her teacher and consider a child psychologist. If math stays hard even with effort, a learning evaluation can rule out a specific difficulty. Nothing you've described so far suggests either. This sounds like a normal, painful first setback.

### One thing to try today
Once she's calm, sit next to her and say: **"That test really hurt, huh?"** Then just listen, with no fixing and no reassurance, for at least a few minutes. Save the detective work and the homework for after she feels heard.
