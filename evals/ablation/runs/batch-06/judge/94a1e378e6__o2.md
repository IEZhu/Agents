## Answer A
Your friend is right. This is one of the most common misreadings of a p-value, and interviewers ask about it precisely because so many people get it wrong. Once you see it the right way, it's hard to un-see.

### Concept: What a p-value actually measures

**Analogy:** A smoke alarm goes off. "How often does this alarm go off when there's *no* fire?" is a different question from "Given that the alarm went off, how likely is it that there's no fire?" A p-value answers the first kind of question. Your interpretation answers the second.

**The correct definition:** a p-value is the probability of getting a result **at least as extreme as the one you observed, *assuming the null hypothesis is true*.** The whole calculation starts by assuming the null is true. That means it can't also tell you the probability that the null is true. It's conditional in one direction only:

- p-value = P(data this extreme | H₀ is true)
- Your reading = P(H₀ is true | this data)

Those two can be very different numbers, just like P(wet ground | rain) is not the same as P(rain | wet ground).

**A concrete example of how far apart they can be:** Imagine a company tests 1,000 website changes, and only 100 of them truly work (900 do nothing). Say you use α = 0.05 and your tests have 80% power:

| | Test says "significant" | Test says "not significant" |
|---|---|---|
| 100 real effects | 80 | 20 |
| 900 null effects | 45 (5% false alarms) | 855 |

You'd get 125 "significant" results, and 45 of them are false alarms. That's **36%**, not 5%. So even with p < 0.05, the chance a given significant result is a dud depends heavily on how common real effects were to begin with (the base rate). The p-value alone can't tell you that. To estimate P(H₀ | data) you'd need a prior, which is a Bayesian approach.

**So what does p = 0.03 mean?** "If there were truly no effect, we'd see a result this extreme (or more extreme) only about 3% of the time. That's unusual enough that we reject the null at the 5% level." That's all it says. It doesn't give a 97% chance the result is real, and it says nothing about how *big* or *important* the effect is.

### Interview-ready version

> "A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as what we saw, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It's not the probability that the null is true, and it doesn't measure effect size. So p = 0.03 means the data would be fairly surprising under the null, which is evidence against it, not a 97% chance the effect is real."

If you want to stand out, add: "That's why I'd also report a confidence interval and effect size, and think about the base rate of true effects, for example how many A/B tests usually win."

### Key Takeaway

A p-value tells you how surprising your data would be *if the null were true*. It does not tell you how likely the null is *given your data*.


## Answer B
Your friend is right. This mistake is common, and interviewers ask about it because so many people make it. A p-value of 0.03 does **not** mean there's a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true. Here's what it does mean and why the difference matters.

### Concept: What a p-value actually measures

**Analogy first.** Think about "the chance the ground is wet, given that it rained" versus "the chance it rained, given that the ground is wet." The first is close to 100%. The second is much lower, because sprinklers, a hose or a spilled bucket can also wet the ground. Flipping the "given" part gives you a different question with a different answer.

Your interpretation makes that same flip:

| | What it says | Correct? |
|---|---|---|
| **p-value** | P(data at least this extreme **given that** H₀ is true) | Yes |
| **Your version** | P(H₀ is true **given** this data) | No |

**Formal definition:** the p-value is the probability of getting results at least as extreme as the ones you observed, **assuming the null hypothesis is true**. The p-value calculation starts by assuming H₀ is true, so it can't also tell you how likely H₀ is. Standard (frequentist) hypothesis testing doesn't give hypotheses probabilities at all. H₀ is either true or false, and the probabilities describe the data.

So p = 0.03 means: *"If there really were no effect, results this extreme would show up only about 3% of the time by random chance."* That's surprising under H₀, which is why we treat it as evidence against H₀. But "surprising under H₀" is not the same as "97% chance my effect is real."

**A concrete example showing the gap.** Imagine a company runs 1,000 A/B tests. In 100 of them the change really works, and in 900 it doesn't. The team uses α = 0.05 and has 80% power, meaning it catches a real effect 80% of the time.

- Real effects flagged as significant: 100 × 0.80 = **80**
- Null effects flagged as significant by chance: 900 × 0.05 = **45**
- Total significant results: **125**

Of those 125 "significant" results, 45 (**36%**) are false alarms, even though every one of them passed p < 0.05. Your "97% real" rule would have predicted about 5% false alarms. The actual rate depends on things the p-value knows nothing about, like how many of the ideas you test are good (the base rate) and your statistical power.

### Interview-ready answer

If you're asked "What does a p-value of 0.03 mean?", you could say:

> "Assuming the null hypothesis is true, there's a 3% probability of observing a result at least as extreme as ours. It's not the probability that the null is true, and 1 − p isn't the probability that the effect is real. It also says nothing about how *large* or practically important the effect is. For that I'd look at the effect size and confidence interval."

That last sentence isn't required, but it shows you know where p-values stop being useful, and interviewers like hearing that.

### Key Takeaway

A p-value tells you how surprising your data would be **if** the null were true. It doesn't tell you how likely the null is **given** your data. If you keep track of which way the "given" points, you won't blank on this question.
