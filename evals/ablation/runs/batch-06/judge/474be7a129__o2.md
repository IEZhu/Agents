## Latest user message
My 10-year-old daughter got a 72% on her math test yesterday (she usually gets 85-90). She came home, cried, and said "I'm stupid, I'm bad at everything, I'll never be good at math." Now she's refusing to do her math homework and says there's no point. I told her "you're not stupid, you're super smart!" but she just got more upset. What should I actually say to her?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Explains plausibly why the parent's direct contradiction ('you're super smart') backfired (e.g. it dismissed her feelings or just argued with her belief) and gives the parent something concrete to do instead
2. Recommends first acknowledging the daughter's disappointment or frustration before questioning her conclusion
3. Gives concrete, age-appropriate questions the parent can ask so the child checks the thought herself (e.g. about her usual 85-90 scores, whether she is really bad at everything, what one test shows), not only statements for the parent to say
4. Names or clearly describes the overgeneralizing / all-or-nothing pattern in the child's words ('everything', 'never') and connects that belief to her refusing the homework ('there's no point')

## Answer A
You were trying to lift her up, and that's a caring instinct. It made her more upset because "you're super smart!" argues with how she feels. From her side, it can sound like "your feelings are wrong." It also keeps the conversation on whether she's smart or stupid, and that's the question that is scaring her.

**Why this is happening at 10**

This reaction is very common at her age. Children around 10 are building their sense of being competent: "Am I good at things?" (Erikson calls this stage *industry vs. inferiority*). They also tend to think in all-or-nothing terms, so one bad result can quickly become "I'm bad at everything." For a child who usually gets 85-90%, a 72% can feel like her identity has been shaken, not like one bad day. Refusing homework is most likely self-protection: *if I don't try, I can't fail again.*

**What to say, in this order**

**1. Name the feeling first. Don't fix it yet.**
> "You were really disappointed about that test. When you're used to doing well, a lower grade can feel awful."

Then stop and let her respond. If she cries, stay close and don't hurry her. She needs to feel understood before she can think clearly.

**2. Gently separate the feeling from the conclusion.**
Don't tell her the thought is wrong. Help her notice that it's a thought:
> "It sounds like your brain is telling you 'I'm stupid and I'll never be good at math.' That's what brains say when we feel really bad. It doesn't mean it's true."

You can make it a small detective game: "Let's check the evidence. What were your last few math grades?" Let her say "85, 90..." herself. Kids believe evidence they find themselves more than evidence we hand them.

**3. Move from "smart" to "what happened."**
> "One test tells us something went differently this time. Want to look at it together and figure out what?"

Go through the test with her when she's calmer. Maybe that evening, maybe tomorrow. Look for patterns: a new topic, careless mistakes, running out of time, a bad night's sleep. Now the grade is information she can use, not a judgment about who she is.

**4. Praise effort and strategy, not ability.**
Instead of "you're so smart," try:
> "I noticed you kept working on that problem even when it was hard."
> "You figured out a different way to do it. That's how people get better at math."

Research on "growth mindset" (Carol Dweck's work is the best known) suggests that praising process tends to help kids cope with setbacks better than praising intelligence. The size of the effect is debated in later studies, so treat it as a useful habit, not a magic phrase.

**5. Share one of your own setbacks (briefly).**
> "When I was in school, I once got a bad grade on ___ and felt like giving up. Here's what I did next..."

This shows her that struggling is normal and that people recover from it.

**About the homework tonight**

Don't turn it into a power struggle, and don't let it disappear either. Make the first step very small:
> "You don't have to do all of it right now. Let's just do the first two problems together, and then decide."

Once she gets started, the "there's no point" feeling usually eases. Sit with her at first, then gradually step back.

**When to look more closely**

A strong reaction to one test is normal and usually fades within days. Talk to her teacher or the school counselor if "I'm bad at everything" spreads beyond math, lasts more than a couple of weeks, or comes with changes in sleep, appetite, friendships, or things she usually enjoys. If it keeps happening, a child psychologist can help. The teacher may also know whether the class started a harder unit, which would explain a lot.

**One thing to try today**

Tonight, open with only step 1, "You were really disappointed about that test," and see what she tells you. Leave the reassurance, the problem-solving and the homework until after she feels heard.


## Answer B
You did what almost every loving parent does in that moment, and it makes sense that it backfired. Here is why, and what to say instead.

### Why "You're super smart!" made it worse

It probably backfired for two reasons.

1. **It argued with her feeling.** She said "I'm stupid," and you said "No, you're not." To a hurt 10-year-old that can sound like "your feelings are wrong," so she pushes harder to be understood.
2. **It kept the conversation about fixed traits.** "Smart" and "stupid" are both labels for *what you are*. If she believes she's "smart," a 72% becomes evidence that maybe she isn't. Research on mindset and praise (Carol Dweck's work is the best known) suggests that kids praised for being smart tend to take setbacks harder than kids praised for effort and strategy. The size of that effect is debated, but the direction fits what you saw.

The aim is to move her away from "What does this say about me?" and toward "What happened, and what can I do about it?"

### This is normal for her age, and it's a teachable moment

At 10, kids start comparing themselves to others and tying their sense of worth to competence. Developmental psychology calls this the "industry vs. inferiority" stage. Big, all-or-nothing reactions to a first real setback are common. "I'm bad at *everything*" from *one* test is classic all-or-nothing thinking, not a sign that something is wrong with her.

### What to say, step by step

**1. Name the feeling first, and don't fix it yet.** Wait until she's calm, not in the middle of homework.
> "That test really hurt. You're used to doing well, and this felt like a punch in the stomach."
> "It's really frustrating to work hard and not get the score you expected."

Then stop and let her talk. If she says "I'm stupid" again, don't argue. Say something like:
> "It feels that way right now. That makes sense when you're this upset."

**2. Separate the event from who she is.**
> "One test tells us what you knew on one day about one topic. It doesn't tell us who you are."
> "Your brain isn't done. Math skills grow the same way muscles do, by working on the hard parts."

**3. Play "thought detective" with her.** This suits her age well. Treat the thought as a clue to check, not a fact.
> "Your brain said 'I'm bad at everything.' Let's check that like detectives. What were your last few math grades? What are you good at outside math?"
> "Is 72% 'bad at everything,' or is it 'this topic was harder'?"

Let *her* find the evidence. It works much better when she reaches the conclusion herself than when you hand it to her.

**4. Get curious about the test itself.**
> "Want to look at it together? Let's find out which kind of problems tripped you up."

Often the drop comes from one new concept, careless errors, or running out of time. Each of those has a clear fix. That turns "I'm stupid" into "I need to practice this one thing."

**5. Share a setback of your own.** A short one: something you weren't good at, how it felt, and what you did about it. Kids this age learn a lot from seeing that adults struggle too.

### About the homework refusal

Don't turn it into a power struggle tonight. Keep the expectation but make the first step tiny:
> "We're not going to fix everything tonight. Let's just do the first two problems together, and then you decide if you want to keep going."

Once she's started, she'll usually keep going. When she finishes something, praise **specific effort and strategy**, not ability:
- "You didn't give up on that one, even when it was annoying."
- "You checked your work. That's exactly what catches mistakes."

Avoid "See, you're so smart!" for now.

### Things to avoid for a while
- "It's just one test, it doesn't matter." To her it does matter, so this dismisses her.
- Comparing her to siblings or classmates.
- Any sign of your own disappointment about the grade. She's already harder on herself than you would be.

### When to look closer
If the "I'm bad at everything" talk lasts more than a couple of weeks, spreads beyond math, or comes with changes in sleep, appetite, or friendships, or if she starts avoiding school, talk to her teacher and consider a child psychologist. If math stays hard even with effort, a learning evaluation can rule out a specific difficulty. Nothing you've described so far suggests either. This sounds like a normal, painful first setback.

### One thing to try today
Once she's calm, sit next to her and say: **"That test really hurt, huh?"** Then just listen, with no fixing and no reassurance, for at least a few minutes. Save the detective work and the homework for after she feels heard.
