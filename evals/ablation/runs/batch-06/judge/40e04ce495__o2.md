## Latest user message
My son is 14. We agreed he'd be home from his friend's place by 10pm on Saturday. He walked in at 11:40, his phone was off the whole time, and I'd called him 6 times and was about to start phoning other parents. When he came in I was so wound up I just said 'go to your room, we'll talk tomorrow.' Now it's tomorrow. I want him to really get how selfish and irresponsible that was, but last time I lectured him he just shut down and put his headphones on. What exactly should I say to open the conversation? Give me the actual words.

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. The suggested opening words state what happened factually (agreed time vs. 11:40, phone off, the unanswered calls) without labeling the son as selfish, irresponsible or thoughtless.
2. The script expresses the parent's own feeling (e.g. scared, worried, panicked) and ties it to a need such as knowing he is safe or being able to trust agreements, phrased as I-statements rather than "you made me feel...".
3. The script includes a concrete forward-looking request or proposed agreement (e.g. text or call if he will be late, phone kept charged/on, a check-in time), not only a general demand to be more responsible.
4. The answer gives actual words for opening the conversation as asked, avoids turning them into a lecture, and leaves room for the son to respond (e.g. asks what happened).

## Answer A
You were scared for almost two hours. Sending him to his room instead of saying something you'd regret was a good call.

Before the script, one honest point. If you open with "selfish and irresponsible," you'll probably get the same headphones response as last time. At 14, a judgment of his character usually sets off defence, not reflection. What tends to get through is your fear, stated plainly, plus a clear expectation. He can argue with "you're selfish." He can't really argue with "I was scared."

## Opening words

Pick a calm, low-pressure moment. Side by side works better than face to face at this age: in the car, on a walk, while cooking. Then say something like:

> "I want to talk about Saturday. I'm not going to lecture you. I actually want to hear what happened. But first I need you to know what it was like on my end.
>
> Ten o'clock came and went. I called you six times and it went straight to voicemail. By half eleven I was about to start ringing other parents because I didn't know if you were okay. I was really scared.
>
> So, what happened?"

Then **stop talking** and let the silence sit, even if it's uncomfortable. Don't fill it.

## What to do with his answer

**If he gives a reason** (phone died, lost track of time, didn't want to be the first to leave):
> "Okay, thanks for telling me. I get that. Leaving before everyone else is awkward. But the deal was 10, and when you went quiet I had no way of knowing you were safe. That's the part that can't happen again."

**If he shrugs or says "I don't know":**
> "Fair enough. Have a think. But I do need us to work out what happens next time, because I'm not going through Saturday night again."

**If he gets defensive** ("You're overreacting," "Everyone else stays out later"):
> "Maybe other parents are fine with it. I'm not arguing about whether 10 was the right time. We can talk about that separately. This is about agreeing to something and then going silent."

## Close with a plan, not a sentence

> "So here's what I need. If you're going to be late, for any reason, you call or text me *before* 10. If your phone's about to die, you borrow a friend's. Deal?"

Then the consequence. State it calmly and tie it to the problem:
> "Because of Saturday, next weekend home time is 9. If that goes fine, we go back to 10."

That's connected to what happened, it's time-limited, and he can earn his way back. For most 14-year-olds that works better than a long or unrelated punishment.

You could also consider (optional, and a good sign of respect at his age):
> "And if you think 10 is too early, bring it to me on a normal day with a good argument and I'll actually listen. Just not by ignoring it."

## What to avoid

- "Selfish," "irresponsible," "you always," "you never." These are labels, not behaviours, and they end the conversation.
- Going back over it again once you've agreed a plan.
- Making it longer than about 10 minutes. Short and clear lands better than long and thorough.

**Today:** Before you talk to him, say the opening lines out loud to yourself once, so you can deliver them calmly rather than as an accusation. How you say them will matter as much as the words.


## Answer B
Sending him to his room last night instead of having it out at midnight was a good call. Neither of you was ready to talk then.

Before the words themselves, one thing to change. If your goal for the opening is to make him feel how selfish he was, you'll probably get the headphones again. At 14, when a kid hears "you are selfish and irresponsible," it lands as an attack on who he is, and he'll defend himself or shut down. What he can actually take in is what his choices cost you. So open with the fear, not the verdict. You'll still get to responsibility, just in a way he can hear.

**Choose the setting.** Talk side by side if you can: in the car, walking the dog, while making food. Teens tend to talk more easily when they aren't being stared at. Keep your opening short. The longer you talk, the sooner he checks out.

### Opening (say roughly this, then stop)

> "I want to talk about Saturday. I'm not going to lecture you. I'm going to tell you what it was like on my end, and then I want to hear what happened on yours.
>
> We agreed on ten. By eleven your phone was off and I'd called six times. By half eleven I was about to start ringing other parents, because I honestly didn't know if you were okay. That's what those hours were for me: scared, not just annoyed.
>
> So what happened?"

**Then stop talking.** Let the silence sit even if it's uncomfortable. This is where he decides whether it's safe to be honest.

### While he answers

- **If he gives a reason** (phone died, lost track of time): don't argue with it yet. Say "Okay. So the phone died. What could you have done once you realised?" That lets him work out the answer himself, like borrowing a friend's phone or asking the friend's parent to text you, instead of you telling him.
- **If he shrugs or says "I dunno":** "I'm not trying to trap you. I actually want to know, because I need to be able to trust the next time you go out."
- **If he gets defensive** ("you're overreacting," "everyone else stays out later"): "Maybe other people's parents are fine with it. I'm not. I don't need you to agree it was a big deal. I need you to get that it was a big deal *to me*, and that we had a deal."
- **If he reaches for the headphones:** "I'm not going to go on for ages. Two more minutes and we're done." Then actually keep to that.

### Closing: agree on a plan together

> "Here's what I need going forward. If you're going to be late, or your phone's dying, I hear from you before the time we agreed, not after. A late text I can deal with. Silence I can't. What would make that easy for you to actually do?"

If you want a consequence, tie it to trust and put a time limit on it, rather than making it feel like revenge. For example: "Next weekend you're home by nine, and if that goes fine we're back to ten." That ties the rule to earning trust back, and at his age that tends to work better than a punishment that just builds resentment.

### What to avoid

- The words "selfish" and "irresponsible." Describe what he did (phone off, 100 minutes late, no contact), not what kind of person he is.
- Opening with "Do you have any idea how...?" He'll hear it as a lecture starting.
- Going back over every detail of the night once he's acknowledged it. Once he's taken it in, stop there. Winning the argument matters less than him coming back to you next time.

If he's honest with you at all, even just "yeah, I turned it off because I didn't want you calling," thank him for telling you the truth before you deal with what he said. That makes it more likely he'll tell you the truth next time.
