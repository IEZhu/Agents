## 1. California statutes (the ones that matter most for you)

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600: the core rule**
- Every contract that restrains anyone from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business is void, except under the narrow statutory exceptions (sale of a business, dissolution of a partnership or LLC, §§ 16601–16602.5). None of these fits an employee non-compete.
- AB 1076 (effective Jan. 1, 2024) added language saying the section is to be read broadly and codifying *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen* (see below). It voids non-competes "no matter how narrowly tailored." (Recalled; confirm the exact wording.)

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1: notice requirement (AB 1076)**
- Employers had to send current employees, and former employees employed after Jan. 1, 2022, individualized written notice by **Feb. 14, 2024** that their non-compete clause is void.
- Failing to send the notice counts as unfair competition. (Recalled; confirm the date and scope.)
- **Question for your lawyer:** Did you ever receive such a notice? If not, that fact could help you.

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5: the extraterritorial provision (SB 699, effective Jan. 1, 2024)**
This is probably the most directly relevant statute. As I recall its substance:
- A contract void under this chapter is unenforceable **regardless of where and when it was signed**.
- It is unenforceable **regardless of whether the contract was signed and employment was maintained outside California**.
- An employer may not attempt to enforce a void contract.
- An employer may not enter into one with an employee or prospective employee.
- An employee, former employee, or prospective employee may sue for **injunctive relief, actual damages, or both**, and a prevailing employee **recovers reasonable attorney's fees and costs**.
- HR's threat to sue may itself count as an "attempt to enforce." Ask your lawyer whether it gives you a claim of your own.

**Cal. Labor Code § 925: choice-of-law and forum clauses**
This statute targets exactly your contract structure:
- An employer may not require an employee who **primarily resides and works in California**, as a condition of employment, to agree to a provision that:
  - requires the employee to litigate a California-arising claim outside California, or
  - deprives the employee of the substantive protection of California law.
- Such a provision is **voidable by the employee**. If it's voided, the matter is decided in California under California law.
- The employee can get injunctive relief and **attorney's fees**.
- It applies to contracts entered into, modified, or extended on or after **Jan. 1, 2017**, so your 2023 agreement is covered.
- **Key exception (§ 925(e)):** it does not apply if you were **individually represented by counsel** when negotiating the choice-of-law and forum terms. Tell your lawyer whether you had your own attorney in 2023. Most employees don't.
- **Key fact:** did you primarily live and work in California *when you signed*, or did you move to San Diego later? § 925 focuses on the employee who "primarily resides and works in California." § 16600.5 is broader and applies regardless of where you signed. Bring your residence history.

## 2. Texas statutes (what HR is relying on)

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §§ 15.50–15.52 (Covenants Not to Compete Act)**
- **§ 15.50(a):** a non-compete is enforceable if it is "ancillary to or part of an otherwise enforceable agreement at the time the agreement is made." It must also contain **reasonable limitations as to time, geographical area, and scope of activity** that do not impose a greater restraint than necessary to protect goodwill or other business interests.
  - Texas courts typically find the "otherwise enforceable agreement" requirement met where the employer promised and provided confidential information or specialized training (*Alex Sheshunoff Mgmt. Servs. v. Johnson*, 209 S.W.3d 644 (Tex. 2006); *Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011); both recalled, verify).
- **§ 15.51(c):** if the limits are unreasonable, the court **must reform** the covenant to a reasonable scope rather than void it. It may not award damages for conduct before reformation.
  - A 12-month, **all-of-North-America, any-competitor** restriction on a software engineer is broad. Even a Texas court may well narrow it. Don't treat "valid under Texas law" as all-or-nothing.
- **§ 15.52:** these procedures preempt other Texas law on non-compete enforcement.

**How Texas treats choice of law:** Texas follows **Restatement (Second) Conflict of Laws § 187**. A contractual choice of law is not applied if another state has a **materially greater interest** and the chosen law would **contravene that state's fundamental policy**. The Texas Supreme Court applied this test *against* a choice-of-law clause in a non-compete case in *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990). (Recalled, verify.) In that case the facts ran the other way: a Florida clause was refused in favor of Texas's policy. The same framework is the best argument that a Texas court should apply California's policy to you.

**Federal level:** the FTC's 2024 nationwide non-compete ban was set aside in *Ryan LLC v. FTC* (N.D. Tex. 2024). As I recall, the FTC later dropped its appeal. Don't count on federal law helping you.
