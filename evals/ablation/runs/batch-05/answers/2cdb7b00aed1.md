## 1. California statutes (your strongest ground)

### Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600: the core ban
- **(a)** "Except as provided in this chapter, every contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void." (recalled; I'm fairly confident of this wording.)
- **(b)** This subsection was added by AB 1076 and took effect January 1, 2024. It says § 16600 is to be read broadly, following *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen*, to void non-competes in the employment context "no matter how narrowly tailored," unless a statutory exception applies. The exceptions cover sale of a business and dissolution of a partnership or LLC, and none of them fits your case. (Paraphrased, not verified.)

### Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1: the notice requirement (AB 1076)
- It is unlawful to include a non-compete in an employment contract, or to require an employee to sign one, unless an exception applies.
- Employers had to send written notice **by February 14, 2024** to current employees, and to former employees employed after January 1, 2022, saying the clause is void. (Recalled; confirm the date and wording.)
- A violation counts as an act of **unfair competition**, meaning a violation of Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.
- **Relevance to you:** you joined in 2023, so you're in the covered group. Ask yourself whether you ever received that notice. If you didn't, that's a separate violation to raise with your lawyer.

### Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5: the out-of-state contract rule (SB 699, effective January 1, 2024)
This one answers HR most directly. My recollection of its structure, not verified:
- **(a)** A contract that is void under this chapter is unenforceable "regardless of where and when the contract was signed."
- **(b)** An employer or former employer "shall not attempt to enforce" a void contract, "regardless of whether the contract was signed and the employment was maintained outside of California."
- **(c)** An employer shall not enter into a contract with an employee or prospective employee that contains a void provision.
- **(d)** Entering into or attempting to enforce such a contract is a **civil violation**.
- **(e)** The employee has a **private right of action** for injunctive relief and/or actual damages, and **is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees and costs**.
- **Relevance to you:** a lawsuit in Texas to enforce the clause is arguably itself an "attempt to enforce," which gives you your own claim, including fees.

### Labor Code § 925: choice-of-law and forum clauses
Recalled wording; verify it:
- **(a)** An employer "shall not require an employee who primarily resides and works in California, as a condition of employment, to agree to a provision that would do either of the following: (1) Require the employee to adjudicate outside of California a claim arising in California. (2) Deprive the employee of the substantive protection of California law with respect to a controversy arising in California."
- **(b)** Such a provision is **voidable by the employee**. If you void it, the matter is decided in California under California law.
- **(c)** You may recover attorney's fees in addition to other remedies.
- **(e)** **Exception:** the section doesn't apply if you were **individually represented by a lawyer** in negotiating the agreement, and that lawyer designated the venue or governing law. **Your lawyer will ask about this.** Did you have your own counsel when you signed in 2023?
- **(f)** The section applies to contracts entered into, modified, or extended on or after **January 1, 2017**. Your 2023 agreement qualifies.
- **Caveat:** § 925 is written for employees who "primarily reside and work in California." You'll need to show that was true when you signed, or at least that it's true now. Remote work from San Diego since you were hired is a good fact for you.

## 2. Texas statutes (what the employer is relying on)

### Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 15.50(a)
Recalled; I'm fairly confident of this wording:
> "Notwithstanding Section 15.05 of this code, and subject to any applicable provisions of Subsection (b), a covenant not to compete is enforceable if it is ancillary to or part of an otherwise enforceable agreement at the time the agreement is made to the extent that it contains limitations as to time, geographical area, and scope of activity to be restrained that are reasonable and do not impose a greater restraint than is necessary to protect the goodwill or other business interest of the promisee."

### Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §§ 15.51–15.52
- **§ 15.51(c):** if a covenant is overbroad, the court **reforms** it to a reasonable scope rather than voiding it. Damages are limited for the period before reformation.
- **§ 15.52:** these criteria and procedures **preempt** other Texas law, including common law, on enforceability.

### Points for your lawyer, even under Texas law
- **"Any competitor in North America" is broad.** Texas courts often find restraints that cover an entire continent, or bar any role at any competitor, to be unreasonable. Under § 15.51 the likely result is **narrowing, not voiding**, so this is only a fallback argument. (My judgment, not a cited holding.)
- **"Ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement"** usually requires the employer to have provided something like confidential information or specialized training. The cases I recall on this, which you should verify, are *Alex Sheshunoff Mgmt. Servs., L.P. v. Johnson*, 209 S.W.3d 644 (Tex. 2006) and *Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011). Both were decided in the employer's favor on this point, so don't treat it as an easy win.

## 3. How the choice-of-law fight actually works

- **A California court** will very likely apply §§ 16600/16600.5 and Labor Code § 925, and refuse the Texas clauses.
- **A Texas court** would apply **Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187**. Under § 187(2)(b), the chosen law is disregarded if another state has a "materially greater interest" and applying the chosen law would violate that state's "fundamental policy."
- **The Texas Supreme Court case on this** is *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990) (recalled; verify). There the court **refused to apply a Florida choice-of-law clause** to a non-compete and applied Texas law, because Texas had the materially greater interest and treated non-compete law as fundamental policy. The facts run the other way from yours, but the reasoning helps you: the state where the employee lives and works (California) has the greater interest, and California has written its policy into statute as expressly "fundamental" and extraterritorial. Expect your employer to argue that a Texas court shouldn't let California's policy override Texas's own. This is a live dispute, not a sure thing.
- **Forum clause in federal court:** if the case lands in federal court, *Atlantic Marine Constr. Co. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013) (recalled) makes forum-selection clauses hard to escape. The main counterargument is Labor Code § 925, and courts have split on how much weight it gets.
- **Timing:** this is why lawyers in your situation often consider **filing first in California**, for example a declaratory-relief claim or a § 16600.5 claim, before starting the new job. Ask your lawyer about this directly.
