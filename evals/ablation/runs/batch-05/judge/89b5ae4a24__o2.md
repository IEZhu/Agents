## Answer A
**Bottom line:** Don't treat this as an at-will termination. He lives and works in Montana, and Montana is the one U.S. state that isn't at-will once an employee is past the probationary period. Under the Montana Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act (WDEA), a firing with no stated reason and no documentation, of an employee with more than two years' service, is the kind of case the statute was written for. Your handbook's at-will clause doesn't change that. The fix is manageable, but it changes the plan for Friday, so sort it out before the call rather than after.

## Why Montana changes the plan

- **Which state's law applies:** He has worked remotely from Bozeman the whole time, so Montana employment law almost certainly governs his termination, even though you're a Delaware corporation based in Denver. A Colorado or Delaware choice-of-law clause in the handbook is unlikely to get you around the WDEA.
- **Good cause requirement** (Mont. Code Ann. § 39-2-904(1)(b), recalled): Once the probationary period is over, a discharge is wrongful if it isn't for "good cause." Good cause means reasonable, job-related grounds, such as failing to perform job duties satisfactorily or another legitimate business reason, judged by the employer's reasonable good-faith judgment (§ 39-2-903, amended 2019, recalled). Mediocre performance can qualify. The weak spot is proving it with nothing on paper.
- **Probation is over:** The probationary period defaults to 12 months if you never set one, and it can't exceed 18 months (recalled). At two-plus years he is past it either way.
- **Your own policies count:** A discharge is also wrongful if it breaks the express terms of your written personnel policy (§ 39-2-904(1)(c), recalled). Check whether the handbook promises progressive discipline, warnings or a PIP. If it does and you skipped those steps, that is a second theory of liability.
- **What he could recover:** Up to 4 years of lost wages and fringe benefits, minus what he earns in the meantime, plus punitive damages in some cases (§ 39-2-905, recalled). He has 1 year to file (§ 39-2-911, recalled). Two weeks' severance is small next to that.
- **Reason letter:** Montana's service-letter statute (§ 39-2-801, recalled) requires you to give him the reason for discharge in writing if he asks. "No reason given" won't hold, so decide the reason now and make sure it's true and consistent.

## Checklist before Friday

**1. Decide: proceed or postpone**
- ☐ Get a short consult with Montana employment counsel. This is the single most useful step.
- ☐ Option A, postpone 30–60 days: put him on a written PIP with specific metrics, document the results, then decide. This is the lowest-risk route.
- ☐ Option B, proceed Friday: state a truthful, job-related reason, pull together whatever evidence exists, and offer severance that is large enough to be worth signing a release for.

**2. Build the good-cause file (even for Option B)**
- ☐ Objective metrics: CSAT/NPS, churn or renewal numbers on his accounts, ticket response times, quota or QBR data, compared with peers.
- ☐ Emails, Slack messages or 1:1 notes where performance concerns were raised, even informally.
- ☐ Performance reviews. Watch for ones that rated him "meets expectations," because those cut against you.
- ☐ A one-page decision memo: who decided, when, why, and what the evidence is.

**3. Discrimination and retaliation screen**
- ☐ His age (40 or older triggers extra release rules; see item 4), any recent leave, medical or disability issues, complaints he made (harassment, wage issues, safety), workers' comp claims, or protected activity.
- ☐ Whether similarly situated reps with similar numbers were treated the same way.
- ☐ At 40 employees, Title VII, the ADA and the ADEA all apply, as does the Montana Human Rights Act. The FMLA doesn't apply below 50 employees.

**4. Separation agreement and release**
- ☐ A written release that expressly covers WDEA claims, with severance he isn't otherwise entitled to as the consideration.
- ☐ If he is 40 or older, OWBPA requires 21 days to consider the agreement, a 7-day revocation period, a written recommendation to consult an attorney, and the ADEA named in the release.
- ☐ Size the severance against the WDEA exposure. Two weeks after two years is thin. Something like 4–8 weeks plus a COBRA subsidy is more likely to get a signature (a judgment call, not a legal requirement).
- ☐ Mutual non-disparagement, a neutral reference, return of company property, and a reminder of his existing confidentiality obligations.
- ☐ Don't make his final pay depend on signing.

**5. Final pay and benefits**
- ☐ Final wages: Montana generally requires payment immediately on discharge, unless you have a written policy allowing payment by the next regular payday or within 15 days, whichever comes first (§ 39-3-205, recalled). Plan to pay on Friday.
- ☐ Accrued PTO: check your policy. Montana has generally treated earned vacation as wages that must be paid out (recalled; verify).
- ☐ COBRA election notice (you have 20+ employees), end dates for benefits, and 401(k) or equity vesting and exercise-window information.

**6. Montana compliance check**
- ☐ Confirm you're registered in Montana for income tax withholding, unemployment insurance and workers' comp. If you aren't, fix it now, because a termination often brings a UI claim that exposes the gap.
- ☐ Any unemployment insurance separation notice Montana requires (verify with the Montana Department of Labor & Industry).

**7. Internal grievance procedure (worth considering)**
- ☐ Under the WDEA as amended in 2019, if you have a written internal appeal procedure and give him a copy within 7 days of the discharge, he generally has to use it before suing (§ 39-2-911, recalled). If you have one, include it in the termination packet.

**8. Logistics for the call**
- ☐ Two company people on the call (manager plus HR), a short script with the reason stated consistently, and no off-script comments.
- ☐ Timed shutoff of system access (SSO, CRM, Slack, email) and a plan to hand off his accounts.
- ☐ A prepaid return shipping kit for his laptop and equipment.
- ☐ A written termination letter, the separation agreement, and benefits and COBRA information, sent by email right after the call.

## Answer B
**Bottom line:** Your plan assumes at-will employment, and for this employee that assumption is probably wrong. He lives and works in Montana, and Montana is the one US state that is not at-will. Once an employee is past the probationary period, Montana requires "good cause" to fire them. Your handbook's at-will clause doesn't override that statute. With no documentation, no stated reason and only two weeks of severance, the plan as written leaves real exposure. Before Friday, you need to fix the approach, not just gather paperwork.

## The core problem: Montana's Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act (WDEA)

*Citations below are from memory and I haven't verified them here. Have Montana counsel confirm the current text, because the Act was amended in 2021.*

- **Which state's law applies.** Montana law very likely governs someone who has worked full-time from Bozeman for two years, even though you're a Delaware corporation based in Colorado. Where you incorporated doesn't matter for employment law, and a Colorado choice-of-law clause (if you have one) is unlikely to get around a Montana employee-protection statute. That second point is my judgment call, not settled law.
- **When a firing is wrongful** (Mont. Code Ann. § 39-2-904, recalled). A discharge is wrongful if any of these is true:
  1. It retaliates against the employee for reporting a violation or refusing to break public policy.
  2. It lacks good cause after the probationary period. The default probationary period is 12 months, and he is well past it.
  3. It breaks the employer's own written personnel policy.
- **What counts as good cause.** Since the 2021 amendments, good cause includes failing to perform job duties satisfactorily. That helps you, but you still have to be able to prove the performance problem, and today you can't.
- **Damages.** If he wins, he can recover up to 4 years of lost wages and fringe benefits, minus what he earns in the meantime (§ 39-2-905, recalled). Punitive damages are possible if there was fraud or malice. Measured against that, two weeks of severance is thin.
- **Deadline to sue.** He generally has 1 year to file. Montana also has an arbitration-offer mechanism that shifts attorney fees (§ 39-2-914, recalled).

## Pre-Friday checklist

**1. Decide your approach first (with Montana employment counsel, this week)**
- [ ] **Option A: terminate for good cause.** Write an honest performance rationale and back it with whatever objective evidence already exists: CSAT/NPS scores, churn or renewal rates on his accounts, ticket metrics, missed targets, Slack or email feedback, and how he compares to peers.
- [ ] **Option B: offer severance in exchange for a release (most common fix).** Give meaningfully more than two weeks as payment for a signed release of claims, including WDEA claims. This is usually the cheapest way to close the risk.
- [ ] **Option C: postpone.** Put him on a real, documented PIP (30 to 60 days) and decide at the end of it.
- [ ] **Don't create backdated or after-the-fact write-ups.** That turns a weak case into a bad one, and it can support a finding of malice.

**2. Screen for other risks**
- [ ] Check for protected characteristics and recent protected activity: age 40+, disability, pregnancy, recent leave requests, workers' comp claims, complaints about pay, safety, discrimination or harassment, and whistleblowing. The Montana Human Rights Act covers employers of any size, and Title VII and the ADA apply at 15+ employees.
- [ ] Read the handbook for any progressive-discipline or "we will warn before terminating" language. If you promised steps you skipped, that alone can make the firing wrongful under the "violated its own written policy" ground.
- [ ] Check his offer letter or contract for severance, notice, or equity-vesting terms.
- [ ] Confirm the decision-maker, the business reason, and that nobody else with similar performance is being treated differently.

**3. Severance agreement (if you go with Option B)**
- [ ] The release names WDEA, Montana Human Rights Act, Title VII, ADA, ADEA and related claims. Carve out claims that by law can't be waived, such as unemployment benefits, workers' comp and vested benefits.
- [ ] **If he's 40 or older**, the release must meet OWBPA requirements: 21 days to consider, 7 days to revoke, and written advice to consult a lawyer. So he won't sign on Friday.
- [ ] Add mutual non-disparagement, confidentiality, return of property, and an agreed reference or neutral-reference script.
- [ ] State that payment depends on signing and that the revocation period has passed.

**4. Pay and benefits (Montana rules, recalled; verify)**
- [ ] **Final paycheck.** Montana generally requires immediate payment on discharge, unless a written policy lets you pay by the next regular payday or within 15 days, whichever comes first (§ 39-3-205, recalled). Have the check ready.
- [ ] **Accrued, unused vacation.** Montana treats it as earned wages, so pay it out.
- [ ] **COBRA notice.** Federal COBRA applies because you have 20+ employees. Tell your plan administrator promptly so the election notice goes out on time.
- [ ] Prepare any Montana-required unemployment insurance information. Don't contest his UI claim just for leverage.

**5. The reason you give**
- [ ] **Don't plan on giving no reason.** Montana has a service-letter statute (§ 39-2-801, recalled): if a discharged employee asks in writing, the employer must state the reason. A reason that shows up later and differs from what you said on the call, or that you can't support, looks like pretext.
- [ ] Prepare a short, factual script: "We've decided to end your employment because your performance in [areas] hasn't met expectations." Have a second person on the call as a witness.
- [ ] **Internal grievance procedure.** If you have a written one, the WDEA requires you to give him a copy within 7 days of termination (recalled).

**6. Logistics**
- [ ] Shut off system access at the time of the call (SSO, CRM, support tools, Slack, email).
- [ ] Send a prepaid return kit for his laptop and equipment. Don't withhold wages to force the return; Montana limits wage deductions.
- [ ] Reassign his customer accounts and prepare a message to customers.
- [ ] Send a written termination letter the same day. It should cover the final pay date, benefits end date, COBRA, the severance offer and signing deadline, and a reminder about confidentiality obligations.

## Risk summary
- **Highest risk:** a WDEA claim for firing without good cause. It's realistic here, and exposure goes up to 4 years of wages.
- **What reduces it most:** a properly drafted release supported by real consideration, or taking the time to build documentation.
- **Worth a call:** an hour with a Montana employment lawyer before Friday is inexpensive compared with the exposure. Also check whether you have EPLI insurance, since many policies require notice before you act.
