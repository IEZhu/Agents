## Answer A
**Short answer (Serbia):** Your friend is wrong on the law, though your position is much stronger than the phrase "not the owner yet" makes it sound. In Serbia you do not own real estate until you are registered in the cadastre (upis). Until then you are a *vanknjižni vlasnik*, an owner outside the register: a buyer with a valid title (the solemnized contract), full payment and possession. The courts and the enforcement rules give that position real protection, but it does not protect itself. You need to act now, before the Tax Administration's lien is registered or enforced.

## 1. Why the notarized contract did not make you the owner

- **Registration is what transfers ownership.** Under the Law on the Basics of Property Relations (*Zakon o osnovama svojinskopravnih odnosa*, ZOSPO), ownership of real estate passes on a legal transaction only when it is entered in the public register (čl. 33 ZOSPO, recalled, not verified; confirm the wording). The Law on the Procedure for Registration in the Real Estate Cadastre (*Zakon o postupku upisa u katastar nepokretnosti i vodova*, 2018) follows the same rule: registration of ownership is **constitutive**, not merely declarative.
- **What the contract gives you.** The solemnized contract is your **legal basis** (*pravni osnov*, *iustus titulus*). Together with payment it gives you an enforceable claim against the seller for transfer of ownership. Your friend is confusing "valid title" with "ownership". Those are two steps, and you have completed only the first.
- **Why the lien threat is real.** Until you are registered, the cadastre shows the seller as owner. A creditor such as the Tax Administration (*Poreska uprava*) can in principle ask to register a lien against a property registered in the debtor's name.

## 2. Why you are still not in a weak position

**a) Priority from the filing date.** Serbian cadastre law ranks registrations by when the application was received, not by when it is decided (*red prvenstva*). If the notary filed your contract electronically in March, as notaries are required to do, your application should rank ahead of any lien the Tax Administration applies for later. This holds as long as your application is eventually approved or is kept alive on appeal (my understanding of the rule; confirm the exact article and how it applies to your case with a lawyer). **This is the key fact to check. Everything below depends on whether your March application is still pending, suspended, or rejected.**

**b) Owner outside the register.** Serbian case law, including Supreme Court of Cassation positions, as I recall them, not verified here, tends to favour a buyer who holds a valid title, has paid in full and is in possession, over the seller's later creditors. In enforcement proceedings a third party who holds a right that prevents enforcement can oppose it. Under general enforcement law this is done by an objection or a lawsuit to have enforcement declared inadmissible against that property. The tax enforcement procedure under the Law on Tax Procedure and Tax Administration (*Zakon o poreskom postupku i poreskoj administraciji*, ZPPPA) has comparable remedies. I am not certain of the exact article numbers and deadlines, so check them before relying on them.

**c) Good faith of the creditor.** The Tax Administration is a creditor that registers a lien. It is not a buyer that paid in reliance on the register. Courts have often weighed that difference in favour of the prior buyer who paid and took possession. This is an interpretive argument, not a guarantee, and outcomes vary by court.

## 3. What to do right now, in order of urgency

1. **Get the cadastre case number and status.** Ask the notary for the case number (*broj predmeta*) and the proof of submission to the RGZ (Republic Geodetic Authority) with its date. Check the status online on the RGZ portal. Find out whether the application is **pending, suspended (*prekinut*), or rejected**.
2. **If a decision was issued, check the appeal deadline immediately.** If the cadastre rejected the application or ordered you to supply documents, the deadlines are short (typically around 15 days, recalled, not verified). An appeal or a timely completion of the file is what **preserves your March priority**. Missing it is the single biggest risk in your situation.
3. **Get a fresh extract from the property register** (*list nepokretnosti*). See whether any lien, pre-notation or pending application by the Tax Administration already appears against the apartment.
4. **Fix the building-permit issue with the seller.** The seller is contractually obliged to deliver unencumbered title that can be registered. Send a written demand, ideally through a lawyer, to supply the missing documents by a deadline. Keep all correspondence.
5. **Consider a court filing to protect your position.** A lawyer may advise filing a **lawsuit to establish ownership** against the seller (*tužba za utvrđenje prava svojine*, or a claim to compel consent to registration). You would then register a **note of the dispute** (*zabeležba spora*) in the cadastre. Anyone who registers later, including the Tax Administration, is then bound by the outcome.
6. **If the Tax Administration moves against the apartment:** file the third-party objection or lawsuit straight away, with the contract, the bank transfer records and proof of possession (utility contracts, residence registration, handover record). Do not wait for a sale to be scheduled.
7. **Check your own property transfer tax.** Transfer tax (*porez na prenos apsolutnih prava*), 2.5% as I recall, not verified, is formally owed by the seller, but the buyer can be liable as guarantor if the seller does not pay. If your contract shifted the tax to you, or you claimed the first-home exemption, confirm it was filed and paid or approved. An indebted seller is exactly the scenario where this comes back to the buyer.
8. **Keep your evidence together:** the solemnized contract, SWIFT or bank confirmations of the EUR 145,000, the handover record and keys, and proof of residence and utilities. This is what proves you are the owner outside the register.

## 4. Key risks

- **Your March application is rejected and the rejection becomes final** (no appeal): you lose priority, and a later tax lien could rank ahead of you. You would then be left with a court fight and a damages claim against an insolvent seller.
- **The building-permit defect cannot be fixed** (for example, the unit is not legalized or not properly recorded): registration may be blocked indefinitely. That changes the strategy toward a court judgment and, if needed, rescission and damages.
- **Seller insolvency:** a money claim against a seller with tax debts is worth little. Protecting the **property itself** matters more than any damages claim.

## 1. Why the contract alone did not make you the owner

- **Law on the Fundamentals of Property Relations** (*Zakon o osnovama svojinskopravnih odnosa*, ZOSPO), Art. 33: when ownership of real estate is acquired through a legal transaction, it is acquired **by registration in the public register** (*upisom u javnu knjigu*) or in another way set by law. (Wording recalled, not verified here. Check the current consolidated text.)
- **Law on Real Estate Transactions** (*Zakon o prometu nepokretnosti*), Art. 4: a sale contract must be **solemnized by a notary** to be valid. This makes your contract valid. It gives you the legal basis for ownership (*pravni osnov*, the "title"). It does not transfer ownership.
- **Law on the Procedure of Registration in the Real Estate and Utilities Cadastre** (*Zakon o postupku upisa u katastar nepokretnosti i vodova*, 2018): registration of ownership is **constitutive**. It is what creates the right, not just a record of it. The notary must submit the contract to the cadastre electronically, ex officio.

So: the contract is valid, you paid, and you have possession. Until the cadastre registers you, the seller is still the **registered owner**, and third parties can rely on the register.

## 2. Why you are probably still in a good position

**a) Priority from the filing date.** Serbian cadastre law follows the **priority principle** (*načelo prvenstva*). As I recall it, the rank of a registration is set by the **moment the cadastre received the request**, not the date of the decision. If the notary actually filed your request in March, then a tax lien requested later should rank **behind** your ownership registration, provided your request is eventually granted. While a request is pending, the cadastre normally marks it on the property sheet (*list nepokretnosti*).

The key risk: if your request was **rejected** (*odbijen*), you can lose that priority unless the rejection is appealed in time. A request that was only suspended or sent back for missing documents may keep it. Find out which of these happened (see step 1 below).

**b) Case law on off-register owners.** Serbian courts, including the former Supreme Court of Cassation, have repeatedly held that a buyer who has a valid contract, **paid the full price and took possession** has a stronger right than the registered owner's creditors. On that basis the buyer can have enforcement against the property declared **inadmissible** (*nedopušteno*). I am recalling this line of cases, not citing a specific decision. Have a lawyer pull the current rulings.

**c) The argument against you.** The counter-argument is the **principle of reliance on the cadastre** (*načelo pouzdanja*): whoever relies in good faith on the register is protected. The Tax Administration could argue that it registered a lien against the registered owner in good faith. Courts and legal writers are split on whether an **enforcing creditor** gets the same protection as a good-faith buyer. Many say it does not, because the creditor gave no value in reliance on the register. This is contested, so don't count on winning it in litigation if you can avoid needing to.

## 3. A separate risk: the transfer tax on your own purchase

Under the **Law on Property Taxes** (*Zakon o porezima na imovinu*), the **seller** pays the absolute-rights transfer tax (2.5% on resale real estate). As I recall, the **buyer is a jointly liable guarantor** (*jemac*) for that tax. If the seller's debt includes the transfer tax on *your* sale (about 3,600 EUR on 145,000 EUR), the Tax Administration could come after you directly. Check this. (Recalled, not verified. It also applies only if the sale was not subject to VAT, which would be the case if the seller were a developer selling a new-build unit for the first time.)
