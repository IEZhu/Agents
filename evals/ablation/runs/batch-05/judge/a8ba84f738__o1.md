## Answer A
**Bottom line:** For an employee who lives and works in California, California law is very likely to control. California has statutes written for exactly this situation: an out-of-state employer using a non-California choice-of-law clause and forum clause to enforce a non-compete. HR's claim that the non-compete "is valid under Texas law" may be true on its own terms. But whether Texas law applies at all is the real question, and California's statutes answer it strongly in your favor. The main practical risk is procedural: the company might sue first in Travis County, and a Texas court won't necessarily defer to California's statutes. ## 1. California statutes (the ones that matter most for you)

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600: the core rule**
- Every contract that restrains anyone from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business is void, except under the narrow statutory exceptions (sale of a business, dissolution of a partnership or LLC, §§ 16601–16602.5). None of these fits an employee non-compete.
- AB 1076 (effective Jan. 1, 2024) added language saying the section is to be read broadly and codifying *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen* (see below). It voids non-competes "no matter how narrowly tailored." (Recalled; confirm the exact wording.)

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1: notice requirement (AB 1076)**
- Employers had to send current employees, and former employees employed after Jan. 1, 2022, individualized written notice by **Feb. 14, 2024** that their non-compete clause is void.
- Failing to send the notice counts as unfair competition. (Recalled; confirm the date and scope.)
- **Question for your lawyer:** Did you ever receive such a notice? If not, that fact could help you.

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5: the extraterritorial provision (SB 699, effective Jan. 1, 2024)**
This is probably the most directly relevant statute. As I recall its substance:
- A contract void under this chapter is unenforceable **regardless of where and when it was signed**.
- It is unenforceable **regardless of whether the contract was signed and employment was maintained outside California**.
- An employer may not attempt to enforce a void contract.
- An employer may not enter into one with an employee or prospective employee.
- An employee, former employee, or prospective employee may sue for **injunctive relief, actual damages, or both**, and a prevailing employee **recovers reasonable attorney's fees and costs**.
- HR's threat to sue may itself count as an "attempt to enforce." Ask your lawyer whether it gives you a claim of your own.

**Cal. Labor Code § 925: choice-of-law and forum clauses**
This statute targets exactly your contract structure:
- An employer may not require an employee who **primarily resides and works in California**, as a condition of employment, to agree to a provision that:
  - requires the employee to litigate a California-arising claim outside California, or
  - deprives the employee of the substantive protection of California law.
- Such a provision is **voidable by the employee**. If it's voided, the matter is decided in California under California law.
- The employee can get injunctive relief and **attorney's fees**.
- It applies to contracts entered into, modified, or extended on or after **Jan. 1, 2017**, so your 2023 agreement is covered.
- **Key exception (§ 925(e)):** it does not apply if you were **individually represented by counsel** when negotiating the choice-of-law and forum terms. Tell your lawyer whether you had your own attorney in 2023. Most employees don't.
- **Key fact:** did you primarily live and work in California *when you signed*, or did you move to San Diego later? § 925 focuses on the employee who "primarily resides and works in California." § 16600.5 is broader and applies regardless of where you signed. Bring your residence history.

**Related California law to be aware of**
- **Cal. Civ. Code § 3426 et seq. (CUTSA)** and the federal **Defend Trade Secrets Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1836**: § 16600 voids the non-compete but **not** your confidentiality and trade-secret obligations. Taking files, code, or customer lists is the most common way departing employees turn a weak case against them into a strong one.
- **Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 (UCL)**: the enforcement route for § 16600.1 violations.

## 2. Texas statutes (what HR is relying on)

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §§ 15.50–15.52 (Covenants Not to Compete Act)**
- **§ 15.50(a):** a non-compete is enforceable if it is "ancillary to or part of an otherwise enforceable agreement at the time the agreement is made." It must also contain **reasonable limitations as to time, geographical area, and scope of activity** that do not impose a greater restraint than necessary to protect goodwill or other business interests.
  - Texas courts typically find the "otherwise enforceable agreement" requirement met where the employer promised and provided confidential information or specialized training (*Alex Sheshunoff Mgmt. Servs. v. Johnson*, 209 S.W.3d 644 (Tex. 2006); *Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011); both recalled, verify).
- **§ 15.51(c):** if the limits are unreasonable, the court **must reform** the covenant to a reasonable scope rather than void it. It may not award damages for conduct before reformation.
  - A 12-month, **all-of-North-America, any-competitor** restriction on a software engineer is broad. Even a Texas court may well narrow it. Don't treat "valid under Texas law" as all-or-nothing.
- **§ 15.52:** these procedures preempt other Texas law on non-compete enforcement.

**How Texas treats choice of law:** Texas follows **Restatement (Second) Conflict of Laws § 187**. A contractual choice of law is not applied if another state has a **materially greater interest** and the chosen law would **contravene that state's fundamental policy**. The Texas Supreme Court applied this test *against* a choice-of-law clause in a non-compete case in *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990). (Recalled, verify.) In that case the facts ran the other way: a Florida clause was refused in favor of Texas's policy. The same framework is the best argument that a Texas court should apply California's policy to you.

**Federal level:** the FTC's 2024 nationwide non-compete ban was set aside in *Ryan LLC v. FTC* (N.D. Tex. 2024). As I recall, the FTC later dropped its appeal. Don't count on federal law helping you.

- **(b)** This subsection was added by AB 1076 and took effect January 1, 2024. It says § 16600 is to be read broadly, following *Edwards v. The exceptions cover sale of a business and dissolution of a partnership or LLC, and none of them fits your case. (Paraphrased, not verified.)

### Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1: the notice requirement (AB 1076)
- It is unlawful to include a non-compete in an employment contract, or to require an employee to sign one, unless an exception applies.
- Employers had to send written notice **by February 14, 2024** to current employees, and to former employees employed after January 1, 2022, saying the clause is void. (Recalled; confirm the date and wording.)
- A violation counts as an act of **unfair competition**, meaning a violation of Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.
- **Relevance to you:** you joined in 2023, so you're in the covered group. Ask yourself whether you ever received that notice. If you didn't, that's a separate violation to raise with your lawyer.

### Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5: the out-of-state contract rule (SB 699, effective January 1, 2024)
This one answers HR most directly. My recollection of its structure, not verified:
- **(a)** A contract that is void under this chapter is unenforceable "regardless of where and when the contract was signed."
- **(b)** An employer or former employer "shall not attempt to enforce" a void contract, "regardless of whether the contract was signed and the employment was maintained outside of California."
- **(c)** An employer shall not enter into a contract with an employee or prospective employee that contains a void provision.
- **(d)** Entering into or attempting to enforce such a contract is a **civil violation**.
- **(e)** The employee has a **private right of action** for injunctive relief and/or actual damages, and **is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees and costs**.
- **Relevance to you:** a lawsuit in Texas to enforce the clause is arguably itself an "attempt to enforce," which gives you your own claim, including fees.

### Labor Code § 925: choice-of-law and forum clauses
Recalled wording; verify it:
- **(a)** An employer "shall not require an employee who primarily resides and works in California, as a condition of employment, to agree to a provision that would do either of the following: (1) Require the employee to adjudicate outside of California a claim arising in California. (2) Deprive the employee of the substantive protection of California law with respect to a controversy arising in California."
- **(b)** Such a provision is **voidable by the employee**. If you void it, the matter is decided in California under California law.
- **(c)** You may recover attorney's fees in addition to other remedies.
- **(e)** **Exception:** the section doesn't apply if you were **individually represented by a lawyer** in negotiating the agreement, and that lawyer designated the venue or governing law. **Your lawyer will ask about this.** Did you have your own counsel when you signed in 2023?
- **(f)** The section applies to contracts entered into, modified, or extended on or after **January 1, 2017**. Your 2023 agreement qualifies.

## 3. How the choice-of-law fight actually works

- **A California court** will very likely apply §§ 16600/16600.5 and Labor Code § 925, and refuse the Texas clauses.
- **A Texas court** would apply **Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187**. Under § 187(2)(b), the chosen law is disregarded if another state has a "materially greater interest" and applying the chosen law would violate that state's "fundamental policy."
- **The Texas Supreme Court case on this** is *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990) (recalled; verify). There the court **refused to apply a Florida choice-of-law clause** to a non-compete and applied Texas law, because Texas had the materially greater interest and treated non-compete law as fundamental policy. The facts run the other way from yours, but the reasoning helps you: the state where the employee lives and works (California) has the greater interest, and California has written its policy into statute as expressly "fundamental" and extraterritorial. Expect your employer to argue that a Texas court shouldn't let California's policy override Texas's own. This is a live dispute, not a sure thing.
- **Forum clause in federal court:** if the case lands in federal court, *Atlantic Marine Constr. Co. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013) (recalled) makes forum-selection clauses hard to escape. The main counterargument is Labor Code § 925, and courts have split on how much weight it gets.
- **Timing:** this is why lawyers in your situation often consider **filing first in California**, for example a declaratory-relief claim or a § 16600.5 claim, before starting the new job. Ask your lawyer about this directly.
