## Latest user message
I'm giving a 10-minute talk to our exec team next Thursday asking for about $400k for year one of a proper data platform. Context: our numbers live in roughly 140 spreadsheets across sales, ops and finance, and closing the quarterly report takes finance about three weeks of reconciling them by hand. Here's my opening line, can you make it punchier and more memorable? I want them hooked from the first sentence and I'd like a couple of options.

"Our data is an untapped goldmine, and this platform is the bridge that will finally unlock its full potential and fuel our next wave of growth."

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Points out that the current line combines several images that do not fit together (e.g. goldmine, bridge, unlock, fuel, wave) and/or relies on stock business phrases, and says this weakens it
2. Each proposed opening uses at most one image or none, and none relies on 'goldmine', 'unlock potential', 'fuel growth' or similar stock phrases
3. At least one proposed opening is built on the concrete facts the user gave (about 140 spreadsheets, about three weeks of manual reconciliation for the quarterly report) rather than on imagery
4. The options are short enough to say aloud at the start of a talk and keep the core message for execs: the data exists but is fragmented and slow to use, and fixing it needs investment

## Answer A
Your current line won't hook anyone, and making it punchier won't fix that. It packs four metaphors (goldmine, bridge, unlock, fuel) into one sentence and has no facts in it. Every exec in that room has heard "our data is an untapped goldmine" from a vendor at least once, and they tune out on those words. It also opens with the solution ("this platform") before they feel any problem, so from the first sentence it sounds like a pitch.

You already have a better hook than the goldmine: **140 spreadsheets and three weeks of manual reconciliation every quarter.** That's specific, it's theirs, and they can check it. Open with that.

## Option 1: The two numbers (my pick)
> "140. That's how many spreadsheets our quarterly report depends on. Three weeks. That's how long finance spends stitching them together by hand, every single quarter."

Pause for a second or two after each number. It sounds bare on paper, but it works well out loud: two numbers they can't unhear, and no adjectives.

## Option 2: The question
> "Quick question: which of our 140 spreadsheets has the right revenue number? Finance spends three weeks every quarter finding out."

It opens a curiosity gap and quietly hints at a trust problem, not just an efficiency one. Only use it if the spreadsheets really do disagree. Ask finance before Thursday.

## Option 3: The reframe
> "Every quarter, our best finance people take three weeks off from finance to do copy-and-paste."

This one hits hardest emotionally, because it presents the cost as wasted talent and not only wasted hours. The risk is that the CFO hears it as criticism of their team, so check with finance first or say it with them in the room.

## Option 4: The money (if you can get one number)
> "We already spend on a data platform. It's just made of people, spreadsheets, and three weeks every quarter."

Follow it straight away with the loaded cost of those three weeks (finance headcount × time × fully loaded rate). You're asking for $400k, so let them compare that to what the manual process already costs. I don't have that figure, and you shouldn't guess it. Work it out with finance. If it turns out small next to $400k, drop this option, because it will backfire.

## Two things to lock in around the hook
- **Close the loop at the end.** Whichever line you pick, come back to it in your call-to-action. For example: "Approve $400k for year one, and next year's Q1 close takes days, not three weeks." Only promise a timeline your plan can actually deliver.
- **Don't follow the hook with an agenda slide.** You get the most attention in the first 90 seconds of a 10-minute talk, so don't use that time on "Today I'll cover…"

**How to fail:** want the CFO checking email by sentence two? Open with "goldmine," go straight to an architecture diagram, and mention the $400k for the first time on slide 11.


## Answer B
Your current line has one problem: any company could say it about anything. "Goldmine", "bridge", "unlock" and "fuel" are four metaphors in one sentence, and they don't fit together (you don't unlock a goldmine with a bridge). It also leaves out your two strongest assets, which are **140 spreadsheets** and **three weeks**. Execs forget adjectives. They remember numbers they can picture.

Here are three options, each built on your real facts.

**Option 1: The delay (my pick)**
> "Three weeks. That's how long it takes us to find out how last quarter went."

Short, concrete, and a little uncomfortable. It turns a back-office annoyance into a leadership problem: *you* are steering on stale numbers. Say "Three weeks," pause for two seconds, then finish the sentence.

**Option 2: The absurd image**
> "Every quarter, our finance team spends three weeks doing one thing: making 140 spreadsheets agree with each other."

This works because the picture is slightly ridiculous, and ridiculous sticks. It's better if you're pitching to a CFO who already feels this pain.

**Option 3: Lead with the ask**
> "I'm asking for $400k. Here's what we're paying right now instead: three weeks of finance time, every quarter, reconciling 140 spreadsheets by hand."

Executives often want the bottom line first, and this one names the price and reframes it as a cost you're already paying. It's the riskiest of the three: if the room reacts badly to the number, you've lost them in the first sentence. It's also the strongest if you can put a dollar figure on those roughly 12 weeks a year of finance time. Only use a figure you can defend, because someone will ask how you got it.

**Two things to check before Thursday:**
- **Make sure finance will back the "three weeks" figure.** If it's really "two to three weeks, depending", someone in the room will correct you, and your hook turns into an argument.
- **The hook creates tension, and it needs a payoff.** Within about 60 seconds, say the one-sentence ask, something like: *"I'm asking you to approve $400k to close the quarter in days, not weeks."* In a 10-minute talk, the opening and the decision you're asking for should be almost the same sentence.

**How to fail:** open with "goldmine", move to a slide of 140 spreadsheet icons, and mention the $400k for the first time at minute nine. That way the CFO spends eight minutes guessing the number, and guesses higher.
