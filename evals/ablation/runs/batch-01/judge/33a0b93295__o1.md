## Answer A
Don't print them one at a time. Get your own measurements first, then check each candidate's bore in the slicer. That cuts 40 downloads down to one or two short test prints. In practice, a parametric knob built from your measurements often ends up being the answer anyway.

## 1. Measure the shaft first

Everything else depends on these numbers, so use calipers, not a ruler:

- **Shaft diameter** (round part)
- **Across-the-flat dimension** (from the flat face to the opposite round side). This is the value that usually differs between "D-shaft" knobs.
- **Shaft length / engagement depth**, and how far the knob has to sit from the panel
- **Retention**: plain press-fit, split/knurled shaft, set screw, or a spring clip inside the old knob
- Measure the **cracked original's** bore, outer diameter, skirt and pointer position if you still have the pieces.

I don't know the A701's exact shaft specs, and I won't guess them. Your caliper reading is the only number that counts here.

## 2. Filter the 40 results without printing

- **Download the STLs and measure the bore in the slicer.** PrusaSlicer has a measure tool, and Meshmixer or FreeCAD work too. Opening a file and checking the D-hole takes about a minute. Throw out anything more than about 0.3 mm off your measurement.
- **Skip files that list no dimensions or model number.** A title like "kenwood knob" covers dozens of different mixers, food processors and hi-fi units. That may be why your first print was off: it might not be a Chef knob at all.
- **Rank by makes and photos, not downloads.** Download count says how many people clicked, not whether it fits. Zero makes and zero comments means the file is untested. Your top result was exactly that.
- **Search more specifically, and on more than one site:** "Kenwood Chef A701 knob", "A701 speed control", "Kenwood Chef speed knob" on Printables, Thangs, Cults3D and MakerWorld, plus Yeggi to search them all at once. Older Chef models (A701, A701A, A901 and so on) may or may not share the same knob. I haven't checked that, so compare dimensions, not model names.

## 3. Test-fit the bore only

Once a file passes the measurement check, don't print the whole knob. Use the slicer's cut tool to keep just the bottom 5–8 mm of the hub, which prints in a few minutes. Better still, print a small **D-hole test coupon** with 3–4 bores in 0.1 mm steps around your measured size. On an Ender 3 V2, small holes usually print a bit undersized, often about 0.1–0.2 mm, depending on your extrusion and slicer settings. A coupon tells you your printer's real offset once, so every later check is quicker.

## 4. Often the quickest route: a parametric knob

Search for "parametric knob", "knob customizer" or "OpenSCAD knob D-shaft". Several of these let you type in the shaft diameter, the flat depth and the outer shape. Enter your caliper numbers plus the offset from your coupon, and you're likely done in one or two prints. If you want it to look like the original, a simple knob is also a fairly easy first model in Fusion or FreeCAD.

## Print notes for the final part

- **Material:** The knob sits near the motor, and it gets twisted and wiped with wet hands. **PETG** will probably hold up better than PLA there. Treat that as a judgment call, not a measured fact.
- **Orientation:** Print it face-down so the bore is vertical. That gives you the most accurate D-profile.
- **Walls/infill:** Use 4 perimeters around the bore and 30–40% infill. The thin wall next to the flat is where knobs usually crack.
- If it's a press-fit, aim for a snug fit and add a drop of glue, rather than forcing a tight bore that splits.

## Also check the official part

Genuine Kenwood Chef spares are sometimes still sold by appliance-parts suppliers, and they're also found on eBay. Search for the A701 speed control knob along with the part number, if you can find it in the service/parts list. I haven't checked whether it's still available. If it costs a few euros, that may beat an afternoon of test prints.


## Answer B
Printing all 40 would waste a lot of filament, and there's a good chance none of them fit anyway. Most "Kenwood knob" uploads are made for a different model, and people often don't say which one. A better approach is to measure your shaft first, then check each model's bore before you print anything.

## 1. Measure your shaft (this is the most important step)

Use calipers on the mixer's shaft, not on the broken knob:

- **Round diameter** across the curved sides
- **Across-the-flat**: from the flat face to the opposite curved side. This is the number that decides whether a D-bore fits.
- **Usable shaft length**, and how far the knob sits down onto it
- **Anything unusual**: knurling, a second flat, a groove, a set-screw hole, or a metal insert in the old knob

If you have the pieces of the cracked knob, measure its bore and its outside shape too (diameter, height, skirt, pointer position). That tells you what "fits" and what "looks right" mean for your mixer.

I don't have confirmed shaft dimensions for the A701, so I won't guess them. Use your caliper numbers, not a figure you find online.

## 2. Search smarter, not wider

- **Search by model number**: "A701", "Chef A701", "Kenwood Chef speed knob", "Kenwood Chef control knob". Knobs labelled only "Kenwood" are often for Chefettes, food processors or later Chef models.
- **Check several sites**: Printables, Thangs (its natural-language search copes well with things like "Kenwood Chef A701 speed control knob"), Cults3D, Maker.World, and Yeggi to search all of them at once.
- **On Thingiverse, sort by Makes, not Downloads.** Downloads mostly show how old and visible a model is. A top result with no makes, comments or photos is untested, and you've already found out what that costs.
- **Keep only models whose description names your model or lists shaft dimensions.** On a spare part, a vague description is a red flag.

## 3. Check the bore on screen before printing

For each model that passes step 2:

1. Download the STL and open it in your slicer.
2. Measure the hole. PrusaSlicer has a measure tool. In Cura, you can cut a thin slice or check in a free viewer or CAD tool.
3. Compare the bore to your caliper numbers. If it's off by more than a few tenths of a millimetre in the wrong direction, drop it.

This usually cuts 40 candidates down to a handful, or to none, which is also worth knowing.

## 4. Test only the hub

Don't print the whole knob to test fit. Use the slicer's cut tool to keep just the bottom 4–6 mm around the bore. That takes about 10 minutes and a gram or two of filament. Push it onto the shaft:

- **Too tight or won't go on**: FDM holes normally come out a little undersized. Scale the XY a touch, or use your slicer's hole compensation / horizontal expansion setting.
- **Loose or wobbly**: go the other way, or pick another model.
- **Good fit**: print the full knob.

## 5. Often the fastest route: make your own bore

If no model fits well, don't keep searching. Pick the model whose outside you like best and replace its hole:

- In **Tinkercad** or **Fusion**, subtract the existing bore and cut a new D-hole from your measurements, plus about 0.1–0.2 mm clearance for a snug push fit. That's a common starting point for PLA/PETG on a stock 0.4 mm nozzle, so check it on your machine.
- Or use a **parametric OpenSCAD knob** (search "parametric knob D shaft" on Printables or Thingiverse) and type in your numbers.
- Before committing, print a small **test coupon** with 3–4 D-holes a tenth of a millimetre apart. Once you know which size fits, you won't need to guess again.

## 6. Material note

A knob on a mixer gets handled and twisted, and it sits near a motor that can get warm during long mixing. PLA can creep over time, so the D-hole slowly loosens. For the final print, **PETG** is probably the better choice, with 4 walls and 40%+ infill around the hub so the flat doesn't strip.

## One more option

Before spending more time, search for "Kenwood A701 speed control knob" as a spare part. Vintage Kenwood Chef parts are still sold secondhand and by specialist parts sellers (I haven't checked current availability). An original part might cost about as much as your time testing prints, and you could also measure it to model a perfect copy.

**Short version:** measure the shaft, filter by model number and makes, check each bore in the slicer, test-print only the hub, and if nothing matches, put your own D-hole into a knob shape you like.
