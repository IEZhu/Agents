## Answer A
Fix one thing before you tighten anything else: **the $2.4M is not annual revenue.** It's 12,000 × $200, the increase in *lifetime* value. A CFO will likely spot this, and it's the number your whole argument rests on.

## What the numbers support

- **LTV math is right.** $40 / 5% = $800 and $40 / 4% = $1,000, a 25% increase (revenue LTV, before gross margin).
- **First-year revenue impact is roughly $270k, not $2.4M.** I assumed the 12,000-customer base with no new customers, churn held at 4% vs 5%, and $40 ARPU:
  - Revenue kept = Σ (t = 1..12) of $40 × 12,000 × (0.96^t − 0.95^t) ≈ $480k × 0.56 ≈ **$270k**
  - That comes out about equal to the CSM cost. So the hires roughly break even in year one. They don't pay for themselves 9× over.
- **"$270k fully loaded per year" is ambiguous.** Does it mean $270k in total or $270k per hire? At $270k each, the cost is $810k and the case falls apart. Spell it out.

## Weak spots the CFO may probe

1. **One month of data.** Comparing March alone to a Jan–Feb average can't separate a real effect from normal month-to-month swings or seasonality. January churn is often high because of annual renewals, for example. Show at least 6–12 months of history so the drop can be judged against normal variance.
2. **Attribution.** Guided onboarding mostly reaches *new* customers, yet churn fell across the *whole* base. Something else may be driving part of the drop. Check churn for the March cohort against older cohorts.
3. **The link to the hires.** The churn drop happened *without* the new CSMs. The paragraph needs to say why headcount is required to sustain or scale it.

## Answer B
Before you make it punchier, fix one number. A CFO will likely catch it, and it carries the whole argument.

**The problem: $2.4M is lifetime value, not annual revenue.** 12,000 customers × $200 LTV uplift = $2.4M, but that $200 builds up over each customer's roughly 25-month life, and it's undiscounted revenue, not margin. Putting it next to a $270k *annual* cost compares two different time frames.

**What the annual number looks like.** I took the existing 12,000 customers, assumed no new sign-ups, and ran them for 12 months at 5% vs 4% monthly churn:
- Revenue at 5% churn: ~$4.41M
- Revenue at 4% churn: ~$4.65M
- **Difference: ~$236k in year one.** That's slightly *below* the $270k cost. It grows in later years, and new customers who also churn less add to it.

So the case still holds up, but it's "pays back within about a year and keeps paying," not "more than covers it 9x over."

**Two other things a CFO may push on:**
1. **Causation.** One month after launch vs. a two-month baseline shows a link, not proof. Jan–Feb churn may run high for seasonal reasons, like annual renewals and budget resets. Say the result is early, and give a date when you'll confirm it.
2. **"$270k" is ambiguous.** Is it the total for 3 hires (~$90k each) or per hire? If it's per hire, the total is $810k and the math changes a lot. Say which one.

The paragraph also never says *why* CSMs keep the churn gain going. One clause on that would help.

**Tightened version:**

> Since we launched guided onboarding on March 1, monthly logo churn has dropped from 5.0% (Jan–Feb average) to 4.0%. If that holds, customer LTV rises 25%, from $800 to $1,000 at $40 ARPU, which is about $2.4M in added lifetime revenue across our 12,000 customers. On the current base alone, that's roughly $240k of extra revenue in the next 12 months, close to the $270k fully loaded annual cost of the 3 proposed CSM hires, and the return grows each year after. [One line on how the CSMs sustain or extend onboarding.] We'll confirm the trend with April–May data.

My $236k is a simple closed-cohort model. It leaves out new customers, expansion revenue and discounting. If you have a finance model, run the numbers through it before the meeting.
