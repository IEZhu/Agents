**Bottom line:** Below are the commonly cited reference values as a starting draft. I'd push back on hardcoding them without a pharmacist signing off, though. This table would be the single most safety-critical artifact in the whole system: it's the limit the model "must never exceed". If one number in it is wrong, the guardrail itself gives the bad advice at 200k-MAU scale. Checking a one-page table takes a pharmacist about an hour, far less than the labelling they're already doing, and it should come before any more labelling.

## Main interactions and cautions (to be verified)

**Ibuprofen**
- **Anticoagulants** (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban and similar DOACs) and **antiplatelets** (clopidogrel, low-dose aspirin): higher bleeding risk. Ibuprofen may also reduce aspirin's cardioprotective effect.
- **SSRIs/SNRIs and corticosteroids:** higher GI bleeding risk.
- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs + diuretics:** acute kidney injury risk (the "triple whammy").
- **Lithium, methotrexate:** raised levels.
- **Other NSAIDs**, including hidden ones in combination products: no stacking.
- **Alcohol:** adds to GI bleeding risk.
- **Refer instead of dosing:** history of stomach ulcer or bleed, kidney disease, heart failure, NSAID-sensitive asthma, pregnancy (especially later pregnancy), older adults on any of the drugs above.

**Paracetamol**
- **Duplicate paracetamol** in cold/flu remedies and combination painkillers. This is probably the most common real-world route to accidental overdose, so the bot has to ask about it.
- **Chronic heavy alcohol use or liver disease:** lower max, or refer.
- **Warfarin:** regular use can raise INR, so regular use needs a pharmacist.
- **Enzyme-inducing drugs** (e.g. carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin): possibly higher liver risk. Treat as refer.
- **Any suspected overdose**, even without symptoms: emergency path. Toxicity can be delayed.

## How I'd implement it

1. **Key the table by SKU, not by molecule.** The dangerous error isn't "15 mg/kg". It's converting that into **mL of a specific syrup strength** (e.g. 120 mg/5 mL vs 250 mg/5 mL). Build each row from your own leaflet: product ID, strength, age/weight band, dose in units (tablets/mL), interval, max doses per 24 h. Then every answer cites the same leaflet your retrieval already returns.
2. **Prefer the leaflet's age bands over computing weight doses.** OTC labels usually dose children by age. Having the LLM calculate mg/kg → mL on the fly is the highest-risk path. If you want weight-based dosing at all, do it with deterministic code and have a pharmacist approve the rounding rules.
3. **Enforce the table as a validator on output, not just a prompt instruction.** Parse the structured answer (drug, dose, unit, frequency). Reject or replace it if it goes over the limit, or if the model states a number that isn't in the table.
4. **Make interactions a mandatory pre-check.** Before giving any dose, the bot asks about current medicines, alcohol, pregnancy, and kidney/liver/stomach history. Any match goes to "see pharmacist", which reuses your existing urgency field.
5. **Track cumulative intake.** If the user mentions another product, the bot adds up the paracetamol/NSAID content, or refers them.
6. **Add dosing cases to the eval set:** borderline weights, the wrong syrup strength, a paracetamol-containing cold remedy plus paracetamol tablets, a user on warfarin, "I've already taken 6 today".

## Minimal ask for the pharmacists

Don't pull them off labelling for long. Send one table (SKU × band × dose × max) and one interaction/refer list, and ask for a yes/no plus corrections, with sign-off recorded and versioned. Also check whether your pharmacy regulator expects a named pharmacist to be accountable for the bot's dosing content. I'd expect it does, but I'm not certain for your jurisdiction. Either way, that sign-off is worth having before this ships.
