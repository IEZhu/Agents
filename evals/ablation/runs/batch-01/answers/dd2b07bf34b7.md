I can write you a takeaway, but not that one. The two reports don't support "it nets out to neutral, so our policy is fine," and leadership could fairly poke holes in it.

**Why that framing doesn't hold:**

- **Both sources have a stake in the answer.** A collaboration-software vendor makes money when people work remotely. A commercial real-estate consultancy makes money when people fill offices. Each report found what its sponsor would want. That makes both weak evidence, and averaging two biased numbers doesn't cancel the bias. It just hides it.
- **They probably aren't measuring the same thing.** "Remote employees" (possibly including hybrid) and "fully remote teams" are different groups. Productivity is also defined very differently from study to study: self-reports, output per hour, calls handled, manager ratings. +13% and −10–20% aren't points on one scale you can average.
- **"Neutral" doesn't mean "our policy is fine."** Even if the average impact were zero, that says nothing about how your policy works for your teams and your kinds of work.

For context, independent academic research, which I haven't checked this turn, generally finds the outcome depends on the setup. Fully remote arrangements tend to show more mixed results. Hybrid setups often show little or no productivity loss and better retention. Confirm that before you cite it as a finding.

**A version you can defend (4 sentences):**

> Research on remote work and productivity is genuinely mixed: recent reports we reviewed range from a 13% gain to a 10–20% loss, but both come from firms with a commercial interest in the outcome, so neither should be taken at face value. Independent studies suggest the effect depends heavily on the arrangement (fully remote vs. hybrid), the type of work, and how productivity is measured. The honest takeaway is that there is no universal answer, and headline numbers from interested parties are not a sound basis for policy. The better guide for us is our own evidence: team output, retention, and employee feedback under our current approach, which we will continue to monitor.

If you have data showing the current policy is working, like stable output or low attrition, swap it into the last sentence. That supports "our policy is fine" much better than either report does.
