## Answer A
No, you're not being an idiot. Your gut is picking up something real: you describe the work as boring, and at 34 that costs you more each year you stay. But the offer as written is weak in two places. One is timing, because the baby comes before your cliff. The other is that the case for the equity rests on a promise ("Series C soon") that you haven't checked. Neither is a reason to say no yet. Both are reasons to get answers before Friday.

## What you're actually deciding

This isn't "startup vs. boring job." It's closer to this: **do you give up about $35-45k/year in cash, and take on job risk around a birth, in exchange for leadership experience, exciting work and a lottery ticket?** Framed that way, the answer depends on facts you can get this week.

**The options:** 0.15% sounds small, and at a ~70-person Series B it's in a normal range for a senior IC or lead, not generous. I don't know their valuation. As a pure illustration, if the post-money were around $200M, 0.15% would be about $300k on paper over 4 years. From that you'd subtract the strike price, dilution from Series C and later rounds (often 15-25% per round), and liquidation preferences, which pay investors back first. Most Series B startups don't produce a meaningful payout for employees. **Value the options at close to zero in your budget, and treat any payout as upside.** If the move only works because the equity pays off, it doesn't work.

## Steel-man: why taking it is right

- Leading a team of 4 on work you care about is a real step up in your career. "Senior data engineer at an insurer" and "led data engineering at a climate-tech company" lead to different next jobs.
- Staying bored for years has a cost. It shows up later as stagnation and makes your next job search harder.
- Senior data engineers are employable. If the startup dies in 18 months, you'll probably land somewhere, likely with a better title than you have now. For you, this decision is more **reversible** than it feels.

## Steel-man: why declining is right

- **Your baby is due about 4 months after you'd start, well inside the 1-year cliff.** If things go badly, you could be laid off with a newborn and zero vested equity.
- **Parental leave:** as I recall, US FMLA job protection requires 12 months of employment at the company (plus other conditions). You'd probably have no federal leave protection when the baby arrives. Company policy and state paid-leave law may cover you, but check that; don't assume it.
- **Health insurance mid-pregnancy:** a new plan can mean a reset deductible and out-of-pocket max right before delivery. Your OB or hospital might also be out of network. That could cost several thousand dollars on its own.
- **The commute with a newborn:** 5 hours a week in the car, during the months when sleep and time at home matter most, while you're also learning to manage a team.
- **"Series C soon":** they raised 14 months ago. Startups usually raise with about 18-24 months of runway, so if that holds here, they're probably getting close to needing money. Everyone at a startup says the next round is coming. That's not evidence; it's what people there are paid to believe. I also can't verify current climate-tech funding conditions, but the market has been choppy.

## Pre-mortem: it's March 2027 and this went badly. Why?

1. The Series C stalled and there were layoffs. You were still inside your cliff and walked away with nothing. → **Preventable in part:** ask for real runway numbers now.
2. The baby arrived, leave was thin, and you came back to a new team that had drifted. → **Preventable:** negotiate leave terms in writing.
3. The commute plus a newborn wore you down, and your marriage and health took the hit. → **Preventable:** negotiate remote months at the start.
4. The delivery cost more than expected because of the insurance switch. → **Preventable:** check the plan and your network before signing.
5. Leading 4 people turned out to be a lot of management and not much of the exciting work. → **Partially preventable:** ask the team what their lead actually does all week.

Notice that almost every failure mode comes from **timing and unchecked promises**, not from the idea of joining a startup. That's good news, because timing and terms can be negotiated.

## Questions to ask before Friday (you're allowed to ask all of these)

1. **Runway:** "How many months of runway do you have at the current burn rate, and what happens if the C slips 6 months?" A vague answer tells you something too.
2. **Equity details:** the number of fully diluted shares, the strike price, the latest preferred price and valuation, and the exercise window after you leave. A 90-day window versus a longer one matters a lot if you leave before an exit.
3. **Parental leave:** what's the written policy, and does it apply to someone who joins 4 months before the birth?
4. **Benefits:** get the health plan documents and check your OB and hospital network and the deductible.
5. **401k match and bonus:** do they exist?

## A third option you might be missing: negotiate the timing

You have leverage. They picked you to lead a team. Ask for some of these:
- **Starting after the birth**, or fully remote for the first 3-6 months.
- **A higher base** (even $160k closes most of the gap) or **more equity** to make up for the lost bonus.
- **Leave in writing** that applies no matter your tenure.
- **A shorter cliff**, e.g. 6 months. It's not always granted, but it's a reasonable ask given the timing.

How they respond is data too. A company that won't work with you on leave for a birth 4 months out is showing you how it will treat you later.

## Biases to watch

- **Social proof / optimism:** the people telling you a C is coming have every reason to believe it.
- **Novelty vs. boredom:** your current job's biggest flaw is that it's boring, and that can make any exciting option look better than it is.
- **Loss aversion (the other direction):** don't turn down a real career move just because stability feels safer during the pregnancy, if the numbers actually work.

## Devil's advocate: the parts that worry me

**1. "They'll raise a Series C soon" is a sign of need as much as good news.** $40M raised 14 months ago with ~70 people is a rough guess, but at a typical fully loaded cost of $180–220k per person, payroll alone is around $13–15M a year. They're very likely in fundraising mode now because they have to be. That's normal. But the people telling you it's coming all hold equity and are recruiting you, so they're the most biased source you could ask. Climate tech funding has also been uneven. - **FMLA** (as I recall) requires 12 months of employment and 1,250 hours, so you likely **wouldn't have job-protected federal leave**. Check your state too, since some states have their own paid leave programs with different rules. That leaves the startup's own policy. Ask for it **in writing**.
- **Health insurance** changes mid-pregnancy: new deductible and out-of-pocket max, and you need to check whether your OB and hospital are in network. This one is easy to overlook and can get expensive.
- Being a new team lead with a newborn is when you'd most want to be proving yourself, and also when you'd have the least sleep.

**3. The worst realistic case.** The Series C slips, and there are layoffs around month 9–11. You're **before the 1-year cliff, so the equity goes to zero**. You're looking for a job with a 6-month-old at home, after giving up ~$35k in cash and a stable remote job. I'd call this unlikely, but it's not a stretch. Early-2020s startup layoffs followed this exact pattern.

**4. Commute plus newborn.** About 5 hours a week in the car, 3 days out of 5, when every hour at home counts. The fully remote setup you have now is probably worth more to you this year than any other year.

## The option you may be missing: negotiate or defer
