## The real decision

You're not choosing between a startup and a boring job. You're choosing whether to take **this offer, on these terms, about 4 months before your baby is born**. Keep those two apart. You could take a lot of different versions of this offer, and "not now" doesn't mean "never."

You'd be taking a cut of roughly **$30–40k a year in guaranteed cash**, before counting the match and commute costs. That happens in the same year your household costs go up and your wife may be on reduced or no income for a while.

The equity needs to cover that gap, and right now **0.15% tells you nothing without these numbers**:
- Total fully diluted shares, strike price, and the Series B preferred price or post-money valuation
- Liquidation preferences (1x non-participating is normal; anything above that eats into common)
- The post-termination exercise window (often 90 days, which could mean you'd have to pay to keep your options if you leave)

To show the scale, here's a made-up example: if the Series B post-money were $200M, 0.15% would be $300k on paper over 4 years. That's before dilution from a Series C and later rounds, and before preferences. It's also only worth anything if there's an exit. Treat startup options as a lottery ticket with better odds, not as salary. If they won't give you those numbers before Friday, that tells you something too.

## Devil's advocate: the parts that worry me

**1. "They'll raise a Series C soon" is a sign of need as much as good news.** $40M raised 14 months ago with ~70 people is a rough guess, but at a typical fully loaded cost of $180–220k per person, payroll alone is around $13–15M a year. They're very likely in fundraising mode now because they have to be. That's normal. But the people telling you it's coming all hold equity and are recruiting you, so they're the most biased source you could ask. Climate tech funding has also been uneven. The questions to ask: **How many months of runway at current burn? Is there a term sheet, or just conversations? What happens if the round slips 6 months?**

**2. The timing with the baby is the hardest part to undo.** Starting now puts you about 4 months into the job at the birth.
- **FMLA** (as I recall) requires 12 months of employment and 1,250 hours, so you likely **wouldn't have job-protected federal leave**. Check your state too, since some states have their own paid leave programs with different rules. That leaves the startup's own policy. Ask for it **in writing**.
- **Health insurance** changes mid-pregnancy: new deductible and out-of-pocket max, and you need to check whether your OB and hospital are in network. This one is easy to overlook and can get expensive.
- Being a new team lead with a newborn is when you'd most want to be proving yourself, and also when you'd have the least sleep.

**3. The worst realistic case.** The Series C slips, and there are layoffs around month 9–11. You're **before the 1-year cliff, so the equity goes to zero**. You're looking for a job with a 6-month-old at home, after giving up ~$35k in cash and a stable remote job. I'd call this unlikely, but it's not a stretch. Early-2020s startup layoffs followed this exact pattern.

**4. Commute plus newborn.** About 5 hours a week in the car, 3 days out of 5, when every hour at home counts. The fully remote setup you have now is probably worth more to you this year than any other year.

## Pre-mortem: it's a year later and you regret it. Why?
1. The round slipped and there were layoffs before your cliff. *You can partly reduce this by asking about runway and term sheet status.*
2. No real parental leave, so you went back to the office 2 weeks after the birth. *Preventable if you negotiate it now.*
3. The pay cut plus new baby costs strained your finances. *Run the household budget at $150k before Friday.*
4. Leading the team was harder than expected, the sleep deprivation hurt your performance, and your first review went badly. *Hard to prevent. Talk with your wife about it honestly.*
5. The equity turned out to be worth far less than you assumed because of preferences and dilution. *Ask for the numbers.*

Most of these can be prevented or at least checked **before you sign**. That's why I'd treat this week as a negotiation, not a yes or no.

## The option you may be missing: negotiate or defer

You have leverage. They want you to lead a team. Ask for some combination of:
- **Base up to $165k or more**, or a signing bonus to cover the gap
- **Paid parental leave in writing** (e.g. 12 weeks), even though you'd be new
- **Remote for your first ~6 months**, or at least until the baby is 3 months old
- **A start date after the birth** plus some leave (they may say no, but asking is free)
- **A longer exercise window** (e.g. 5–10 years) and the cap table numbers

How they respond tells you as much as the answers. A company that says "we're family-friendly" and then won't put parental leave in writing for a lead hire has told you how it will treat you when things get hard.

## Recommendation

**Don't accept as offered. Push hard on the terms, and take it only if the key pieces come back right.**

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The direction is solid, but it depends on facts I don't have: their real runway, your savings cushion, your wife's leave and income, and your state's leave rules.

Good climate-tech data roles will still be around, and you'd be looking with FMLA and a newborn behind you.
