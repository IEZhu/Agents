I can't confirm the key. Your student got all three right. (a) is TRUE, but (b) and (c) are both **FALSE**, and her margin note ("the salt one overflows, the pebble one goes down") is exactly the correct physics. She should get full credit.

Every case comes down to one idea: **a floating object pushes aside an amount of liquid that weighs exactly as much as the object does** (Archimedes). What changes from case to case is what happens to that weight after the ice melts.

---

### (a) Fresh water, pure ice: TRUE ✔

- Say the cube has a mass of 10 g. While it floats, it pushes aside 10 g of fresh water, which is 10 cm³.
- When it melts, it becomes 10 g of fresh water, which is also 10 cm³.
- The meltwater fills exactly the "hole" the cube was making. The level doesn't change.

### (b) Salt water, fresh ice: FALSE (the level rises, so a brim-full glass overflows)

- The 10 g cube still pushes aside 10 g of liquid, but now that liquid is **salt water**, which is denser (about 1.025 g/cm³ for seawater). 10 g of salt water takes up only about **9.76 cm³**.
- When it melts, the cube turns into 10 g of **fresh** water, which takes up **10 cm³**.
- The meltwater needs about 0.24 cm³ more room than the hole the cube made, so the level rises and a full glass spills over.

The "same principle as (a)" reasoning fails because the principle only gives "no change" when the meltwater has the same density as the liquid it's floating in. Here it doesn't.

(Ignoring mixing effects: once the fresh meltwater mixes in, the total volume changes slightly, but that effect is far too small to cancel the rise. Physics textbooks usually treat this as a standard "level rises" problem, and it's a known small factor in sea-level discussions about floating ice shelves.)

### (c) Fresh water, ice with a pebble: FALSE (the level drops)

This is the classic trick question, and the "pebble adds extra volume" reasoning is the trap.

- **While floating:** the ice and pebble together weigh, say, 10 g of ice plus 5 g of pebble. The cube pushes aside water equal to the **full 15 g**, which is 15 cm³. The pebble's weight is being carried by displaced water.
- **After melting:** the ice becomes 10 g of water (10 cm³), which fills its share exactly, as in (a). The pebble sinks to the bottom. Now it only pushes aside **its own volume**. A 5 g pebble with a density of about 2.5 g/cm³ (typical rock) takes up only **2 cm³**.
- **Net:** before, the pebble accounted for 5 cm³ of displaced water. After, it accounts for 2 cm³. The level **drops** by about 3 cm³ worth.

The rule of thumb: a dense object displaces more water while it's **floating** (by its weight) than while it's **sunk** (by its volume). The pebble's volume doesn't "add" anything new. It was already inside the cube, and its weight was already raising the water level more than its volume ever could.

---

### How to handle it tomorrow

I'd suggest owning it directly with her, and probably with the class, because it's a great teaching moment. She reasoned more carefully than the key did. For the rest of the class, a quick in-class fix could be:

1. Ask: "What does a floating object displace: its volume or its weight's worth of water?"
2. Then ask for each case: "After melting, does the meltwater (and pebble) take up more, less, or the same space as what was displaced?"

If you want to demo (c), the related "boat with a rock in a pool" version works the same way: throw the rock overboard and the pool level goes **down**. Students usually find that result surprising and memorable.

If you keep the items for future years, (b) and (c) make excellent questions as long as the key reads (a) T, (b) F, (c) F. You might also drop "exactly" from (b), since "the level rises" is the cleaner claim to test.
