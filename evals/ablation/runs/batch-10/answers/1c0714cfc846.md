## The one principle behind all three

A floating object displaces its own **weight** of the liquid it floats in (Archimedes). So each question comes down to this: is the volume the ice pushes aside while floating the same as the volume it takes up after melting?

## (a) Fresh water, pure ice: TRUE ✔

Take a 10 g cube. While floating, it displaces 10 g of fresh water, which is 10 cm³. When it melts it becomes 10 g of fresh water, which is also 10 cm³. The meltwater fills exactly the "hole" the ice was making, so the level doesn't change. (This ignores tiny temperature effects, which is fine at this level.)

## (b) Salt water, fresh ice: FALSE (level rises, glass overflows)

The "same principle" is exactly what makes this one come out differently. The ice now displaces 10 g of **salt water**. Salt water is denser (seawater is roughly 1.025 g/cm³; I'm recalling that figure, not looking it up, and a classroom brine could be denser). So 10 g of it is only about **9.76 cm³**.

After melting, the cube becomes 10 g of **fresh** water, which is **10 cm³**. That's more than the 9.76 cm³ "hole," so the level goes up. In a full-to-the-brim glass, it overflows. (Mixing salt and fresh water shrinks the volume very slightly, but not nearly enough to cancel this.)

## (c) Fresh water, ice with a pebble inside: FALSE (level drops)

The reason given ("the pebble adds extra volume") is the trap. What matters is the pebble's **weight**, and where it ends up.

- **While floating:** the ice and pebble together float, so they displace their combined weight in water. Say 10 g of ice and a 5 g pebble with a density of about 2.5 g/cm³. Together they displace 15 g of water, or **15 cm³**.
- **After melting:** the ice turns into 10 cm³ of water. The pebble sinks, and a sunken object displaces only its own **volume**: 5 g ÷ 2.5 g/cm³ = **2 cm³**. The total is **12 cm³**.

That's 15 cm³ before and 12 cm³ after, so the level **falls**. While the pebble rides on the ice, it pushes aside water equal to its weight. On the bottom, it pushes aside only its own volume. Because rock is denser than water, the second amount is smaller.

(This is the well-known "rock in a boat" puzzle: throw a rock overboard from a boat in a pool, and the pool level drops.)

## Suggestions for handing it back

- **Give her full credit, and tell her she caught the error.** She didn't overthink it; she got it right where the key didn't. A 9th grader who pushes back on an official answer and turns out to be correct should hear that clearly.
- **Re-grade the class.** Anyone who followed your key on (b) or (c) was marked right for wrong answers, and anyone who reasoned like her lost points.
- **Consider keeping (b) and (c) on purpose.** "Same principle as (a)" is a great distractor. The principle is the same, but applying it gives a different answer. If you have ice, salt, and a full glass, it also makes a quick demo (keep a towel handy for (b)).

A quick check you could ask the class afterward: *"If the pebble were replaced by a piece of cork frozen in the ice, what would happen to the level?"* (The level stays the same. Cork keeps floating after the ice melts, so it still displaces its own weight.)
