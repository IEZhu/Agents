I'm writing a literary short story (around 6,000 words) set on a tidal island off the Northumberland coast, loosely based on Lindisfarne but I call it Saint Aidan's Holm. The climax: my protagonist, a grieving woman in her fifties, starts walking back across the causeway at dusk on the night of a full moon, gets distracted by a memory halfway, and the sea comes in around her. She survives by reaching one of the refuge boxes on stilts. I want the water to feel almost like a character, but I've never actually seen a tide come in and my writing group has a retired harbour master in it who will pick apart anything physically wrong. Can you draft this climax (about 800-1,000 words) and tell me what I need to understand about tides so the rest of the story holds up?

2. The explanation correctly ties the full moon to spring tides (larger tidal range than at neap tides, occurring around full and new moon) and uses that as a reason the flood is higher or faster that night, without claiming the moon visibly drags the water in at moonrise

4. The climax matches the brief: about 800-1,000 words, dusk and full moon, distraction by memory mid-crossing, rescue by reaching a raised refuge box, with the sea rendered as a presence rather than a plot device

## Answer A
## The Crossing

The sun had gone down behind the Cheviots and taken the colour out of everything but the sky. Ahead, the road ran west across the flats, grey on grey, its edges lost in wet sand. Behind her, over the open sea, the moon was coming up full and swollen, the colour of a bruise that has started to heal.

She walked the way you walk when no one is waiting: steadily, and not quite fast enough.

The flats made small sounds. A trickle somewhere she couldn't place. The click of shells settling. Far out on the banks the seals had started up their long, wavering call, a sound like a question nobody ever answers. Redshank lifted in front of her, complained, and landed again a little further on, always a little further on, as if leading her somewhere.

Halfway, where the road dips and the drifted sand lies in its hollows like snow, she stopped.

It was the light that did it. The moon had laid a path across the flats, a thin bright line from the horizon to her feet, and she had seen that exact line once before, from this exact place, with Daniel's hand at the small of her back. *There,* he'd said. *That's the only road you can't walk on.* She had laughed at him. She had been thirty-one and laughed at almost everything he said, because so much of it was meant to be laughed at, and she had not known then that she would one day go looking for the jokes the way you go through coat pockets for a key.

She stood there a long time. Or not long. Long enough.

What brought her back was her feet. They were cold, and they had not been cold before.

She looked down. The ruts at the edge of the tarmac had filled. Not with a wave; nothing had crashed or broken. The water had simply arrived, the way a thought arrives, already there by the time you notice it. It lay in every hollow, flat and full of sky, and as she watched, the hollows reached for each other and joined.

To her left, where the channel crossed under the road, there was a sound now. Not the sea's sound. A river's. The flood was pouring through the low ground with a steady, muscular hurry, running *across* the road rather than towards her, dark and quick and full of the moon.

She began to walk. Then she began to run, which was worse, because the sand in the hollows took her shoes and the water slapped up her shins and she understood, in the part of her that had always understood these things before the rest of her would admit them, that she did not know how far she had come. The island was behind her. The mainland was a low dark line that did not seem to be getting nearer. Between them the water had stopped being a thing that lay in places and become the place itself.

It came up without effort. That was what she would remember. It did not rise the way anger rises. It rose the way a bath fills — patient, level, entirely unconcerned. At her ankles it was cold. At her calves it had weight. By the time it reached her knees it had opinions: it leaned on her from the north, a slow insistent shove, and she had to lean back into it to stay upright, and every step she felt the road through her soles and was grateful for it the way she had never been grateful for a road.

The box stood on its legs in the half-light, a little wooden hut on stilts, absurd and beautiful, like something a child would draw to mean *safe*. She had passed it a hundred times. She had once, God help her, made a joke about it.

The ladder rungs were slick with weed. Her hands would not close properly. The water tugged at her coat as she climbed, not violently, almost fondly, as if it had only wanted to ask her something, and when she hauled herself up onto the boards it let her go without argument.

She sat with her back against the planks and her knees pulled up and breathed.

Below her the road was gone. There was no edge now to anything. The flats, the channel, the causeway she had walked along in daylight, all of it had become one moving sheet, silvered and restless, sliding past the stilts with a sound like someone whispering through their teeth. The moon had risen clear of the sea. Its road lay across the water, bright and unbroken, running right to the foot of the ladder.

*That's the only road you can't walk on.*

She watched it for a long time. Somewhere out on the banks the seals were still calling, and the water went on rising, quietly, beneath her, as though it had all the time in the world, which of course it did. She had only ever had hours. The sea had tides.

Around midnight, she thought, it would turn. Then it would begin, just as patiently, to give the road back.

## What you need to understand about tides

These are the things your harbour master is most likely to test. General principles are stated plainly; anything specific to the real Lindisfarne I've marked as recalled, so check it against the published crossing times and tide tables for Holy Island before you rely on it.

**1. A full moon means spring tides, which is useful for you.** Near full and new moon the tidal range is at its largest: higher high waters, lower low waters, and faster flow in between. That gives your climax a real physical reason to be worse than usual. Two refinements a harbour master might raise:
- The biggest springs usually come a day or two *after* full moon, not on the night itself. "The night after the full moon" is the most defensible choice.
- Springs at any given place happen at roughly the same clock time of day each fortnight. So whether a spring flood can really cover the causeway at dusk on Holy Island depends on the local timings. **This is the most important thing to check.** Pick a real full moon in the season you want, look up the Holy Island times for that date, and see whether the water is flooding at sunset. If it isn't, move the season or the date, or have her cross earlier and linger longer. Don't change the physics.

**2. The moon's rising doesn't cause the flood.** At full moon the moon rises in the east around sunset, which is why I have it coming up over the sea behind her while the sun sets over the Cheviots to the west. That geometry is correct. But nothing surges when the moon clears the horizon. Keep the moon as image and the tide as mechanism.

**3. There's no wall of water.** This is the classic mistake. On flat sands the flood arrives quietly: wet sand darkening, hollows and ruts filling, pools joining up, then a continuous sheet. The real danger is that the **channels and low ground fill first**, often behind you or between you and safety, while the road ahead still looks passable. The water also tends to run *across* the causeway as a current (the flood pouring through a channel), not straight at the walker. I built the scene around that.

**4. The rule of twelfths.** Over the roughly six hours between low and high water, the level rises by about 1/12, 2/12, 3/12, 3/12, 2/12, 1/12 of the range, hour by hour. The middle two hours are the fastest. If she's caught mid-flood, the water should rise noticeably in minutes, which fits "at her ankles… by the time it reached her knees." It's an approximation (real curves vary by location), but harbour masters know it and will respect it.

**5. Moving water is stronger than its depth suggests.** Knee-deep water with a current can knock an adult off balance. That's why she leans into it. Standard flood-safety guidance also says fairly shallow moving water can shift a car (commonly quoted figures are around 30 cm to move one and 60 cm to float it; recalled, so check before you use numbers). If any character drives, that matters.

**6. Weather changes the prediction.** Tide tables assume average conditions. An onshore wind (from the east or north-east on this coast) and low air pressure can push the water higher and earlier than predicted. An offshore westerly can hold it back. A line about the wind turning, or a deep low coming in, gives you a plausible reason she misjudged it even if she'd checked the times.

**7. The refuge box means a long wait.** Once she's up there, she waits for the tide to turn and ebb far enough to uncover the road. That's hours, not minutes. After high water there's a short period of slack before the ebb begins. My "around midnight it would turn" is a placeholder: set it from your chosen date's tide table. On the real island I believe there's one refuge on the road causeway near the bridge over the main channel and another on the Pilgrims' Way (recalled, not verified), and rescues are usually by coastguard or lifeboat. Since Saint Aidan's Holm is fictional, you can place the box wherever you need it. Just keep it near the lowest, most dangerous stretch, because that's the logic behind where they're built.

**8. Get the vocabulary right.** A harbour master will say *high water* and *low water* more than "high tide", *flood* for the rising tide, *ebb* for the falling tide, and *slack* for the pause between them. Springs *make* as they build toward the biggest tides. Using these in the narration, or in the mouth of an islander, will reassure him more than any amount of description.

**9. Details that ring true:** the smell of the flats (weed, mud, a faint sulphur under the surface), wading birds retreating just ahead of the tideline, grey seals calling from the banks (there's a well-known colony in the area), sand drifted across the tarmac, and weed on anything the tide regularly covers, including the refuge ladder.

If you tell me the season and roughly what time she sets out, I can help you fit the timeline together (when she leaves, when she stops, how long she's in the box) so that it matches a real tide table.


## Answer B
## The Crossing

The posts ran ahead of her toward the mainland, each one a little fainter than the last, as if the light were rubbing them out one by one.

She had left late. She knew it the way you know you've left the gas on: a small cold fact at the back of the mind, easy to walk away from. The sand was still firm, ribbed and hard, and the ripples pushed up against her soles through her boots. Behind her, over the priory, the moon was rising the colour of a peeled apricot. Ahead, above the mainland hills, the last of the day lay in a thin line of brass.

She thought she was about halfway. She'd counted the posts once, years ago, with him. He'd got a different number, and they'd argued all the way to the car about whether you counted the first one.

That was where she stopped.

She didn't decide to. There was a razor shell at her feet, split open, its two halves lying the way his hands used to lie on the table when he'd finished speaking and was waiting for her to understand. She crouched and picked up one half. It was lighter than it had any right to be.

What she remembered wasn't the argument. It was afterwards: the car, the heater ticking, his thumb working a flake of dried mud off her cheek. *You've got the island on you,* he'd said. She hadn't wiped it off, because he'd liked it there.

Afterwards she couldn't say how long she crouched there. Long enough.

The first thing was the sound. It wasn't a roar. It was a fizzing, as if the whole flat were a glass of something just poured. Then the sand beside her boot darkened, and she watched a thread of water find a trough between two ripples and run along it, patient, looking for the next. Then another beside it, and another. The ribs of the sand were turning into the veins of something.

She stood and turned toward the mainland. The brass line had gone to ash.

Between her and the next post lay a channel she had crossed dry twenty minutes earlier. She remembered stepping over it, a shallow gutter, nothing at all. Now it was moving, and it was moving sideways. Her body refused to believe that part. The water wasn't coming at her like an animal. It was coming around her like a thought, filling the low places first, places she hadn't known were low.

She looked back. The way to the island had silvered over too.

So it wasn't a wall, or a wave. It was the floor rising.

She went forward, because forward was where the car was, and his jacket was still on its back seat. Her first step into the channel took her to the ankle, and the cold was so total it was almost a sound. On the second step the water reached her shin, and the sand under her gave way and sucked at her foot. She understood that what lay under the water was no longer ground. The ground was still making up its mind. The current leaned on her calves, not hard, just enough, the way a crowd leans.

The box.

She had seen it from the car a hundred times and never once thought of it as somewhere a person might need. It was a hut on legs, an absurd garden shed on stilts, standing a little off the line of posts with a ladder hanging from its door. From the car it had looked like a joke. It didn't now. It looked like the only upright thing in the world.

Thirty yards to it, maybe forty. Distances on the flats lied the way the water did, quietly and in every direction.

She walked toward it. She didn't run. Some animal part of her knew that running was how you fell. The water was at her knees now and pulling, a steady drag she couldn't place, west or south, and it didn't matter, because every direction had become the same direction, which was up. She lost a boot and let it go. The sand took it without comment.

At the ladder the water was at her thighs, and the rungs were as cold as cutlery. Her hands didn't want to close, so she made them. One. Two. Her soaked jeans tried to drag her back down, reasonably, as if they had a prior claim. Three. Four. Then the door. The floor of the box was boards and bird droppings, and she lay on it with her cheek against the wood and breathed.

When she looked down through the doorway, the ladder was going under one rung at a time, slowly and steadily, like someone counting. She watched five rungs go before she stopped watching.

The moon was high now and white. It had laid a road across the water from her feet to the island: straight, bright, easy to walk, and a lie.

She sat with her back to the planks. Out on the flats the posts stood in the sea up to their necks, then their chins. Then some of them were only a row of dark dots, like the marks you put on a page to show that something has been left out.

The water got to where it was going and paused there. She felt it pause, the whole bay still, like a held breath. In that stillness she said his name aloud, only to check whether she still had a voice.

She did. It sounded like someone else's. She let it.

Somewhere toward the mainland, a blue light began to turn.

## What you need to know about tides

These are the things a harbour master will check first. Where I give a specific number or a fact about Lindisfarne itself, it comes from memory, not from a source I've checked, so verify it before you rely on it.

**1. It isn't a wave.** This is the most common mistake in fiction. On flat sands the flood arrives as a rising floor. The channels and gutters fill first, water spreads sideways through low ground you didn't notice, and walkers get cut off from behind as well as in front. The vertical rise may be modest, but on a very gentle slope the water's edge can advance fast horizontally. The famous line that it comes in "faster than a galloping horse" is an exaggeration, and your harbour master will know that.

**2. The rise isn't steady.** Water rises slowly for the first hour or so of the flood, fastest in the middle two hours, then slowly again near high water. Sailors call this the "rule of twelfths". If your protagonist loses track of time partway into the flood, she hits the fastest part. That's why the water goes from ankle-deep to thigh-deep so quickly in the draft.

**3. Full moon means spring tides.** Springs have the biggest range: the highest highs, the lowest lows, and the strongest flows. (The name has nothing to do with the season.) The largest tides usually come a day or two *after* the full moon, not on the night itself. A full moon rises in the east at about sunset, so a moon coming up behind her over the island while she walks west into the afterglow is accurate.

**4. Timing is where you're most likely to be caught out.** At any given place, spring high water falls at roughly the same clock time every fortnight. So "full moon, dusk, flood tide" either works at that location or it doesn't. Dusk at Lindisfarne's latitude also shifts a lot through the year, from around 4pm in December to around 10pm in June. My advice: take a real tide table for Holy Island (an Admiralty table or an online equivalent), find a full-moon date where the flood is running at sunset, and quietly set the story in that month. Also keep the rest of the story consistent: high tides come about 12 hours 25 minutes apart and about 50 minutes later each day.

**5. Safe crossing times are published.** Northumberland County Council publishes safe crossing times. As I recall, the causeway is unsafe from a few hours before high water until a few hours after. The danger starts well before high water, because the flood has to fill the flats first. Your reader will ask why she was out there at all, so she needs to set off near the end of the safe window. That's why the draft has "She had left late." A harbour master will read her pausing as the plausible failure, not the tide itself.

**6. Road or Pilgrims' Way.** There are two routes. One is the tarmac road causeway. The other is the Pilgrims' Way, a walking route straight across the sands marked by tall posts. As I recall, each has a refuge box on stilts. I put her on the Pilgrims' Way, because walkers use it and the posts give you imagery. If you want her on the road, swap the ribbed sand for water sheeting across tarmac from the low side, and put the box near the bridge over the channel.

**7. Depth, current and cold.** At spring high water the crossing is well over head height in places. From memory it's around 2 m in the channels, but check. Knee-deep water that is *moving* can knock an adult over, and saturated sand gives underfoot. The North Sea is cold even in summer, roughly 8 to 14°C (recalled), so the first contact is a gasp, not a chill.

**8. Weather changes the numbers.** Tide tables predict only the astronomical tide. An onshore wind or low air pressure can push the water higher and earlier. That gives you a useful lever if you want her misjudgement to be partly bad luck.

**9. Afterwards.** Once she's in the box, she waits out high water and then most of the ebb. That's several hours before the sands are walkable again. Real rescues there usually involve HM Coastguard, often with the RNLI lifeboat or a helicopter; the blue light at the end reflects that. I invented the inside of the box. Check what's actually in them, or keep it bare.

**10. Use the right words.** She'd say "the tide came in", but your narrator can use "flood" and "ebb", "springs" and "neaps", and the "slack" or "stand" at high water. That's the pause she feels in the last section, and it's real, though on open flats it's brief.

If you want to take the scene further, I can write a colder version with less interiority, or rework it for the road causeway.
