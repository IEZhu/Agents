## Latest user message
Hi, quick one. We're a small B2B SaaS (Irish entity, customers mostly in DE/FR). A former customer, a sole trader in Munich, emailed support on Sept 22 saying: "Delete everything you have about me, immediately, I withdraw all consents." What we hold on him:

- account profile (name, email, phone, billing address)
- 14 invoices from 2023-2025
- marketing newsletter subscription (consent-based)
- ~40 support tickets
- product usage analytics tied to his user ID
- an open chargeback he filed with his card issuer in August for his last invoice, still unresolved

Our own privacy policy, which is public on our site, says: "When you ask us to delete your data, we will delete all of it within 30 days." My CEO says just nuke the whole account today to be safe. Our finance lead says we can't touch invoices. Who's right, what exactly should we delete vs keep, and what do we tell him and by when?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Gives a split outcome rather than siding wholesale with either the CEO or the finance lead: invoices (and data needed for them) are kept under the legal-obligation exception of Article 17(3)(b), while data without a retention basis (newsletter/marketing data, analytics tied to him, and support tickets not needed for a claim or legal duty) is deleted or anonymised.
2. Identifies the unresolved chargeback as a separate reason to retain the related records for establishing, exercising or defending legal claims (Article 17(3)(e)), and suggests restricting or limiting access to retained data rather than keeping it in active use.
3. Flags that the public privacy policy promising deletion of 'all' data conflicts with the retention obligations and should be corrected, and that the customer should be told which data is kept and why.
4. States the response deadline correctly: without undue delay and at the latest within one month of receipt, extendable by two further months where necessary, with the customer informed of any extension within the first month (Article 12(3)); withdrawal of consent means marketing emails must stop.

## Answer A
**Short answer:** Neither is fully right. Your CEO is wrong that deleting everything today is "safe". It would put you in breach of Irish tax record-keeping rules, and it would destroy the evidence you need to fight the open chargeback. Your finance lead is right about the invoices, but "we can't touch them" goes too far. You delete most of the data, keep a small set on specific legal grounds with restricted access, and reply to him in writing by **22 October 2026** at the latest.

This is EU GDPR territory. You're an Irish controller, so the Irish DPC is your lead supervisory authority under the one-stop-shop. He's a sole trader, but he is still a natural person, so his data is fully covered. Being "B2B" doesn't exempt you.

---

## Why each position fails

**"Nuke it all today" (CEO)**
- Art. 17(3) GDPR sets out exceptions to erasure, and two of them apply to you: **(b)** compliance with a legal obligation (tax and accounting retention) and **(e)** establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims (the chargeback).
- If you delete the invoice, the related tickets and the usage logs, you will probably lose the chargeback by default, because you'd have nothing left to show the issuer.
- Wiping everything also wipes your record of how you handled the request. Under Art. 5(2) accountability, you need to be able to show the DPC what you did and why.

**"Can't touch invoices" (Finance)**
- Correct: invoices and the accounting data behind them must be kept. As I recall, Irish law requires about **6 years**: s.886 Taxes Consolidation Act 1997, s.84 VAT Consolidation Act 2010, and s.282 Companies Act 2014 for accounting records. Those section numbers are from memory, not verified here, so have your accountant confirm the exact periods.
- But "keep" doesn't mean "leave as is". Retained data should be restricted to finance and legal staff and used for nothing else.

**His "I withdraw all consents"**
- Withdrawing consent (Art. 7(3)) only affects processing that is based on consent: the newsletter, and analytics if that runs on consent. Invoices and billing rely on contract and legal obligation, not consent.
- Most of the rest still has to go, though. He's a former customer, so under Art. 17(1)(a) the data is "no longer necessary" for the purpose it was collected for.

## Delete vs. keep

| Data | Action | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Newsletter subscription** | Stop **today**. Delete the marketing profile and keep only a suppression entry (email, ideally hashed) so he never gets re-added | Art. 7(3), Art. 21(3) (an objection to direct marketing is absolute). Suppression lists are standard practice for honouring an objection |
| **14 invoices** | **Keep** for the statutory period, with restricted access | Art. 17(3)(b) plus Irish tax/VAT/company law (about 6 yrs, verify) |
| **Account profile** | Close the account. Delete phone number and any profile fields not needed elsewhere. Keep only what's on the invoices (name, billing address, VAT ID if any) and the billing email while the chargeback is open | Art. 17(1)(a). Remainder kept under 17(3)(b)/(e) |
| **~40 support tickets** | **Delete**, except any that relate to the disputed invoice, the service delivered in that period, or billing/cancellation. Keep those until the dispute is closed | Art. 17(3)(e) for the subset only |
| **Usage analytics** | **Delete, or genuinely anonymise** (pseudonymised data is still personal data). Keep only logs that prove service delivery for the disputed billing period, until the dispute is closed | Art. 17(1)(a)/(b). 17(3)(e) for the evidence subset |
| **Chargeback file** | **Keep** until it is finally resolved. Then review: delete, or keep for a defined period if further claims are realistically possible | Art. 17(3)(e) |

Whether a card chargeback counts as a "legal claim" under 17(3)(e) isn't settled, but I think it very likely qualifies: it is a formal payment dispute that can escalate. Keep only what is actually relevant to it, and document why.

**Don't forget processors and backups:**
- **Processors:** Tell your processors to delete (email platform, helpdesk tool, analytics vendor, CRM). Art. 19 requires you to pass the erasure on to recipients unless that's impossible or disproportionate, and your Art. 28 contracts should require them to act on it.
- **Backups:** It's generally acceptable to let the data age out of backups on your normal rotation, provided you don't restore or use it in the meantime. Tell him this.

## Your privacy policy is a problem

The policy says "we will delete all of it within 30 days", which is wrong as a matter of law. It doesn't override your tax obligations, but it does create a transparency issue: Art. 5(1)(a) and Art. 13(2)(a) require you to state retention periods accurately. He could reasonably complain that you broke your own published commitment.

- **For this request:** explain the exceptions clearly in your reply. Being upfront is your best defence.
- **Afterwards:** change the policy to something like: "We will delete your data within one month, except data we must retain by law (e.g., invoices, for [X] years) or need for pending disputes, which we will retain only for as long as necessary."

---

## Deadline and what to tell him

- **Deadline:** Art. 12(3) allows one month from receipt, so **22 October 2026**. Your own policy's "30 days" also lands on **22 October**. You can extend by two months only for complex requests, and this one isn't, so don't.
- **Identity:** if the email came from the address on the account, that is normally enough. Don't ask for ID documents without a real reason for doubt (Art. 12(6)).

**The reply should say:**
1. His marketing consent withdrawal and objection have been actioned. He has been unsubscribed and added to a suppression list so he isn't contacted again.
2. What you have deleted: account, contact details, support history, usage data. Say that backups will be overwritten within [X days] as part of normal rotation.
3. What you are keeping, why, and for how long:
   - invoices and billing data for [6] years under Irish tax law;
   - data related to the open chargeback until it is resolved.

   This is required: Art. 12(4) says you must give reasons for not acting in full.
4. That the retained data is restricted and not used for anything else.
5. His right to complain to a supervisory authority and to seek a judicial remedy (Art. 12(4)). He can go to the Irish DPC or to his local authority; for a Munich resident and a private-sector controller, I believe that's the Bavarian BayLDA.

**Internally:** log the request, the date you received it, each deletion action, and the justification for each item kept.

---

**Main risks:** having your prompt reply undermined by the privacy-policy wording, keeping more tickets or logs "for the chargeback" than you can justify, and processors never actually deleting. Given the live dispute, a quick review by Irish data protection counsel before you send the reply would be worth it. This is information, not legal advice.

## Why "delete everything" is wrong, and why his wording doesn't decide it

- **Withdrawing consent only affects processing that was based on consent** (Art. 7(3) GDPR). Here that's the newsletter and possibly some analytics. Most of what you hold rests on other grounds: the contract (Art. 6(1)(b)), legal obligation (Art. 6(1)(c)) and legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)).
- His email still counts as a valid **erasure request under Art. 17**. Being a sole trader doesn't change that, because he's a natural person and his business data is personal data. Most of the data is no longer needed now that the contract has ended (Art. 17(1)(a)), so that part has to go.
- The right to erasure is not absolute. Two exceptions apply to you:
  - **Art. 17(3)(b)**: processing needed to comply with a legal obligation under EU or member-state law. This covers the tax and accounting retention rules.
  - **Art. 17(3)(e)**: processing needed to establish, exercise or defend legal claims. This covers the open chargeback.
- Your privacy policy promises "we will delete all of it within 30 days". That promise doesn't override a statutory retention duty, because the legal obligation wins. But it is an inaccurate public statement, and it creates a transparency problem under Arts. 12–13 (Art. 13(2)(a) requires you to state retention periods). **Fix the policy** so it names the exceptions (tax records, legal claims, marketing suppression). Until you do, the policy is the likeliest thing a regulator would pick up on if he complains.

| **~40 support tickets** | Delete them all, **except** tickets about the disputed invoice or service period. Move those into a restricted "dispute file". | Art. 17(1)(a); Art. 17(3)(e) for the dispute-related ones. |
| **Product usage analytics** | Delete, or properly anonymise (an irreversible break from his user ID; pseudonymised data is still personal data). **Except** usage logs for the disputed billing period, which prove the service was delivered. Those go in the dispute file. | Art. 17(1)(a)/(b); Art. 17(3)(e). |
| **Chargeback materials** | Keep the invoice, the dispute-relevant tickets, the usage evidence, and the contract/ToS acceptance record until the chargeback **and any follow-up claim** are finally resolved. Then delete everything except the invoice. | Art. 17(3)(e). Handle this as restricted processing, in the spirit of Art. 18. |
| **Record of his request and your response** | Keep a minimal log (date, what you deleted, what you kept and why). | Accountability, Art. 5(2). This is standard practice. |

**Also do the following:**

- **Processors**: instruct your email tool, helpdesk, analytics tool, CRM and billing provider to delete or suppress his data too. Art. 19 requires you to tell recipients about the erasure. Payment providers like Stripe will keep their own copies as independent controllers under their own legal obligations. You can mention this to him, but you can't delete it for them.
- **Backups**: you don't need to restore backups just to delete from them. Put the data "beyond use" and let it age out on your normal backup cycle, and tell him how long that takes.
- **Identity**: if the request came from the email address on file, that's normally enough. Don't demand ID. Art. 12(6) only allows extra verification where you have reasonable doubts.

## What to tell him, and by when

- **Deadline**: "without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt" (Art. 12(3)). You received it on 22 September 2026, so the deadline is **22 October 2026**. That date also matches your own 30-day promise, so treat it as the hard limit and aim to reply in the first week of October. An extension of two more months is possible only for complex or numerous requests, and this one doesn't qualify.
- **Content** (Art. 12(4) requires this when you don't fully act on a request):
  1. Confirm the newsletter has stopped and the account is closed.
  2. List what you deleted.
  3. List what you kept, **why** (the specific legal basis: tax retention law and the open chargeback), **for how long**, and that it is restricted to those purposes only.
  4. Say that the dispute-related data will be deleted once the chargeback is resolved, and the invoices when the statutory retention period ends.
  5. Tell him he has the right to complain to a supervisory authority and to seek a judicial remedy. Your lead authority is the Irish DPC. Under Art. 77 he can also complain in Germany, where the Bavarian authority for the private sector is the BayLDA (recalled; confirm the name).

A two-line version for the key paragraph: *"We have deleted [X]. We are legally required to keep your invoices for [6] years under Irish tax law, and we are retaining limited records relating to your open card chargeback until that dispute is resolved (Art. 17(3)(b) and (e) GDPR). These records are restricted and used for no other purpose."*

## Risks

- **Over-deleting** (the CEO's plan) likely breaches tax record-keeping duties. It also means you'll probably lose the chargeback, because you can't prove the service was delivered.
- **Over-keeping** (holding all 40 tickets or all his analytics "because of the chargeback") is also a problem. Only material that is actually relevant to the dispute falls under Art. 17(3)(e).
- **Your privacy policy** is currently your weakest point. Update it this week.
- I haven't verified the Irish retention periods or the German authority details this session. Confirm them with your accountant or an Irish data protection lawyer. This is general information, not legal advice.
