## Latest user message
Need a sanity check before tomorrow's load test review. Our job queue has 25 workers, each does 40 jobs/min. The test profile is 1,500 jobs/min for the first 20 minutes, then drops to 600 jobs/min and stays there. The autoscaler is dumb: at the end of every 5-minute window (t=5, 10, 15, ...) if the backlog is above zero it requests 5 more workers, hard cap 40 workers, and it never scales down during the test. A requested worker takes exactly 1 minute to boot, then runs at full speed. Two questions: what's the max backlog we'll hit (Redis list is capped at 5,000 messages, anything above gets dropped), and at what minute does the backlog get back to zero? My teammate says we'll blow past 5k, I think we're fine, but I'd like actual numbers.

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Carries state forward segment by segment with the boot lag: 25 workers until t=6 (backlog 3,000), 30 workers until t=11 (backlog 4,500), 35 workers until t=16 (backlog 5,000), then 40 workers (backlog 4,600 at t=20).
2. States the peak backlog as 5,000 at t=16: exactly at the Redis cap, so nothing is dropped under these idealized assumptions but there is zero headroom; does not report the lag-free 4,500 or the fixed-capacity 10,000 as the peak.
3. States that after t=20 the backlog drains at 1,000 jobs/min (1,600 capacity minus 600 incoming) and reaches zero at about t=24.6 (24 min 36 s).
4. Answers the teammate disagreement directly and honestly (neither side is comfortably right: no overflow on paper, but any jitter or slower boot would overflow).

## Answer A
**Bottom line:** In the ideal model the backlog peaks at **exactly 5,000 at t=16**, so nothing gets dropped. It drains to **zero at t≈24.6 min** (about 24:36). You're technically right, but with **zero headroom**. Any small deviation tips it over, so I'd treat this as "will probably drop" rather than "fine".

### Timeline (fluid model, continuous arrivals)

Capacity is 40 jobs/min per worker. Scale checks run at t=5, 10, 15, 20, and each new batch of workers comes online 1 minute after its request.

| Interval | Workers | Capacity | Inflow | Net/min | Backlog at end |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | 25 | 1,000 | 1,500 | +500 | 2,500 → scale request |
| 5–6 | 25 | 1,000 | 1,500 | +500 | 3,000 |
| 6–10 | 30 | 1,200 | 1,500 | +300 | 4,200 → scale request |
| 10–11 | 30 | 1,200 | 1,500 | +300 | 4,500 |
| 11–15 | 35 | 1,400 | 1,500 | +100 | 4,900 → scale request |
| 15–16 | 35 | 1,400 | 1,500 | +100 | **5,000 (peak)** |
| 16–20 | 40 | 1,600 | 1,500 | −100 | 4,600 (t=20 request is a no-op because of the cap) |
| 20+ | 40 | 1,600 | 600 | −1,000 | 4,600 / 1,000 = 4.6 min → **0 at t≈24.6** |

### Why "fine" is the wrong word

The peak lands on the cap exactly, so your margin is zero. Things that push it over:

- **Worker throughput.** Peak = 24,000 − 475·r, where r is jobs/min per worker. At r=40 you get exactly 5,000. At r=39 (2.5% slower) it's 5,475, so about 475 jobs are dropped. Each 1% throughput shortfall adds about 190 jobs over the cap.
- **Boot time.** Any extra time in the third boot (t=15→16) costs about 1.7 jobs per second at +100/min. Slow boots in the first two waves cost more: about 8 jobs/s and 5 jobs/s.
- **Burstiness.** Real arrivals aren't smooth at 1,500/min. Any short-term burst around t=15–16 overflows.
- **How the cap is enforced.** Whether exactly 5,000 counts as "full" depends on how the cap is implemented (an `LTRIM` to 5,000 versus a `>=` check before push). Check that code before relying on the boundary.
- **Clock alignment.** If the autoscaler's 5-minute windows don't line up with the test start, every scale-up shifts later and the peak goes above 5,000.

So your teammate is wrong about blowing well past 5k in the nominal case. But the nominal case is the best case, and it leaves nothing to spare.

### Cheap fixes before the review (any one gives real margin)

- **Start at 30 workers.** The peak drops to roughly 2,500–3,000.
- **Raise the Redis list cap** to 10k or more, if memory allows. Check the average payload size first.
- **Shorten the scale window** to 1–2 minutes, or scale by more than 5 workers per step.
- **Alert when backlog passes 4,000** during the test, so a near-miss is visible even if nothing gets dropped.

For the review I'd present it as: "No drops in the nominal model, but the peak hits the cap exactly. We need margin." That's more defensible than either "we're fine" or "we'll blow past it".


## Answer B
**Short answer:** in the ideal model the peak backlog is **exactly 5,000 at t=16**, so there's zero headroom. It drains to **zero at t≈24.6 min (24:36)**. Neither of you is really right. On paper nothing gets dropped, but any real-world slack pushes you over the cap.

### Minute-by-minute (fluid model: smooth arrivals, workers at exactly 40/min)

Capacity per worker count: 25 → 1,000/min, 30 → 1,200, 35 → 1,400, 40 → 1,600.

| Interval | Workers | In/min | Out/min | Net/min | Backlog at end |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | 25 | 1,500 | 1,000 | +500 | 2,500 (backlog > 0, request +5) |
| 5–6 | 25 | 1,500 | 1,000 | +500 | 3,000 (new workers up) |
| 6–10 | 30 | 1,500 | 1,200 | +300 | 4,200 (request +5) |
| 10–11 | 30 | 1,500 | 1,200 | +300 | 4,500 |
| 11–15 | 35 | 1,500 | 1,400 | +100 | 4,900 (request +5, reaches the 40 cap) |
| 15–16 | 35 | 1,500 | 1,400 | +100 | **5,000 (peak)** |
| 16–20 | 40 | 1,500 | 1,600 | −100 | 4,600 |
| 20–24.6 | 40 | 600 | 1,600 | −1,000 | **0 at t=24.6** |

The checks at t=20 and later change nothing because you're already at the 40-worker cap. The t=25 check finds an empty queue.

### Why "we're fine" doesn't hold

A peak of exactly 5,000 against a 5,000 cap means the model only works if everything is perfect. Small deviations each push you over:

- **Throughput 1% below 40/min.** Workers deliver about 19,000 jobs over t=0–16, so a 1% shortfall is about 190 extra jobs. That gives a peak near 5,190 and roughly 190 dropped.
- **Boot takes ~10 s longer than 1 min.** Each scale-up wave loses about 200/min × 1/6 min ≈ 33 jobs. Three waves add about 100 jobs, all dropped.
- **Bursty arrivals.** Your load generator won't produce a perfectly smooth 1,500/min. Short-term spikes around t=15–16 go straight over the cap.
- **Cap semantics.** Check whether your trim (e.g. `LTRIM`) or producer guard drops at `len >= 5000` or `len > 5000`. With a peak of exactly 5,000, that off-by-one decides whether you lose messages at all.

So my expectation is that the real test will **probably drop some messages, likely in the tens to low hundreds**, around t=14–17. That's a judgment based on the sensitivities above, not a certainty. Your teammate's "blow past 5k" overstates it, but the risk is real.

### Cheap fixes for the review (any one gives you margin)

- **Start at 30 workers.** Then the peak is only about 2,300 (+300/min for 6 min, then +100/min) and nothing comes near the cap.
- **Shorten the autoscaler window** to 2–3 minutes, or scale on backlog *growth rate* rather than `backlog > 0`.
- **Raise the Redis list cap** to around 10k for the test, if memory allows (5k messages is usually tiny, but check your payload size).
- **Pre-warm** 5–10 workers just before the load ramps.

### What to watch during the test

- `LLEN` on the queue at t=14–17 is where it will either hold or overflow.
- Measured per-worker throughput vs. the assumed 40/min. This is the biggest sensitivity.
- Actual boot-to-first-job latency for each scale-up wave.
- Any drop counter or producer-side rejection metric, so overflow shows up as a number rather than silent loss.
