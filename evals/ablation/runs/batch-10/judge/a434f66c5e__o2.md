## Latest user message
Our CFO wants to exit our SaaS contract with a vendor this week and I've been asked to sanity-check her reasoning before we send the notice. Relevant clauses (we're 14 months into a 36-month prepaid term):

9.2 "Customer may terminate this Agreement for convenience on sixty (60) days' written notice, provided that Customer is not then in breach of any payment obligation."
9.4 "Upon any termination by Customer under Section 9.2, Customer shall remain liable for all Fees for the remainder of the then-current Subscription Term."
9.5 "If Vendor fails to meet the Availability SLA in three (3) consecutive calendar months, Customer may terminate immediately upon written notice and Vendor shall refund all prepaid Fees for the remainder of the Subscription Term."
9.6 "Section 9.4 shall not apply where termination results from Vendor's material breach."

Facts: the SLA was missed in March, April and June; May met it. We also have one invoice (support add-on, $8,400) that is 15 days past due because we're disputing it.

CFO's argument: "They missed the SLA three times this year, so under 9.5 we can walk away today and get the prepaid fees back. And even if 9.5 somehow doesn't apply, three SLA misses is obviously a material breach, so 9.6 switches off 9.4 — we terminate under 9.2 and owe nothing more." Does this hold up?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. States that 9.5 is not triggered because the SLA misses (March, April, June) are not three consecutive calendar months.
2. Notes that the right to terminate under 9.2 is conditioned on the customer not being in breach of a payment obligation, so the overdue $8,400 invoice may block or be used to contest a 9.2 termination until it is paid or the dispute is resolved.
3. Explains that the fallback does not follow: SLA misses are not shown to be a material breach on these facts, and a termination made under the convenience clause 9.2 is not obviously one that 'results from' a vendor breach, so relying on 9.6 to switch off 9.4 is not supported; a breach-based exit would need grounds beyond the clauses quoted.
4. Concludes that on the clauses given, a convenience termination would likely leave the company liable for fees for the rest of the term, and suggests concrete next steps (for example resolving the disputed invoice, checking the contract for a general material-breach termination clause and SLA remedies, or waiting to see whether the misses become consecutive).

## Answer A
**Short answer: no. Both parts of the argument have serious problems, and sending an immediate-termination notice this week on this reasoning could put *you* in breach.** Below I read only the four clauses you quoted. The governing-law clause and the rest of the contract could change parts of this, so I note where.

---

## 1. Section 9.5 is not triggered

9.5 requires the SLA to be missed in **"three (3) consecutive calendar months."** Your facts are March (miss), April (miss), May (**met**), June (miss). May breaks the run, so the longest consecutive streak is two months. "Three times this year" is not what the clause says, and a court will almost certainly hold you to the word "consecutive." That word is plainly there to exclude the scattered-misses case you have.

Two things are worth checking before you drop this route:
- **July, August and September.** Your facts stop at June. If July and August were also missed, then June, July and August *is* three consecutive months, and 9.5 would be live. Pull the availability reports now.
- **Whether May really met the SLA.** Look at how "Availability" is defined: the measurement window, excluded maintenance, and whether "met" is decided on the vendor's own report or can be challenged. If May's figure only passes because of a questionable exclusion, you may have an argument. It's a weaker one, and you'd need evidence.

If neither check helps, a 9.5 notice today is a notice you're not entitled to give.

## 2. The "material breach → 9.6 switches off 9.4" route fails at three separate points

**(a) You may not be able to use 9.2 at all.** 9.2 applies only if "Customer is not then in breach of any payment obligation." You have an $8,400 invoice 15 days past due. Whether withholding it counts as a *breach* depends on whether the contract has an invoice-dispute mechanism (typical wording lets you withhold a disputed amount if you give written notice of a good-faith dispute within X days) and whether you followed it. If there's no such clause, or you didn't give proper dispute notice, the vendor will say 9.2 was never available and your notice is invalid. **This condition is the most immediate practical risk, and it's easy to fix.** See section 4 below.

**(b) "Three SLA misses is obviously a material breach" is not obvious.** Materiality depends on the facts: how bad the outages were, how much of the benefit you actually lost, and whether damages can compensate. In US jurisdictions courts usually weigh factors like those in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 241 (recalled, not verified here). English-law-style jurisdictions ask a similar question about "repudiatory" breach. Two things in your own contract cut against you:
- The parties **negotiated a specific threshold** for when SLA failures justify walking away, which is three *consecutive* months in 9.5. A tribunal is likely to read that as the agreed measure of a serious SLA failure. Calling fewer or scattered misses "material" would get around the bargain, and the specific clause will generally govern over a general "material breach" argument.
- Most SaaS agreements also make **service credits the "sole and exclusive remedy"** for SLA misses. If yours does, the material-breach argument based on SLA misses is close to dead. Check the SLA schedule.

**(c) 9.6 probably doesn't attach to a 9.2 termination anyway.** 9.6 disapplies 9.4 "where termination *results from* Vendor's material breach." A 9.2 termination is by definition *for convenience*, meaning it needs no reason. Saying "we terminate for convenience, but it results from their breach" is a hard position to hold. The more natural reading is that 9.6 is aimed at a termination *for cause*. There is probably a for-cause clause (often 9.3, usually with a notice-and-cure period) that you haven't quoted. If material breach is the theory, that is the clause to use, and it almost certainly requires notice plus a cure window first.

## 3. Even if it all worked, "owe nothing more" doesn't mean "get money back"

The term is **prepaid**. Switching off 9.4 only removes liability for *future* Fees. It does not give you a refund. The only refund right in what you quoted is in 9.5. So on the CFO's fallback theory, the best case is roughly: you stop paying for things not yet paid (e.g., the support add-on) and the vendor keeps the prepaid 22 months. Recovering prepaid fees without 9.5 would need a separate damages or restitution claim for material breach. That runs into the problems in 2(b), plus whatever limitation-of-liability cap the contract has.

Also note that **9.2 requires 60 days' notice**. Even on the CFO's own theory, you don't "walk away this week"; the termination takes effect about 60 days after the notice.

## 4. What I'd recommend instead

1. **Don't send a 9.5 notice** unless the July–September data shows three consecutive misses. A termination notice you aren't entitled to give can be treated as wrongful termination or repudiation and hands the vendor the moral and legal high ground.
2. **Fix the payment exposure first.** Either confirm you gave a compliant written dispute notice for the $8,400 invoice under the contract's dispute clause, or pay it under protest with an express reservation of rights. That keeps 9.2 available.
3. **Pull the rest of the contract**: the Availability SLA definition, service-credit and sole-remedy language, any for-cause termination and cure clause, the invoice-dispute clause, the limitation of liability, notice formalities, and governing law.
4. **Preserve your SLA position.** Claim any service credits within the contract's deadline, and send a written notice documenting the March, April and June failures with a reservation of rights. If the vendor misses the next months, 9.5 could become available later.
5. **Use the SLA record as negotiating leverage.** Realistically, the best route to getting part of the prepaid fees back is a **negotiated mutual termination** with a partial refund, not a unilateral notice. The documented misses plus a credible for-cause notice are your leverage there.

**Bottom line for the CFO:** 9.5 fails on "consecutive." The material-breach route is contestable at best and likely blocked by the SLA's own remedy structure. 9.2 may be unavailable because of the unpaid invoice. And none of it produces a refund of prepaid fees except 9.5.

---

## Answer B
**Bottom line:** No. Neither route works as the CFO describes it, and sending the notice this week on her theory could make *you* the party in breach. Section 9.5 isn't triggered on these facts. The 9.2 + 9.6 route has three separate problems. Even if it worked, it wouldn't get the prepaid fees back.

I don't know the governing law yet. The points below come mostly from the text of the clauses, so they should hold in most jurisdictions. The materiality point is the exception: it depends on the governing law, so tell me which law governs.

---

## 1. Section 9.5 isn't triggered

9.5 requires failure "in three (3) **consecutive** calendar months." March, April and June are three misses, but May met the SLA, which breaks the run. The clause doesn't count misses per year. It asks for an unbroken run of three months.

**Something to check right away:** your facts stop at June, and it's now late September. If the SLA was also missed in **July and August**, then June–July–August is three consecutive months and 9.5 may be live *now*. Get the vendor's SLA reports, or your own monitoring data, for July and August before doing anything else. That's the only way you realistically get "immediate exit plus refund of prepaid fees."

Also read the SLA exhibit itself for:
- how "Availability" is measured (exclusions, scheduled maintenance, who measures it),
- any requirement to claim or report a miss within a set period,
- any wording that says service credits are the "sole and exclusive remedy" for SLA failures. That wording would also undercut the material-breach argument below.

## 2. The 9.2 + 9.6 route has three problems

**(a) Termination under 9.2 is by definition not "for breach."** 9.2 is termination *for convenience*. 9.6 switches off 9.4 only where "termination **results from** Vendor's material breach." The vendor will argue that a notice sent under 9.2 is a convenience termination, so it can't also be a termination that "results from" breach. The more natural reading is that 9.6 covers termination for breach: under a separate breach clause (is there a 9.3?) or under the general law. It doesn't let you pick the convenience route and then claim the breach carve-out. I think the CFO's reading is possible but weak.

**(b) Three SLA misses is not "obviously" a material breach.** It's arguably the opposite. The parties specifically agreed when SLA failures justify an exit: three consecutive months (9.5). A court or arbitrator may well treat that as the agreed threshold, and read two non-consecutive runs of misses as falling below it. This is especially likely if the SLA provides service credits as the remedy. Materiality usually turns on things like how much benefit you lost, whether money can compensate you, and whether the vendor is likely to fix the problem. (In US law the factors are set out in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 241, recalled, not verified.) What matters is how bad the misses were. 99.4% against a 99.9% target is very different from multi-day outages.

**(c) You may not be eligible to use 9.2 at all right now.** 9.2 applies only if "Customer is not then in breach of any payment obligation." You have an $8,400 invoice that is 15 days past due. Whether a *disputed* invoice counts as a breach depends on the rest of the contract:
- If there's an invoice-dispute clause (good-faith dispute, written notice, right to withhold the disputed amount) and you followed it, you probably aren't in breach.
- If there isn't one, or you didn't give proper written notice of the dispute, the vendor has a clean argument that 9.2 isn't available to you.

The cheapest fix is to pay the $8,400 under protest, with a written reservation of rights, or to make sure the dispute notice complies with the contract, *before* sending any termination notice.

## 3. Even if the route worked, it doesn't get money back

Two further points the CFO's summary glosses over:

- **Timing:** 9.2 requires 60 days' written notice. Under 9.2 you can't "walk away today" or this week. The earliest effective date is 60 days after the notice.
- **No refund right:** you prepaid all 36 months. 9.4 makes you "liable for all Fees" for the rest of the term, and those fees are already paid. Switching off 9.4 through 9.6 removes that liability, but **nothing in 9.2 or 9.6 says the vendor must refund anything.** The only express refund right is in 9.5. Recovering the ~22 months of prepaid fees without 9.5 would mean a damages or restitution claim under the governing law, which is a lawsuit, not a notice.

## 4. The main risk

If you send a notice relying on 9.5 or a material-breach theory that doesn't hold up, the vendor can treat it as a **wrongful termination or repudiation**. You would then be the party in breach, which undercuts your position on the prepaid fees and on the disputed invoice.

## Suggested next steps

1. **Don't send a 9.5 or breach-based notice this week** unless the July–August data shows a third consecutive miss.
2. Pull the July and August availability figures now.
3. Read the full SLA exhibit and the rest of Section 9 (a breach clause, cure periods, notice formalities, limitation of liability).
4. Resolve the $8,400 issue: comply with the dispute procedure, or pay under protest.
5. Send a **reservation-of-rights letter** that records the March, April and June misses and your service-credit claims, without terminating. It keeps your options open if another miss happens.
6. Use the SLA record as **negotiating leverage** for an agreed exit with a partial refund. That's often more realistic than a litigated fight over materiality.
7. Confirm the governing law and have local counsel review before anything goes out. A wrong termination notice is hard to reverse.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Have a lawyer licensed in the contract's governing jurisdiction review the full agreement before you send any notice.*
