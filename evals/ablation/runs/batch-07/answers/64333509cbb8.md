**No, don't use it as written.** A CFO who checks it will probably find three problems: the "multiple studies" are almost certainly one study, that study doesn't measure what the sentence claims, and newer research points the other way for fully-remote work.

## Why five sources count as one here

Five citations of the same number are not five independent confirmations. The 13% figure almost certainly comes from a single experiment: Nicholas Bloom et al.'s randomized trial at Ctrip, a Chinese travel company. It was published as "Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015, with a working-paper version around 2013). The 2014 HBR piece is, as I recall, Bloom summarizing his own study. The Forbes, Medium, vendor and LinkedIn posts are very likely repeating HBR or each other. When sources trace back to one primary study, they count as one source. So "multiple studies confirm" is wrong, and the ten-year spread just means the number kept being shared, not that it was re-tested.

Your list also mixes source quality. The peer-reviewed paper is the only one that matters. HBR is a decent secondary source. A SaaS vendor selling remote-collaboration tools has a conflict of interest. LinkedIn reactions tell you nothing about accuracy.

## What the study actually found

These details are from memory and I haven't checked them here, so confirm them against the QJE paper before the memo goes out:

- **Who:** roughly 250 call-center staff who **volunteered** for home working, randomly assigned by even or odd birthday.
- **Setup:** about 9 months around 2010–11. The home group worked from home **about four days a week**, not fully remote.
- **Result:** about a 13% performance gain. Most of it came from working more minutes (fewer breaks and sick days) and the rest from more calls per minute (a quieter environment). Attrition fell by roughly half.
- **Caveat the CFO may know:** when the firm later offered home working to everyone, a large share of the treatment group chose to go back to the office. As I recall, that group also got fewer promotions for the same performance.

So the finding is narrow: volunteers, doing measurable individual call-center work, mostly at home, in one company in 2010. It doesn't show that "remote workers" in general are 13% more productive, and it doesn't directly test fully-remote work.

## The evidence a sceptic will raise

Also from memory, so verify before citing:

- **Bloom et al., *Nature*, 2024 (Trip.com, hybrid with 2 WFH days):** no measurable productivity difference, but quit rates fell by about a third. This is a strong result for flexibility, but it is about hybrid work, and the productivity finding is neutral.
- **Studies of fully-remote or pandemic-era work** (e.g., Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth on IT staff in India; Atkin et al. on data-entry workers in India; Emanuel & Harrington on call-center selection effects) mostly found **lower** measured productivity for fully-remote workers, or found that less productive workers select into remote jobs.
- Bloom's own recent public summary, as I recall it, is roughly: hybrid is about neutral for productivity and positive for retention; fully remote averages somewhat lower productivity but can still pay off through cost savings and a wider hiring pool.

If you open with 13% and the CFO knows this literature, the whole memo loses credibility.

## Rewrites

**Accurate and still useful:**
> "In a widely cited randomized trial, employees who worked from home most of the week were about 13% more productive and half as likely to quit (Bloom et al., *QJE*, 2015). The gains came from fewer breaks and sick days and a quieter environment."

**Stronger for a leadership audience (retention and cost, where the evidence is sturdier):**
> "The best research on remote work finds its clearest benefits in retention and cost rather than raw output. Randomized trials at Trip.com found that flexible work cut attrition by a third to a half with no measurable productivity loss (Bloom et al., *QJE* 2015; *Nature* 2024). For us, that means [X fewer departures × our replacement cost ≈ $Y] plus [$Z in avoided office costs]."

**Most persuasive for a CFO:** your own numbers. Compare output, attrition, time-to-hire, candidate pool size and real-estate spend from before and after going remote. Use the research to support your figures, not to stand in for them.

## Other points to get right

- **Be upfront about the weak spot.** The external evidence is stronger for hybrid than for fully remote. Say that openly and argue fully remote on your own grounds (talent pool, cost, your measured results), instead of letting the CFO point it out.
- **Cite the primary paper, not the posts.** Drop Forbes, Medium, the vendor blog and LinkedIn from the memo.
- **Check the details above** (sample size, four days a week, the 2024 attrition figure) in the papers themselves before sending. I've given them from memory.
