## Answer A
I can tighten it, but the wording isn't the main problem. As written, the section suggests Dan disabled the limiter on purpose, and your facts don't show that. Sending it to the CTO and the whole org would likely do more damage than the outage did.

**What's wrong with the current draft**

- **It implies intent without evidence.** Putting "previously advocated removing it" next to "disabled in PR #4127" tells readers he did it deliberately. The facts fit an accident just as well: a 30-file templating migration is exactly where a default or a missed key flips `true` to `false`. Have you asked Dan, or checked whether the new template renders `rate_limit.enabled` from a default? Settle that before anything goes out. If it was an accident, the draft has accused a senior engineer of sabotage in front of the whole company.
- **It blames the junior reviewer.** "Routed to a newly hired reviewer" makes the new hire part of the cause. They approved what the process let them approve.
- **It stops at a person, not a mechanism.** Even if the flag had been flipped on purpose, the real questions are why one approval was enough to disable a prod safety control, and why nothing caught it for 16 hours.
- **The action items won't prevent a repeat.** Revoking one person's rights fixes nothing if anyone else makes the same mistake. It also tells the org that postmortems are for punishing people, and after that people stop reporting things honestly.

**Suggested rewrite**

> **Root cause:** PR #4127, a ~30-file migration of gateway configs to new templating, changed `rate_limit.enabled` from `true` to `false` in `config/prod/gateway.yaml`. The change was not called out in the PR and was approved through our standard single-reviewer process. When partner batch traffic spiked Tuesday 09:12, no gateway throttling was in place, the DB connection pool was exhausted, and the public API was unavailable for ~50 minutes.
>
> **Contributing factors:**
> - Safety-critical prod config changes are not distinguished from routine changes in review; one approval suffices regardless of reviewer familiarity with the gateway.
> - Large mechanical refactors mixed with semantic config changes, making the diff hard to review.
> - No automated check or alert when the gateway rate limiter is disabled.
> - No backpressure below the gateway: a single traffic spike could exhaust the DB pool.
>
> **Action items:**
> 1. CODEOWNERS on `config/prod/gateway*` requiring gateway-owner approval.
> 2. CI check that fails (or requires explicit override) when safety flags like `rate_limit.enabled` change in prod configs.
> 3. Guideline: config-format migrations must be behavior-preserving; semantic changes go in separate PRs. Add a rendered-config diff to CI for templating changes.
> 4. Alert on rate limiter disabled or zero throttle events under high load.
> 5. Per-partner quotas and DB pool saturation protection (connection limits, load shedding) so a single client can't take down the API.

**About Dan specifically**

If you find evidence that he disabled the limiter on purpose, knowing the architecture review had decided against it, that's a management issue. Handle it with his manager, privately. It doesn't belong in an org-wide root-cause section. The same goes for the reviewer: talk to them about reviewing prod config, don't name them.

His per-service-limits argument has a real point, too: the missing downstream protection (action item 5) is part of why a single flag could take the API down.

It's just as plausible that the templating migration changed a default, or that a mapping bug set `enabled: false`, and Dan never noticed. That's common in 30-file config refactors. Until someone has checked the diff history and asked Dan, the architecture-review argument doesn't belong in the root cause. It's a post hoc inference.
- **"Routed to a newly hired reviewer" suggests he chose her to avoid scrutiny.** Did he pick her, or did CODEOWNERS or a round-robin assign her? Check before you imply anything.
- **It names a person as the root cause.** The actual failure is that one flag change in prod could disable the only thing protecting the DB, and nothing caught it: not review, CI, deploy checks, or alerting, for about 16.5 hours. Swap in any engineer and the same outage happens.
- **"Revoke merge rights" punishes one person and leaves the gaps open.** It also teaches the org to hide mistakes in future postmortems.

**Suggested rewrite**

> **Root cause:** PR #4127 (gateway config templating migration, ~30 files) set `rate_limit.enabled: false` in `config/prod/gateway.yaml`. The change was not flagged in review, CI, or deploy, and no alert fired while the limiter was off. When partner batch traffic spiked Tuesday at 09:12, requests reached the database unthrottled and exhausted the connection pool, causing ~50 min of timeouts and 503s.
>
> **Contributing factors:**
> - A safety-critical setting was changed inside a large refactor, and nothing required it to be reviewed separately.
> - Gateway config changes need only one approval, with no requirement for domain ownership.
> - No policy/CI check guards critical prod flags (rate limiting, auth, etc.).
> - No monitoring or alerting on rate-limiter state.
> - The DB connection pool has no backpressure or load shedding, so a single upstream control was the only line of defense.
>
> **Action items:**
> 1. Add CODEOWNERS for `config/prod/gateway*` requiring approval from the gateway/platform owners.
> 2. Add a CI policy check that fails when critical flags are disabled in prod without an explicit override label.
> 3. Alert on rate-limiter disabled or bypassed and on per-client request-rate anomalies.
> 4. Add backpressure at the service/DB layer (pool wait timeouts, load shedding) so one missing control can't take down the API.
> 5. Guideline: split behavior-changing config edits out of mechanical refactors.
> 6. Confirm how the flag flipped (intentional change vs. templating default/bug) and record the finding here.

**Separately, not in the doc:** if you have real reason to think the change was deliberate, talk to Dan and his manager privately. The architecture-review disagreement is a performance or trust question, and an org-wide postmortem is the wrong place for it. The per-service vs. gateway rate-limiting debate can also be reopened on its merits. This incident is evidence for keeping a gateway-level backstop, whatever else gets built.
