## Latest user message
I'm writing an internal memo for our leadership team arguing we should keep fully-remote as the default policy. I want to open with this line: "Multiple studies confirm that remote workers are 13% more productive than office workers." I found the 13% figure in five places: a Forbes contributor article (2021), a blog post from a remote-collaboration SaaS vendor (2023), a LinkedIn post by a VP of People with 12k reactions, a Harvard Business Review piece (2014), and a Medium article (2022). Five independent sources over almost ten years seems pretty solid to me. Is this sentence OK to use as-is? If not, how should I rewrite it so it holds up when our CFO pushes back?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Identifies that the 13% figure traces back to a single experiment (the Stanford study of Ctrip call-center employees) and states that the five articles are therefore effectively one source, so 'multiple studies confirm' is not supported.
2. Differentiates the five items by credibility: flags the SaaS vendor's commercial interest and treats the LinkedIn and Medium posts as weak secondary repetition, while pointing the user to the original study itself as the thing to cite.
3. Flags limits on generalising the figure: a single experiment in one call-center setting, conducted before the pandemic-era shift, may not transfer to the user's company or to fully-remote knowledge work, and should not be stated as a universal fact.
4. Offers a concrete rewritten sentence that attributes the figure to the single study with its context, and does not fabricate additional studies, citations or statistics to shore up the claim.

## Answer A
**No, don't use it as written.** A CFO who checks it will probably find three problems: the "multiple studies" are almost certainly one study, that study doesn't measure what the sentence claims, and newer research points the other way for fully-remote work.

## Why five sources count as one here

Five citations of the same number are not five independent confirmations. The 13% figure almost certainly comes from a single experiment: Nicholas Bloom et al.'s randomized trial at Ctrip, a Chinese travel company. It was published as "Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015, with a working-paper version around 2013). The 2014 HBR piece is, as I recall, Bloom summarizing his own study. The Forbes, Medium, vendor and LinkedIn posts are very likely repeating HBR or each other. When sources trace back to one primary study, they count as one source. So "multiple studies confirm" is wrong, and the ten-year spread just means the number kept being shared, not that it was re-tested.

Your list also mixes source quality. The peer-reviewed paper is the only one that matters. HBR is a decent secondary source. A SaaS vendor selling remote-collaboration tools has a conflict of interest. LinkedIn reactions tell you nothing about accuracy.

## What the study actually found

These details are from memory and I haven't checked them here, so confirm them against the QJE paper before the memo goes out:

- **Who:** roughly 250 call-center staff who **volunteered** for home working, randomly assigned by even or odd birthday.
- **Setup:** about 9 months around 2010–11. The home group worked from home **about four days a week**, not fully remote.
- **Result:** about a 13% performance gain. Most of it came from working more minutes (fewer breaks and sick days) and the rest from more calls per minute (a quieter environment). Attrition fell by roughly half.
- **Caveat the CFO may know:** when the firm later offered home working to everyone, a large share of the treatment group chose to go back to the office. As I recall, that group also got fewer promotions for the same performance.

So the finding is narrow: volunteers, doing measurable individual call-center work, mostly at home, in one company in 2010. It doesn't show that "remote workers" in general are 13% more productive, and it doesn't directly test fully-remote work.

## The evidence a sceptic will raise

Also from memory, so verify before citing:

- **Bloom et al., *Nature*, 2024 (Trip.com, hybrid with 2 WFH days):** no measurable productivity difference, but quit rates fell by about a third. This is a strong result for flexibility, but it is about hybrid work, and the productivity finding is neutral.
- **Studies of fully-remote or pandemic-era work** (e.g., Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth on IT staff in India; Atkin et al. on data-entry workers in India; Emanuel & Harrington on call-center selection effects) mostly found **lower** measured productivity for fully-remote workers, or found that less productive workers select into remote jobs.
- Bloom's own recent public summary, as I recall it, is roughly: hybrid is about neutral for productivity and positive for retention; fully remote averages somewhat lower productivity but can still pay off through cost savings and a wider hiring pool.

If you open with 13% and the CFO knows this literature, the whole memo loses credibility.

## Rewrites

**Accurate and still useful:**
> "In a widely cited randomized trial, employees who worked from home most of the week were about 13% more productive and half as likely to quit (Bloom et al., *QJE*, 2015). The gains came from fewer breaks and sick days and a quieter environment."

**Stronger for a leadership audience (retention and cost, where the evidence is sturdier):**
> "The best research on remote work finds its clearest benefits in retention and cost rather than raw output. Randomized trials at Trip.com found that flexible work cut attrition by a third to a half with no measurable productivity loss (Bloom et al., *QJE* 2015; *Nature* 2024). For us, that means [X fewer departures × our replacement cost ≈ $Y] plus [$Z in avoided office costs]."

**Most persuasive for a CFO:** your own numbers. Compare output, attrition, time-to-hire, candidate pool size and real-estate spend from before and after going remote. Use the research to support your figures, not to stand in for them.

## Other points to get right

- **Be upfront about the weak spot.** The external evidence is stronger for hybrid than for fully remote. Say that openly and argue fully remote on your own grounds (talent pool, cost, your measured results), instead of letting the CFO point it out.
- **Cite the primary paper, not the posts.** Drop Forbes, Medium, the vendor blog and LinkedIn from the memo.
- **Check the details above** (sample size, four days a week, the 2024 attrition figure) in the papers themselves before sending. I've given them from memory.


## Answer B
**No, don't use it as written.** It will probably fall apart the first time your CFO asks "which study?" You don't have five independent sources. You most likely have one study quoted five times, and that study doesn't test what your memo argues for.

## Why the sentence won't hold up

**1. Your five sources are one source.** A 13% remote-productivity figure almost certainly comes from Nicholas Bloom's Ctrip experiment. The HBR piece from 2014 is very likely Bloom's own summary of it. The Forbes, vendor, LinkedIn and Medium posts are downstream repetitions. Copies of one study count as one piece of evidence, however many times the figure appears. Ten years of repetition shows the number is popular. It doesn't show it has been replicated. So "multiple studies confirm" is the sentence's most exposed claim.

**2. The source quality varies a lot.** The only item with real standing is the HBR piece, and only because it points back to the peer-reviewed paper (Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015; recalled, not verified, so confirm the citation). A SaaS vendor that sells remote collaboration tools has a commercial interest in the result. A LinkedIn post's reach tells you nothing about its accuracy. Forbes contributor and Medium pieces are unedited opinion platforms. A CFO will cite the paper or nothing.

**3. The study doesn't match your claim.** These details are recalled, so check each one against the paper:
- **One firm, one job type:** call-center staff at a Chinese travel agency. Their output is easy to count, and that isn't typical of knowledge work.
- **Not fully remote:** as I recall, participants worked from home four days a week and came in one day. That was early hybrid, not your policy.
- **Where the 13% came from:** roughly 9 points came from working more minutes (fewer breaks and sick days) and about 4 points from more calls per minute. That's output per shift, not "remote workers are more productive" in general.
- **Side effects:** attrition roughly halved, which helps your case. But promotion rates for home workers fell, and many participants chose to return to the office after the trial. Both hurt your case.

**4. Later evidence cuts against fully remote.** Again recalled, so verify before citing:
- Bloom et al. (*Nature*, 2024) ran a larger hybrid trial at the same company. It found roughly no productivity change and about a one-third drop in quits.
- Other experiments on fully remote work have found productivity losses, for example a data-entry study in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) and a call-center study by Emanuel & Harrington.
- Bloom himself has said in public commentary that fully remote tends to reduce productivity unless it is managed well, while hybrid is roughly neutral.

A CFO who has read any of this can turn your opening line against you.

## How to rewrite it

Claim only what the evidence supports, and cite the primary paper:

> "In a well-known randomized trial at a 16,000-employee company, employees working from home most of the week handled 13% more output than office-based peers and quit at about half the rate (Bloom et al., *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015)."

If you'd rather frame the evidence as a whole, which is honest and harder to argue with:

> "Research on remote productivity is mixed and depends on the job: some rigorous trials show gains, others show losses. The most consistent finding is lower attrition, which directly cuts our hiring and onboarding costs."

## How to build a case that survives the CFO

- **Lead with retention and cost, not raw productivity.** The retention evidence is stronger across studies. Translate it into your own numbers: your attrition rate, cost per hire, time to productivity, and office costs avoided.
- **Use your own data.** Output metrics, attrition and hiring reach from your remote period will persuade a CFO more than any outside study, because they describe your company.
- **Address the counter-evidence first.** Name the weaknesses of fully remote work (mentoring, promotion gaps, onboarding) and explain how you'll mitigate them, for example with in-person onboarding and scheduled team weeks. This makes your case stronger.
- **Cite peer-reviewed papers or working papers (QJE, *Nature*, NBER) directly.** Drop the vendor, LinkedIn and Medium sources completely.

**Before you circulate the memo:** the study details above (sample, schedule, the 9/4 split, promotion effects, the 2024 follow-up, the other remote-work studies) come from memory and I haven't checked them here. Open the QJE paper and the 2024 *Nature* paper and confirm each figure you quote.
