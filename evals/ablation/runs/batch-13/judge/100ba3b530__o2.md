I'm giving a 12-minute lightning-plus talk at a local DevOps meetup next Thursday about how we cut our CI pipeline from 41 minutes to 9. Things I definitely want in there: the before state (monorepo, 1,800 tests run serially on every PR), test sharding across 8 runners, dependency caching (saved about 6 min alone), only running affected packages via change detection, the flaky-test quarantine we set up, and the one thing that backfired — we cached Docker layers too aggressively and shipped a stale base image to staging once. ## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Per-slide time budgets sum to 12 minutes or less, with about 2 minutes reserved for Q&A, the intro/opening at 1 minute or less, and the stale Docker base image story given at least 2 minutes.
2. All six required points each appear on an identifiable slide: the before state (monorepo, 1,800 serial tests, 41 min), sharding across 8 runners, dependency caching (~6 min saved), affected-packages change detection, flaky-test quarantine, and the stale Docker base image incident.
3. Speaker notes on each slide are short enough to be delivered in that slide's allotted time (no multi-paragraph notes for a 30-60 second slide).
4. The talk has a coherent arc (setup, the improvements, the backfire lesson, a closing takeaway) and uses the user's numbers consistently (41 to 9 minutes) without inventing additional metrics presented as the user's.

## Answer A

**Audience:** DevOps practitioners at a meetup. They're technical and a bit skeptical ("sure, but *your* monorepo isn't mine"), and they want mechanics and scars, not slogans. They'll probably forgive a missing slide faster than a missing number.

**Core message:** *CI speed comes from running less, running in parallel and reusing work. Every shortcut you cache is a shortcut you have to be able to invalidate.*

**Structure:** Hook, then Before, then four fixes, then the backfire, then the takeaway. I tease the Docker story in the first 45 seconds (a curiosity gap) so the whole room waits for it.

## Delivery notes for someone who runs over

- **Use checkpoints, not a clock you glance at nervously.** Memorize three times. At **2:45** you start caching. At **6:15** you start the Docker story. At **8:45** you start the closer. If you're 30+ seconds late at 6:15, cut from slides 5 and 6 on the spot and **never from Docker**.
- **Plan your cuts in advance.** If you're behind, drop the dependency-graph detail on slide 5 and the ownership detail on slide 6. Together that's about 30 seconds.
- **Don't take mid-talk questions.** Say "hold that for Q&A" and keep going. One early question can easily cost you more than your whole buffer.
- **Rehearse the Docker story out loud at least twice with a timer.** Stories are exactly the part that swells on the day, because you remember one more detail.
- **Start slide 1 with the number, not with "Hi, so, um, I'm..."** Your name is already on the slide.

## "How to Fail" Corner

- Want to blow the 1-minute intro cap? Open with your company's founding story and a team photo, while the organizer glares at you from the back.
- Want the Docker story to fall flat? Put it on one bullet ("cache issue, fixed w/ digest pinning") and rush through it because you're 90 seconds over. That's the part people came to hear.
- Want nobody to trust your 41 → 9? Skip the per-step numbers and let the audience wonder whether 20 of those minutes came from a bigger runner size.
- Want Q&A to vanish? Treat the 30-second buffer as bonus content time. It isn't.

## Slide by slide

**Slide 1: "41 minutes" (0:45)**
Show the number alone, huge, in red. Nothing else on the slide.
*Say:* "Every PR we opened cost 41 minutes of waiting. I'm [name], I work on [team] at [company]. In the next ten minutes I'll show you how we got it to 9, and the one optimization that shipped broken code to staging."
The last clause is a curiosity gap you'll pay off at minute 6. Don't use an agenda slide.

**Slide 2: "1,800 tests. One at a time." (0:45)**
Show a simple diagram: monorepo → one long bar labeled "1,800 tests, serial, every PR."
*Say:* "One monorepo, 1,800 tests, run one after another on every PR, whatever you touched. Fix a typo in the README and you wait 41 minutes." (Use the typo line only if it's true for you.)

**Slide 3: "Split it 8 ways" (1:15)**
Show one bar splitting into 8 shorter parallel bars.
*Say:* How you split the tests (by file, by historical timing, or round-robin) and why. Then give the honest catch: 8 runners don't give you 8x, because setup costs are paid 8 times and the slowest shard sets the pace. Give your real before/after for this step: "[X] → [Y] minutes."

**Slide 4: "~6 minutes, just from caching" (1:00)**
Show a before/after bar where the dependency-install chunk shrinks.
*Say:* "The biggest single win was the most boring one. Caching dependencies saved about 6 minutes on its own." Say briefly what the cache key is based on (lockfile hash, for example).
*Hook:* close with "Remember this slide. Caching comes back later, and not in a good way." That sets up the Docker story.

**Slide 5: "Don't test what you didn't touch" (1:15)**
Show a monorepo package graph: one changed package in color, its dependents lit up, everything else grayed out.
*Say:* How change detection works for you (the tool, and how you handle transitive dependents). Say what you do when shared or root config changes, because someone will ask. Give your number: "A typical PR now runs [N]% of the suite."
This is the "do less work" pillar. Say that phrase out loud, since it's your controlling idea.

**Slide 6: "Quarantine, not delete" (1:00)**
Show a flaky test moving into a separate "quarantine" lane that doesn't block merges.
*Say:* How a test gets flagged, where quarantined tests still run, and who owns getting them back out. One sentence on why it mattered: flaky reruns were eating [X] minutes and everyone's trust. If quarantined tests have no owner and no exit rule, admit it. That honesty lands well with this crowd.

**Slide 7: "So we cached Docker layers too" (0:45)**
Show slide 4's green "caching = fast" visual again, now with Docker layers stacked on it.
*Say:* "Caching had just given us 6 minutes. Naturally we went looking for more." Explain what you cached and how aggressively, and why it seemed safe at the time. Slow down here. This is the start of the part people will remember.

**Slide 8: "Staging ran an image nobody built" (1:00)**
Show a story slide: a timeline of the incident, or one quote from the Slack thread if you have a good one.
*Say:* Tell it as a story. What someone noticed first, how long it took to find the cause, the moment you realized the base image was stale. Pause for two or three seconds before you reveal the root cause. Don't rush this beat. It's the emotional peak of the talk.

**Slide 9: "Cache speed, verify correctness" (0:45)**
Show a problem/solution split: left (red) is the cache key that ignored base image changes, right (green) is your fix.
*Say:* What you actually changed. Common fixes include pinning base images by digest, putting the base image digest into the cache key, or forcing a fresh pull on a schedule, but only claim the one you really did. Lesson in one line: "A cache is a claim that nothing changed. Make sure something checks that claim."

**Slide 10: "41 → 9" (1:00)**
Show the red 41 from slide 1 next to a green 9. Optionally add a small stacked bar showing what each change saved, but only if you have real numbers for each step.
*Say:* A 20-second recap: split it, cache it, skip it, quarantine it. Then the ask: "Before you optimize anything, measure where your minutes actually go. And every cache you add, ask what happens when it's wrong." Then "Questions?" and stop talking.
