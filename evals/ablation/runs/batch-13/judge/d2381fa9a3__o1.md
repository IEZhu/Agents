## Latest user message
My son (11th grade physics) got into an argument with his teacher and now I'm stuck in the middle. The question: a closed hourglass sits on a very precise digital scale. When all the sand is resting in the bottom, the scale reads 500.0 g. You flip it and the sand starts streaming down. Does the scale read less than 500 g while the sand is falling? My son says obviously yes, because the grains that are in mid-air aren't resting on anything, so they can't push on the scale. The teacher says no, it reads the same, because the sand hitting the bottom pushes extra. My son says that's just hand-waving and the two effects wouldn't cancel exactly. Who's right, and how do I explain it to him so it actually clicks?

## Rubric (what a strong answer does)
1. Frames the problem with a system-level principle (Newton's second law for the whole hourglass: scale force minus total weight equals total mass times center-of-mass acceleration) rather than only tracking individual grains.
2. Concludes that during steady flow the reading equals the full weight (500 g) to a very good approximation, so the teacher's conclusion is right and the son's 'obviously lighter' is wrong.
3. Notes that the reading does deviate briefly when the flow starts (slightly less, as the center of mass begins accelerating downward) and when the last sand lands (slightly more, as it decelerates), which addresses the son's valid instinct that something happens.
4. Directly answers the son's objection that the two effects would not cancel exactly (e.g. via the system-level argument or by showing the airborne mass and the extra impact force balance), in language a parent can relay to an 11th grader.

## Answer A
**Short answer:** For the main part of the flow, the teacher is right. The scale reads 500.0 g. Your son is still right to ask why, because "the impact pushes extra" is only a claim until someone shows that the two effects cancel. They do cancel, and the proof is about four lines of algebra. He's also partly right about something else: at the very start and very end of the flow, the reading *does* change for a moment.

## The calculation that settles it

Take the steady middle part of the flow and suppose sand leaves the neck at a constant rate **μ** (kg per second) and falls a height **h** before it lands.

**Effect 1: sand in the air (your son's point).**
A grain takes t = √(2h/g) to fall. In that time μ·t of sand leaves the neck, so the mass in the air at any moment is

  m_air = μ·√(2h/g)

The scale is missing the weight of that sand: **m_air·g = μ·g·√(2h/g)**.

**Effect 2: the landing force (the teacher's point).**
Each grain hits at speed v = √(2gh) and stops. To stop a stream of mass μ per second moving at v, the bottom has to push up with force μ·v, and the sand pushes down on the glass by the same amount. So the extra force is

  **μ·v = μ·√(2gh)**

**Compare them.** Write √(2gh) as g·√(2h/g), which is the same number:

  μ·√(2gh) = μ·g·√(2h/g)

The two expressions are identical. The cancellation is exact, with no approximation, and it holds for any flow rate and any fall height. That's the result your son said wouldn't happen, and it's why the teacher's claim holds up.

## Why it has to cancel (the deeper reason)

This way of seeing it tends to make it click. **The scale doesn't measure "what is resting on it." It measures the total weight, plus whatever extra force is needed to change the momentum of everything inside.**

As a formula: scale force = Mg + (rate of change of the system's total momentum).

- In steady flow, the same amount of sand is always in the air, always moving at the same range of speeds. The total momentum inside the glass isn't changing, so the extra term is zero and the scale reads Mg = 500.0 g.
- The scale reads less only while the system's downward momentum is *growing*, and it reads more only while that momentum is *shrinking*.

An analogy: stand on a bathroom scale and hold still, and it reads your weight. Start to crouch quickly and it dips, because you're speeding up downward. Stop the crouch and it jumps, because you're slowing down. Moving down at a *steady* speed changes nothing. The steady stream of sand is like that steady-speed part.

## Where your son has a real point

- **At the start**, just after the flip, the first grains are falling but none have landed yet. There's sand in the air and no impact force, so the scale **briefly reads less**. His intuition is correct for that moment.
- **At the end**, the last grains are still landing but nothing new is leaving the neck. There's impact force with no matching sand in the air, so the scale **briefly reads more**.
- **A small correction in the middle:** the pile at the bottom rises, so the fall height slowly gets shorter. That means the downward momentum in the air slowly shrinks, which in principle makes the hourglass read *slightly more* than 500 g. The effect is far too small for a kitchen-type scale to detect. As I recall, this was worked out in a paper by Shen and Scott in the *American Journal of Physics* (1985). That's from memory, so check the citation before quoting it.

So the fairest verdict: the teacher's answer is correct for the question as it's usually meant (the steady flow). Your son's instinct that "the hand-waving needs a proof" is exactly how a physicist should think. The proof just happens to go the teacher's way.

## A way to present it to him

Don't hand him the answer. Try this order:

1. Ask: "How much sand is in the air at any moment? Write it using μ, h and g." Let him work out m_air = μ√(2h/g).
2. Ask: "How hard does the stream hit? What force stops μ kg per second moving at speed v?" He should get μ√(2gh).
3. Ask: "Now compare those two numbers." Let him find the cancellation himself. It's much more convincing when he does the algebra than when he's told.
4. Then the challenge question: **"So when *would* the scale read less than 500 g?"** If he answers "right after the flip, before any sand lands," he has understood it, and he also gets to be right about something.

That last step matters. It turns the argument from "who wins" into "we both learned where the effect really lives."


## Answer B
**Short answer:** The teacher is right about the main point. While the sand is flowing steadily, the scale reads 500.0 g. Your son's objection is still a good one. "Those two effects might not cancel exactly" deserves a real calculation, not a hand-wave. When you do the calculation, the two effects cancel exactly. There is also a tiny leftover correction, and it makes the reading slightly *higher*, not lower.

## The clean argument: the missing weight and the extra push are the same number

Suppose sand leaves the neck at a steady rate of **μ** grams per second and each grain falls for a time **t** before it lands.

**1. How much weight is "missing"?** The grains in mid-air are the ones that started falling during the last *t* seconds. That's a mass of **μ·t**. Your son is right that they aren't pressing on anything, so the scale loses their weight: **μ·t·g**.

**2. How big is the extra push from the impacts?** A grain lands going at speed **v = g·t**, because it has been in free fall for time *t*. The bottom pile has to stop that grain, which means removing momentum. Each second, a mass μ arrives at speed g·t, so the pile has to remove momentum at a rate of **μ·g·t** per second. A rate of momentum change is a force, so the impacts push down with an extra **μ·g·t**.

The missing weight is μ·t·g and the extra impact force is μ·g·t. They are the same expression, just written in a different order. That's the rebuttal to "they wouldn't cancel exactly": they have to cancel, because both depend on the same fall time *t*.

## The deeper reason (why it had to work out)

For the whole hourglass as one system, Newton's second law says:

> (scale force) − (total weight) = rate of change of the system's total momentum

The scale only reads something other than the weight if the contents *as a whole* are speeding up or slowing down. During steady flow, the falling stream looks the same from one moment to the next. Every second, one grain-packet starts falling and another one lands, so the total momentum doesn't change. Change in momentum is zero, so the reading equals the weight.

The "mid-air grains can't push" argument looks at only one part of the system. The impacts are the other part, and they carry exactly the force those mid-air grains would otherwise supply.

## Where your son's instinct is partly right

The reading is **not** 500.0 g at every moment:

- **Right after the flip:** the stream is still forming. Grains have left the top but none have landed yet, so the scale briefly reads **less**. This is the effect your son is picturing. It's real, but it only lasts for the first fraction of a second.
- **At the very end:** the stream shuts off, the last grains are still landing, and nothing new is leaving the top. The scale briefly reads **more**.
- **During steady flow:** the pile at the bottom slowly rises, so each grain falls a little less far. The stream's downward momentum therefore shrinks slowly over time, and the scale reads *very slightly more* than 500 g. The excess works out to roughly μ × (the speed at which the pile's surface rises). For a real hourglass that's something like a hundred-thousandth of a gram, far too small for a 0.1 g display to show. As I recall, this effect was worked out in the *American Journal of Physics* in the 1980s under the name "the hourglass problem" (recalled, not verified, so confirm the exact reference if you want to cite it).

So on the question as the teacher asked it, the teacher wins. Your son's sense that "exactly equal" needs proof is good scientific instinct, and he can turn it into the stronger result that the tiny correction actually goes the *other* way.

## How to make it click for him

1. **Don't start by declaring a winner.** Ask him to calculate both effects himself: "How much sand is in the air at any moment? How hard does the sand hit the bottom?" Once he writes μ·t·g next to μ·g·t, he's convinced himself.
2. **Use a juggler analogy.** A juggler stands on a scale with three balls. At any instant, one or two balls are in the air. Does the scale's *average* reading drop below juggler + three balls? It can't, because the balls would sink over time. The hands have to push up on the balls, on average, with their full weight, and the scale feels that push. The hourglass is a smooth, continuous version of the same thing.
3. **Give him the transients as a reward.** Tell him he's right that the reading changes when the flow starts and stops, and ask him to predict the direction at each end. That respects his intuition and places it where it's actually true.

**A question to check he's got it:** if you put the hourglass in an elevator that moves up at *constant speed*, does the scale reading change? (No. Constant velocity means no change in momentum, for the same reason as above.)
