## Why his case is stronger than it looks

Here is the strongest version of his argument:

- **A 19-hour median to first review means the typical PR sits idle for most of a working day.** That's before any back-and-forth. With ~25 PRs a week across 6 people, several PRs are stuck in the queue at any moment. The costs are context switching, stacked branches, merge conflicts, and bigger PRs, because people batch work when each review costs a day.
- **He isn't proposing "no review".** He's proposing review that doesn't block the merge. Risky categories still get a blocking review. Feature flags separate merging from releasing, so unfinished or broken code doesn't reach users.
- **Blocking review is often less rigorous than it looks.** A reviewer who is under time pressure or looking at a large diff tends to skim and approve. Small PRs reviewed after merge can get more real attention than big PRs reviewed before it.

If your answer to "PRs wait 19 hours" is "unreviewed code is bad", you'll look like you're defending the bottleneck. Your team will notice that.

## Points that hold up

These are the strongest arguments against the proposal as described:

1. **"Reviewed within a day" is a promise your data says you won't keep.** Today the author is blocked and chasing reviewers, and the median is still 19 hours. Once nobody is blocked, that pressure disappears. Ask him: what enforces the one-day SLA, what happens when it slips, and who tracks it? If review after merge quietly becomes no review, you've lost review without deciding to.
2. **Findings after merge are expensive and often don't get acted on.** Once code is on main, other work builds on top of it. A design problem found a day later becomes "fix it later", and a lot of that never happens. Ask how review findings will be tracked and closed.
3. **"Risky stuff" is decided by the author, at the moment they're least objective.** Migrations and auth are easy to spot. Incidents often come from changes that looked harmless: a query change, error handling, a config default, retry or timeout logic, a dependency bump. If the rule depends on the author's judgment, it leaks. At minimum, ask for objective triggers (file paths, CODEOWNERS, schema or security-sensitive modules) rather than self-classification.
4. **Feature flags don't cover everything a backend change touches.** Refactors, shared utilities, logging, performance changes, and the code paths that run with the flag off all ship unprotected. Flags also add their own cost: stale flags, combinations of flag states to test, and cleanup work. Ask what share of your actual PRs could realistically go behind a flag.
5. **The proposal only works if your safety net is strong.** That means good automated test coverage in CI, monitoring that catches regressions quickly, and fast, reliable rollback. If any of these is weak, unreviewed code on main is a real risk, not a theoretical one. This is the point to press hardest. Ask him to show these are in place, not just assume them.
6. **Compliance may require it.** If you're subject to SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI or customer contracts that require review before deploy, this may not be a team-level decision at all. I don't know your obligations, so check before you raise it.
7. **Knowledge sharing gets weaker.** Blocking review is partly how 6 people stay familiar with each other's code. Feedback after merge is easier to skip.

## What I'd bring instead of a list to shut it down

You and he actually agree that the 19-hour wait is a problem. You disagree on the fix. Options that address the wait without giving up blocking review:

- **A review SLA for blocking review**, for example first response within a few working hours, with a rotating "reviewer on duty". Many teams cut wait times a lot with this alone.
- **Smaller PRs and a cap on PR size**, which also makes reviews faster.
- **Pairing or mobbing counts as review** for the work it covers.
- **A tiered model.** Some teams use a three-level model, as I recall called "Ship / Show / Ask" (I haven't verified the name or source). Trivial changes merge directly, some merge and are shown for review afterwards, and the rest are asked for review before merge. That is roughly what he's proposing, but with explicit, agreed criteria.
- **A time-boxed experiment.** Try review after merge for one clearly defined low-risk category for 4–6 weeks. Measure time to review after merge, the number of issues found after merge, incidents, and throughput. Then decide from data rather than conviction.
