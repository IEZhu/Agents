**The short answer:** your risk is most likely about the same as any pregnancy's: roughly **3 in 100** for a major birth defect. That is the baseline for every pregnancy, whatever was taken. Six doses of ibuprofen at 4–5 weeks, if they add anything at all, probably add a fraction of a percentage point. That is too small for studies to measure reliably, so no one can honestly give you a more exact number.

(The figures below are from my recall of the published literature. I have not checked them against a source this turn, so please confirm them with your obstetrician or the services listed at the end.)

### Why the number is reassuring

- **Baseline risk.** About 3% of all babies (the usual range quoted is 2–4%) have a major congenital anomaly. Most of these have no identifiable cause. Clinically recognised miscarriage happens in roughly 10–20% of pregnancies, and the rate falls once a heartbeat is seen on ultrasound. Both of these risks were already there before the ibuprofen.
- **Birth defects.** Large cohort studies of NSAIDs in the first trimester have mostly found no meaningful overall increase in major malformations. A few studies reported weak links with specific defects, such as some heart defects and gastroschisis. Those findings have not held up consistently, and even where they appeared, the absolute increase was very small. A short, standard-dose course like yours is the most reassuring kind of exposure.
- **Miscarriage.** The "NSAIDs cause miscarriage" idea you read about online comes mainly from observational studies. Some found an association and others, including a large cohort study, did not. Those studies could not fully separate the effect of the drug from the reasons people were taking it, such as pain, illness or early pregnancy symptoms. The evidence is mixed and low quality, and it does not show that a few days of use causes miscarriage.
- **Timing.** At 4–5 weeks from your last period, the embryo was about 2–3 weeks post-conception. That is the very start of organ formation. Exposure this early is generally regarded as low-concern, particularly for a short course.

### What the real NSAID warnings are about

The firm warnings are for use **later** in pregnancy. From about 20 weeks, NSAIDs can reduce amniotic fluid and affect the baby's kidneys. In the third trimester they can cause premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, a blood vessel in the baby's heart. Neither applies to what you took.

### What to do now

1. **Stop ibuprofen for the rest of the pregnancy** unless a doctor specifically advises it. For back pain, paracetamol (acetaminophen) at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time is the usual first choice. Heat, gentle stretching and physiotherapy also help.
2. **Tell your obstetrician or midwife at your booking visit.** Mention it so it is in your notes. It is not an emergency, and it does not call for any special extra tests. Your routine first-trimester screening and the anatomy scan at around 18–22 weeks are the standard checks for every pregnancy.
3. **If you want an expert second opinion:** MotherToBaby in the US/Canada and UKTIS in the UK offer free, confidential advice about medication exposures in pregnancy.

### When to seek care promptly

These are unrelated to the ibuprofen, but good to know in early pregnancy:
- Heavy vaginal bleeding
- Severe one-sided abdominal or pelvic pain
- Shoulder-tip pain
- Fainting or dizziness

Taking ibuprofen before you knew you were pregnant is extremely common, and the evidence does not suggest you have harmed your baby.

*This is general information, not a substitute for advice from your own clinician.*
