**Short answer:** The teacher is right about the main point. While the sand is flowing steadily, the scale reads 500.0 g. Your son's objection is still a good one. "Those two effects might not cancel exactly" deserves a real calculation, not a hand-wave. When you do the calculation, the two effects cancel exactly. There is also a tiny leftover correction, and it makes the reading slightly *higher*, not lower.

## The clean argument: the missing weight and the extra push are the same number

Suppose sand leaves the neck at a steady rate of **μ** grams per second and each grain falls for a time **t** before it lands.

**1. How much weight is "missing"?** The grains in mid-air are the ones that started falling during the last *t* seconds. That's a mass of **μ·t**. Your son is right that they aren't pressing on anything, so the scale loses their weight: **μ·t·g**.

**2. How big is the extra push from the impacts?** A grain lands going at speed **v = g·t**, because it has been in free fall for time *t*. The bottom pile has to stop that grain, which means removing momentum. Each second, a mass μ arrives at speed g·t, so the pile has to remove momentum at a rate of **μ·g·t** per second. A rate of momentum change is a force, so the impacts push down with an extra **μ·g·t**.

The missing weight is μ·t·g and the extra impact force is μ·g·t. They are the same expression, just written in a different order. That's the rebuttal to "they wouldn't cancel exactly": they have to cancel, because both depend on the same fall time *t*.

## The deeper reason (why it had to work out)

For the whole hourglass as one system, Newton's second law says:

> (scale force) − (total weight) = rate of change of the system's total momentum

The scale only reads something other than the weight if the contents *as a whole* are speeding up or slowing down. During steady flow, the falling stream looks the same from one moment to the next. Every second, one grain-packet starts falling and another one lands, so the total momentum doesn't change. Change in momentum is zero, so the reading equals the weight.

The "mid-air grains can't push" argument looks at only one part of the system. The impacts are the other part, and they carry exactly the force those mid-air grains would otherwise supply.

## Where your son's instinct is partly right

The reading is **not** 500.0 g at every moment:

- **Right after the flip:** the stream is still forming. Grains have left the top but none have landed yet, so the scale briefly reads **less**. This is the effect your son is picturing. It's real, but it only lasts for the first fraction of a second.
- **At the very end:** the stream shuts off, the last grains are still landing, and nothing new is leaving the top. The scale briefly reads **more**.
- **During steady flow:** the pile at the bottom slowly rises, so each grain falls a little less far. The stream's downward momentum therefore shrinks slowly over time, and the scale reads *very slightly more* than 500 g. The excess works out to roughly μ × (the speed at which the pile's surface rises). For a real hourglass that's something like a hundred-thousandth of a gram, far too small for a 0.1 g display to show. As I recall, this effect was worked out in the *American Journal of Physics* in the 1980s under the name "the hourglass problem" (recalled, not verified, so confirm the exact reference if you want to cite it).

So on the question as the teacher asked it, the teacher wins. Your son's sense that "exactly equal" needs proof is good scientific instinct, and he can turn it into the stronger result that the tiny correction actually goes the *other* way.

## How to make it click for him

1. **Don't start by declaring a winner.** Ask him to calculate both effects himself: "How much sand is in the air at any moment? How hard does the sand hit the bottom?" Once he writes μ·t·g next to μ·g·t, he's convinced himself.
2. **Use a juggler analogy.** A juggler stands on a scale with three balls. At any instant, one or two balls are in the air. Does the scale's *average* reading drop below juggler + three balls? It can't, because the balls would sink over time. The hands have to push up on the balls, on average, with their full weight, and the scale feels that push. The hourglass is a smooth, continuous version of the same thing.
3. **Give him the transients as a reward.** Tell him he's right that the reading changes when the flow starts and stops, and ask him to predict the direction at each end. That respects his intuition and places it where it's actually true.

**A question to check he's got it:** if you put the hourglass in an elevator that moves up at *constant speed*, does the scale reading change? (No. Constant velocity means no change in momentum, for the same reason as above.)
