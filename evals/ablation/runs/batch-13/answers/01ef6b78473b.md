**The number: about 97 out of 100.** That's roughly how many babies are born without a major birth defect in any pregnancy. The evidence suggests your 3 days of ibuprofen most likely didn't change that in any measurable way. Studies can't put an exact figure on the extra risk from an exposure this small, but the best estimate is close to zero.

I can't honestly give you anything more precise than that, and I'll explain why below. The figures here come from memory of the published literature and I haven't checked them against sources this turn, so please confirm them with your obstetrician or midwife.

### Birth defects
- **Baseline risk:** about 3% for any pregnancy, with no medication exposure at all (recalled figure).
- **Ibuprofen in early pregnancy:** large observational studies have mostly found no meaningful increase in major malformations overall. A few studies reported weak links to specific defects, such as some heart defects or gastroschisis. Those findings haven't been consistent, and even where they appeared, the absolute increase was small.
- **Your timing:** if 4-5 weeks is counted from your last period, that's only about 2-3 weeks after conception. The earliest part of that window is often called the "all-or-none" period. An exposure then usually either has no effect or ends the pregnancy very early. It doesn't typically cause a malformation. Organ formation starts around week 5 from the last period, so part of your exposure may overlap its very start. That's one reason I'm saying "close to zero" and not "zero".

### Miscarriage
- **Baseline risk:** at 34, roughly 10-15% of recognized pregnancies end in miscarriage. The risk drops noticeably once a heartbeat is seen on ultrasound (recalled figure).
- **The NSAID studies you read about:** some observational studies did find a higher miscarriage rate in people who took NSAIDs around conception. Other large studies found no link once they controlled for confounders. The positive studies have a known problem. Early miscarriage often starts with cramping or pain, and people take painkillers for that pain, so the pain can come first and the pill second. Even taken at face value, those studies describe a modest increase on top of the baseline, not a high risk. I think the honest summary is: possibly a small effect, and quite possibly none.

### Where the real NSAID warnings come from
The clear harms from NSAIDs in pregnancy happen **later in pregnancy**, not at 4-5 weeks:
- From about **20 weeks**: reduced amniotic fluid, caused by effects on the baby's kidneys. This is why the FDA added its warning.
- From about **30 weeks**: early closure of a blood vessel in the baby's heart (the ductus arteriosus).

A lot of the scary material online merges those later-pregnancy risks with early exposure. They're different situations.

### What to do now
- **Stop the ibuprofen** for the rest of the pregnancy unless a clinician tells you otherwise.
- **For your back pain,** paracetamol/acetaminophen at standard doses is the usual first choice in pregnancy. Heat, gentle movement and physiotherapy also help. Check the dose with your pharmacist or clinician.
- **Tell your prenatal provider** about the exposure at your first visit. It won't usually change your care, and no special testing is needed for it. It belongs in your record, and they can reassure you in person.
- **Take folic acid** if you haven't started yet. It's the evidence-based step that actually lowers birth-defect risk right now.

**Get care promptly** if you have heavy bleeding, severe one-sided abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, or dizziness or fainting. These can happen in any early pregnancy and have nothing to do with the ibuprofen, but they need same-day assessment.

You did nothing unreasonable: you treated pain before you knew you were pregnant, which is extremely common. The most likely outcome by far is that this has no effect on your baby.
